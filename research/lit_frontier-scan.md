## 1. Frontier taste (goal a): does the top-3 still take pure theory here?

**Verdict: yes, but the template has changed.** Pure theory is still accepted at JF/RFS in governance/activism/voting — but the accepted papers are (i) built on a *crisp, novel mechanism* stated in one sentence, (ii) *fully proven analytically* (no numerical comparative statics), and (iii) wrapped in *institutional anchoring + an explicit empirical-implications section that reinterprets existing data*. Theory *without* a data hook now tends to land at RAPS/JFQA/Management Science or field journals; the premium top-3 slots in activism/takeovers are increasingly occupied by **structural estimation**.

RAPS publishes essentially no corporate-control theory; JFQA publishes activism empirics (e.g., pension-plan channel, JFQA 57(6) 2022) but no recent flagship activism theory.

**What this means for us:** A static microstructure model whose headline results are *numerically verified only* (our R1 hump) does not fit the JF/RFS theory template — every accepted theory paper above proves its comparative statics in closed form under general assumptions. The bar is not "theory + empirics in one paper"; it is "theory clean enough to be fully provable, plus a section that speaks to measurable quantities." Our paper's viable top-3 path: (1) shrink the core to a provable theorem set (existence + the disclosure-attenuation comparative static, if provable, + endpoint characterization of Δ(κ)); (2) add the institutional anchor (13D regime, 2024 acceleration as a fact, not the identification); (3) add a "guidance for empirical work" section in the TSD/Voting-Premium style mapping each comparative static to an existing or planned measurement. Failing full provability of R1, reposition toward JFE-style "theory + calibration/structural anchoring" or RAPS/JFQA is unrealistic — JFE 2022–2026 in this area is dominated by structural estimation, and its rare theory acceptances (Chen–Gupta–Starmans) are still fully proven.

### 2.1 Burkart & Lee (RFS 2022), "Activism and Takeovers" — local PDF (`lit/hhab039.pdf`)

- **Citation:** M. Burkart, S. Lee, *Review of Financial Studies* 35(4), 2022, 1868–1896 (open access, OUP).
- **Question/summary:** Compare activism vs hostile takeovers as intervention modes for a blockholder with continuous effort choice, under a *dual* free-rider problem (Jensen–Meckling at effort stage, Grossman–Hart at tender stage). Takeovers are more efficient but can be less profitable; "takeover activism" (campaign to broker a sale) can dominate both.
- **Architecture:** Single blockholder with toehold t<1/2; continuum of atomistic shareholders (GH/Bagnoli–Lipman limit); effort e continuous with V(e,θ), C(e) twice-differentiable (Assumptions 1–4: concavity, Inada-type interiority, toehold gains cover fixed cost, returns vanish as θ→0). Tender offer = Burkart–Gromb–Panunzi (1998) with effort instead of diversion: first-and-final restricted conditional cash bid; shareholders tender iff p ≥ V*(conjectured stake). Activism = reduced-form success probability q(a,ψ,t_a), cost K(a) (Assumptions 5–6; microfoundations relegated to Internet App. C via Brav et al. 2017, Maug–Rydqvist 2009, Brav–Mathews 2011, Gantchev 2013). Takeover activism = campaign, then activist (with all bargaining power) negotiates binding merger with third-party bidder; legal risk ε of ex post price revision à la Müller–Panunzi (2004). Equilibrium: backward induction; all results closed-form comparative statics in (θ, t, ψ, ε).
- **Key results (all analytically proven):** Lemma 1–2 (effort/tender-offer structure: bidder buys exactly to 50%, pays full post-takeover value); Prop 1 (bidder profit falls in θ for small toeholds — "unrecompensed effort problem"); Prop 2 (activist profit rises in θ — "limited effort problem"); Prop 3 (central: activism more profitable than takeovers when toeholds/campaign costs small and θ large, despite lower efficiency); Lemma 4 + Prop 4 (takeover activism dominates regular activism for low legal risk ε, and tender offers for small toeholds, large θ); Lemma 5 (free-riding on a tender offer dominates regular activism); Prop 5 (takeover activism survives only if it beats the tender-offer outside option → selection effect: observed takeover-activism returns are high); Cor 1–2 (activist–bidder alliances Pareto-improve; higher campaigning efficacy ψ raises total M&A, reduces hostile bids). Figure 1 is the only numerical object (illustrative).
- **Institutional facts used:** 13D 5% threshold and its price-spike consequence (fn.1, citing Orol 2008, Brav et al. 2008, Klein 2009); Gantchev (2013) $10.5M mean campaign cost, 47.6% failure (Brav et al. 2010); 97.5% of >$100M deals litigated in 2013 (Cain & Solomon 2014) motivating legal risk ε; Lyondell v. Ryan business-judgment baseline; Holderness (2009) blockholder prevalence.
- **Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses:** Strength — first unified comparison deriving activism/takeover differences from modus operandi alone; clean closed-form propositions; rich, sign-flipping predictions. Weaknesses a referee would press: activism technology is reduced-form (q, K); no pre-disclosure toehold accumulation in anonymous markets — they explicitly punt to Back et al. (2018) and "leave an analysis of how predisclosure and post-disclosure decisions interact to future work"; no liquidity, no information asymmetry vis-à-vis the market, no exit option; static post-disclosure setting; no managerial agency problem (manager is passive).
- **Implications for repositioning:** This is our closest *published* theory neighbor and our best friend. It occupies "post-disclosure intervention-mode choice"; it explicitly leaves pre-disclosure trading/liquidity open (citable gap). Our κ-hump and disclosure-attenuation results answer their open problem. Cite prominently; differentiate: they have no prices/inference, no disclosure rule as design object, no bidder entry based on price. Their Prop 5 selection logic (observed campaign returns are selected on outside options) is a caution for any reduced-form premium regression — we can borrow it.

### 2.2 Levit, Malenko & Maug (JF 2024), "Trading and Shareholder Democracy" — ECGI WP 631/2024 (= published version), `lit/levit-malenko-maug-2024-ecgi.pdf`

- **Citation:** D. Levit, N. Malenko, E. Maug, *Journal of Finance* 79(1), 2024, 257–304. Brattle Group First Prize 2024 (verified at afajof.org/prizes and Foster/Mannheim news).
- **Question/summary:** Does shareholder voting improve welfare when the shareholder base forms endogenously through trading? Trading and voting are complementary → self-fulfilling expectations, multiple equilibria; prices can move opposite to welfare; relaxing trading frictions can *reduce* welfare.
- **Architecture:** Continuum (measure 1) of risk-neutral shareholders, each endowed with e shares; valuation v(d,θ,b) = v0 + (θ+b)(d−λ)-style linear in decision d∈{0,1}, common state θ∈{−1,1}, private bias b ~ G (publicly known, full support); key assumption: private values scale with ownership. Timing: trade (buy up to x, sell up to e; no shorts; market depth λ=x/(x+e) is the liquidity/friction parameter) → public signal q ~ F on [−Δ̄,Δ̄] → vote with majority rule τ (record date after trading). Equilibrium: SPNE in undominated (as-if-pivotal) strategies; cutoff rule q* (Lemma 1). Benchmarks: voting w/o trading (unique, Prop 1) and trading w/o voting (unique, Prop 2).
- **Key results (all proven):** Prop 3 (full model equilibria; activist and conservative cutoffs); Prop 4 (frictionless limit dominates); Prop 5 (multiplicity region: high depth λ, intermediate τ, enough heterogeneity, intermediate λ-disagreement); Lemma 3 (price maximized when median voter = marginal shareholder; welfare when = average post-trade shareholder); Prop 6 + Cor 3 (prices vs welfare move oppositely under median-voter-shifting changes, e.g., majority rule); Lemma 4 (board rule: constrained efficient, depth helps); Prop 7 (relaxing trading frictions can reduce welfare when no-trade median voter is already extreme and |H(q*)−λ| small); Prop 8 (optimal board is biased toward post-trade base). Extensions: index investors, E&S, post-vote trading.
- **Functional forms that load results:** linearity of valuation in (θ+b); the trading cap x (bang-bang trades); the tie-breaking no-trade rule; symmetry of F's support (stated as inessential).
- **Institutional facts:** record-date mechanics; proxy-advisor recommendations arrive ~1 month after record date (Li–Maug–Schwartz-Ziv 2022 fig.1); majority-rule heterogeneity (Papadopoulos 2019; Maug–Rydqvist 2009); Exxon proxy fight as ESG vignette.
- **Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses:** Strength — one-line mechanism (feedback loop trading↔voting), fully general proofs, immediate reinterpretation of event-study practice. Weaknesses: no information asymmetry (public signal only); static; price = marginal shareholder valuation, no noise traders/Kyle-style inference — their "liquidity" is a trading *quantity cap*, not order-flow informativeness.
- **Implications for repositioning:** Template to copy: the entire empirical connection is one section mapping propositions to published moments + testable implications ("event studies unreliable when votes are close," "multiplicity more likely in liquid stocks"). Note their Prop 5: multiplicity *more likely when the market is liquid* — a liquidity-comparative-static with sign opposite to intuition; our κ-hump is the takeover analog. Cite as proof that liquidity-governance comparative statics are prize-worthy at JF *when proven*. Also note what we add that they lack: informed trading + price inference (Kyle lineage) and a policy object (disclosure threshold).

### 2.3 Levit, Malenko & Maug (JF 2026), "The Voting Premium" — author-archived published PDF, `lit/levit-malenko-maug-voting-premium-jf2026.pdf`

- **Citation:** D. Levit, N. Malenko, E. Maug, *Journal of Finance* 81(3), June 2026, 1321–1375 (DOI 10.1111/jofi.70037; submitted Nov 2023, accepted Jan 2025).
- **Question/summary:** Unified theory of blockholder governance and the voting premium *without takeovers or controlling shareholders*: premium = blockholder's net marginal payoff from one more vote; reconciles the 40-study empirical mess (positive average, many negative values, tiny lending-market/record-date estimates).
- **Architecture:** Continuum of small shareholders + one minority blockholder (stake α, bias β); same preference structure as TSD; quadratic trading costs (γ small shareholders, η blockholder; γ>γ̄ ensures nice properties, limits to arbitrage); blockholder moves first (limit-order interpretation); then public signal q, then vote at threshold τ. Equilibrium: SPNE undominated; cutoff q*; price decomposes into "hypothetical exogenous-decision-rule price" + voting premium = net marginal payoff of a vote.
- **Key results (all proven):** Lemma 1 (cutoff rule); Prop 1 (small-shareholder trading/voting response; median-voter movement); Prop 2 + Cor 1 (equilibrium blockholder trade y*, price, premium decomposition); Prop 3 (sign of voting premium vs blockholder bias cutoffs β_L, β_H: positive premium when blockholder is moderate-to-activist; premium = 0 when blockholder becomes median voter); Prop 4 (conditions for premium >0 with binding constraints); Prop 5 (negative premium when small shareholders free-ride on blockholder's trades — price impact on voting shares endogenously exceeds that on nonvoting shares). Extensions: separately traded votes (price can be zero while bundled premium >0), post-vote trading, multiple blockholders (competition ↓ premium; conflict ↑), proxy advisors, manager-decides benchmark, passive investors.
- **Key mechanism:** blockholder's trades move the median voter directly (votes accumulated) and indirectly (dissenters sell disproportionately → composition shifts). **Liquidity (price impact) is endogenous** and differs across share classes — their headline: "liquidity, if measured by price impact, is endogenous in our setting."
- **Institutional facts:** record dates; dual-class/coattail regulations (Maynes 1996, Nenova 2003); six empirical methodologies (dual-class premium 22.68%, dual-class tender-offer premium 42.16%, block-trade premium 20.27%, option replication, equity lending, record-day drop); Lewellen–Lewellen (2022) ownership stats.
- **Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses:** Strengths — rationalizes conflicting *measurements*, not just facts; proofs general. Weaknesses: γ-large assumption drives tractability (referee could ask how binding); continuum kills pivotal-voter private information (they defend via Feddersen–Pesendorfer 1997 limit); no takeover channel at all (deliberate, but a referee wanting premia will note it).
- **Implications for repositioning:** Directly adjacent to our premium object: they decompose a *control-related premium* into price-impact and influence terms — our premium wedge λ = 1−q(1−γ)ψ should be positioned relative to their decomposition (ours comes from tender-game free-riding + bidder entry on price; theirs from vote-reallocation). Their empirical-methodology taxonomy (Section VI) is a model for how to make a theory paper speak to measurement. Their "endogenous liquidity" claim pre-empts any naïve claim of ours that liquidity is exogenous — we must frame κ as market-wide noise-trading intensity (exogenous to the blockholder's order), distinct from their price-impact endogeneity.

### 2.4 Celentano & Levine (2025), "Shareholder Activism, Takeovers, and Managerial Discipline" — local PDF (`lit/celentano-levine-2025-ssrn.pdf`, SFI RP 25-81, Oct 2025)

- **Status:** Working paper; **R&R at RFS** (per `lit/competitor_scope_tables.md`); FMA 2025. THE most direct competitor on nouns (activism × takeovers × discipline).
- **Question/summary:** Quantify activism's role in the market for corporate control in an estimated equilibrium model with both activism and M&A. Activism complements M&A (cuts agency frictions in takeover negotiations) but crowds out disciplinary takeovers (effort-incentive substitution); net effect on M&A volume ≈ 0; activist threat adds 0.33% to all firms' equity value.
- **Architecture:** Infinite-horizon discrete time; risk-neutral agents, discount β=0.952. Firm: profit π=z k^α − c_k k, log-AR(1) productivity + project boost e·p, p~Exp(λ_p). Activist: receives private signals s_p, s_w (signal = truth + σ_x·η) about project quality and acquirer quality w~Exp(λ_w); enters by buying *fixed* stake ν̄ (set to 5% = 13D statutory threshold) at cost (1+ξ)(V−π)ν̄, ξ ~ "limited liquidity and information leakages" + campaign costs — i.e., our entire engine collapsed into one scalar. Manager: binary effort at private cost c_e·π (type-I EV preference shocks). Board: binary CEO replacement at cost c_f(ν)·π (activist reduces entrenchment). Takeover: acquirer quality w revealed to board+acquirer; Nash bargaining (equal power) over P_t = (1−θ_a)V(w) + θ_a V(z_{t+1})/(1−c_m(ν)); board bears private cost c_m(ν) of takeover (entrenchment). Shareholder value = PDV of cash flows on public info.
- **Methodology:** SMM, 12 parameters, 15 moments; data 2001–2019: CRSP/Compustat, SDC (Ewens mapping), Audit Analytics 13D filings (initial filings only; amendments dropped; first filing per 12-month window; >50% owners dropped), Equilar turnover/comp, Brav hedge-fund list. Moments: asymmetric sales persistence (Wang–Wu 2020 style), turnover frequency/correlations, entry frequency, announcement returns (−30,+1 CAR, mean 4.4%), takeover frequency, mean bid premium 36.6% (offer ÷ price 25 days prior), correlations with profit growth, 3-year post-campaign profit growth. Clustered SEs at firm level.
- **Key results (estimated):** activist presence: takeover probability +7.7% (7% of deals marginal); **bid premia 13.7% (5.2pp) lower** with activist present — facilitation lowers board's bargaining position; campaign's average shareholder gain via M&A only ~6bp, but 15.4% in marginal deals; effort-incentive channel worth +0.35% value, +1.17% productivity per campaign; equilibrium threat of activism: +0.33% equity value economy-wide (≈ same size as an actual campaign); crowding-out ≈ 0.8% of M&A volume; acquirer surplus +0.22% net. Decomposition of 4.1% announcement return: 91% selection (76% takeover signal, 14% project signal), 9% treatment — **contrasts with AFS's 75% treatment**; board turnover cost ≈ 2.1× profits; activist cost ξ ≈ 3.0% of stake value (≈ Gantchev/Johnson–Swem/AFS magnitudes); net activist return 1.1%. Hedge funds modestly better at both technologies; efficacy rising over time only via facilitation; information advantage rising. Policy experiment: +20% entry cost → less entry, stronger selection, higher conditional takeover frequency.
- **Institutional facts:** 13D 5% threshold hard-wired as the stake size; 2001–2019 sample (pre-2024-rule); wolf-pack treatment of repeat filings; two-year window to attribute outcomes to campaigns (Boyson et al. 2017).
- **Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses:** Strengths — quantifies threat effects and externalities reduced-form can't reach; internally consistent decomposition; clean validation (untargeted premium distribution). Weaknesses a referee (or we) can press: no trading, no price formation, no liquidity state variable — ξ is a black box that *is* our κ; stake fixed at 5% (no intensive-margin accumulation, no disclosure design); free-rider problem asserted not modeled (info advantage overcomes it by construction); Nash bargaining with fixed weights rather than tender mechanics → premium reduction is a bargaining artifact, not a free-rider wedge; announcement-return decomposition conflicts with AFS (identification differs: filing-choice margins vs full-Compustat entry); sample ends 2019 — silent on 2024 13D acceleration.
- **Implications for repositioning:** (i) They will be the incumbent "activism × takeovers" reference at RFS; we must cite and differentiate in the first page: we microfound *where their ξ and their premium reduction come from* (order-flow inference + tender free-riding) and add the two state variables they lack (κ, disclosure rule). (ii) Their −13.7% premium result is our low-appropriability region — the existing D7 Prop d7:afs mapping stands (but see §4 correction: this number is C-L's, not AFS's). (iii) Their 91%-selection decomposition strengthens our "price capitalizes activism before the bid" denominator channel. (iv) Their counterfactual style (shut down a channel, report Δ) is exactly how to present our disclosure-threshold counterfactuals quantitatively.

### 2.5 Burkart, Lee & Voss (2024), "The Evolution of the Market for Corporate Control" — ECGI WP 956/2024, `lit/burkart-lee-voss-2024-ecgi.pdf`

- **Status:** Working paper (FTG 00137-00, Feb 2024); presented WFA 2023, FIRS 2023, Cavalcade 2023. Serious threat — same intellectual lineage as our most-cited theory anchors.
- **Question/summary:** An informed large shareholder chooses between making a bid herself (smart-buyer problem) or brokering a sale to an uninformed outside bidder (lemons problem). The buy/sell choice itself mitigates asymmetric information → full efficiency of control allocation; as control-investor capital grows, takeover activism substitutes for direct takeovers — explaining the secular decline of hostile tender offers and the hedge-fund/PE symbiosis.
- **Architecture:** Shleifer–Vishny (1986) base: L owns minority block α, knows V~F on [0,1] (log-concave, A3); dispersed continuum free-ride (tender iff P ≥ E[V|P], A1); credible-beliefs refinement (Grossman–Perry 1986, A2) selects min-bid / max-ask PSE. Tender offer: conditional first-and-final cash bid for control. Merger invitation: binding sale negotiated with outsider B via random-proposer Nash bargaining (ρ); B learns V only in control. Market extension: continuum of firms, control investors choose to enter as large shareholder or outside bidder, random matching; comparative dynamics in investor mass.
- **Key results (all proven):** Lemma 1 (SV86 benchmark cutoff V*_0); Prop 1 (cutoff V*_1: low types invite merger at P*_L = E[V|V≤V*_1], high types bid at P* = E[V|V≥V*_1]; P*_B=0); full efficiency (all V taken over) vs SV86's inefficient bottom; information rents accrue at top in tender offers, at bottom in merger invitations — intermediate types earn negative rents either way ("lesser evil"); market evolution: early stage all large shareholders (1980s), complementarity-driven transformation, mature stage with possible bidder competition; bidder-chain extension (Section 4.3, Glode–Opp-style chain of block trades) eliminates remaining inefficiency in the limit. Section 4 shows binding merger agreement inessential (block-trade + tender-offer variant replicates cutoff).
- **Institutional facts:** hedge fund AUM 50× since 1997; PE buyout value $30bn→$1,121bn (1995–2022, Bain); hostile-offer decline (Eckbo 2009; Betton–Eckbo–Thorburn); Icahn/Southwest Gas 2021 vignette; Greenwood–Schor/Boyson et al. on profitable campaigns ending in takeover.
- **Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses:** Strengths — solves a real puzzle with one added option; refinement yields uniqueness; market-level evolution story is fresh. Weaknesses: no prices from trading (block is endowed), no liquidity (they state it explicitly: "Market liquidity plays no role in our analysis"), binding-agreement and refinement reliance, exogenous stake α.
- **Implications for repositioning:** Cite as the information-theoretic wing of takeover activism; differentiate: their inefficiency source is adverse selection about V; ours is inference from order flow and the disclosure regime. Their "liquidity plays no role" sentence is a gift — quote it as evidence the liquidity margin is open. If a referee knows BLV, our κ-hump and disclosure-attenuation are clearly orthogonal.

### 2.6 Albuquerque, Fos & Schroth (JFE 2022), "Value Creation in Shareholder Activism: A Structural Approach" — local WP (ECGI 685/2020), `lit/albuquerque-fos-schroth-2022-wp.pdf`

- **Citation:** JFE 2022 (published version; WP read in full).
- **Question/summary:** Model the 13D-vs-13G filing choice and estimate the decomposition of the 6.34% average 13D announcement return into treatment (75.2%), stock picking (12.2%), sample selection (12.6%).
- **Architecture/methodology:** Static choice model. Investor with an established stake chooses filing type; valuation gains Δ_D = μ_D + ε (N(0,σ²_ΔD)), Δ_G analog; due-diligence signals s_x = Δ_x + ε_sx; file 13D iff posterior expected gain net of private activism cost c beats 13G. Parametric linear mapping of firm/investor characteristics to μ_D, μ_G. **ML estimation of the joint distribution of filing choices and announcement returns** — modified orthogonality condition with selection correction; c identified from relative selection across filing subsamples. Sample: all 13D/13G filers (78% of 13D filers also file 13G), hedge-fund subsample robustness (treatment 92.4%).
- **Key results (estimated):** decomposition above; mean 13D filer net return 1.74%; 60% of 13G filings would have higher counterfactual 13D returns — if activism costs were shared, aggregate firm value +$60bn/yr; treatment loadings driven only by *investor* filing experience (not target characteristics); expected returns of both types load positively on **stock illiquidity** and book-to-market, negatively on sales growth/past returns ("illiquidity premium"; contrasts with Edmans–Fang–Zur 2013); treatment component predicts ROA/sales-turnover improvements and lower proxy-contest probability.
- **Institutional facts:** 13D vs 13G legal difference (intent to influence); the filing choice itself as data; 13D announcement return 6.34% vs 13G 0.59%.
- **Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses:** Strengths — first structural decomposition of filing returns; clever use of censored distributions. Weaknesses: cost c is a single scalar (no distribution); stake size exogenous; no trading/price impact (they cite BCDFLL 2018 for that margin); announcement return ≡ value creation assumption (contested by Ben-David et al. below, and by C-L's selection finding).
- **Implications for repositioning:** Their illiquidity-loading result is an empirical hook we can explain: if both filing types' expected returns rise with illiquidity while EFZ find liquidity predicts 13D filing, our κ-dependent intervention profitability is a candidate organizing mechanism. Cite for the decomposition benchmark; our model delivers the *cross-section* of the treatment component (by κ and disclosure regime) that they estimate as a black box.

### 2.7 Ben-David, Bhattacharya, Huang & Jacobsen (JF 2026), "The (Missing) Relation between Acquisition Announcement Returns and Value Creation" — NBER WP 27976 rev. Jan 2025, `lit/bendavid-etal-jf2026-nber.pdf`

- **Citation:** *Journal of Finance* 81(3), June 2026, 1265–1320 (DOI 10.1111/jofi.70038; verified on afajof.org issue page).
- **Question/summary:** CAR around acquisition announcements — used in 92% of 202 top-3 M&A value-creation papers (1972–2021) — does not correlate with ex-post outcomes; CAR is dominated by acquirer-standalone information, not deal NPV.
- **Methodology:** 47,000+ acquisitions, 1980–2018. Ex-post outcome measures: deal-level goodwill impairment (hand-collected, validated against delisting/turnover), short/long-run abnormal ROA, completion. Simple characteristics model (deal/acquirer traits known at announcement) predicts outcomes in- and out-of-sample; CAR fails to correlate even with the *predictable* component. Brute-force subsample search finds no "golden subset." Second-moment analysis: CAR distribution nearly invariant to relative deal size (1:1000 deals show same CAR dispersion as 1:10) → CAR mostly acquirer-specific signal X, not NPV. "Listening to CAR" (withdraw negative-CAR deals) loses 5% long-run; listening to characteristics model gains >20%.
- **Key results:** all empirical; no theory. Anticipation/truncation/selection/feedback ruled out as primary drivers.
- **Institutional facts:** the CAR literature census itself; goodwill-impairment data construction; DGTW-adjusted 5-year BHAR spreads (7.9–10.7% for characteristics vs 0.8–2.8% for CAR).
- **Referee-facing note:** This is the measurement backdrop for ANY premium-based empirics at JF now — referees primed by this paper will attack naive announcement-CAR premia.
- **Implications for repositioning:** (i) If our empirical leg measures "takeover premia" as announcement CARs, we must confront this paper head-on: use their characteristics-model benchmark, or measure premia as offer/unaffected-price with run-up adjustment, and state the limitation. (ii) Our "inferred branch" (prices partially capitalize the blockholder's information before any bid) is precisely their "anticipation/run-up" force (they cite Schwert 1996; Cai et al. 2011) — our model gives structure to why announcement returns understate value creation when a 13D has already moved the price. That is a *constructive* engagement: we explain part of the missing relation via pre-bid price capitalization of activism/disclosure events.

## 3. Direct competitors to 'liquidity × disclosure × takeover premia' (goal b), ranked
