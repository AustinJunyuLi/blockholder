## 1. Edmans, Goldstein & Jiang (2012, JF) — "The Real Effects of Financial Markets: The Impact of Prices on Takeovers"

**Access:** published JF PDF from Goldstein's Wharton page; read in full (39 pp. incl. appendices).

**Research question.** Does the stock market price causally affect takeover activity, and how much of the price–takeover correlation is reverse causality (prices anticipating takeovers)?

**Summary.** An empirical simultaneous-system paper. Firms trade at a "discount" to an estimated frontier value; the discount triggers takeovers (trigger effect), while takeover likelihood is priced back into the stock, shrinking the observed discount (anticipation effect). Using mutual-fund outflow-induced price pressure (MFFlow) as an instrument for the discount, the trigger effect is shown to be an order of magnitude larger than naive OLS/probit estimates suggest. The paper explicitly flags that existing takeover theory cannot explain why *market* prices (as opposed to fundamental values) drive bids, calling for models with asymmetric learning between acquirer and target shareholders.

**Methodology (detail).**
- *Frontier value / Discount:* potential value V* = f(X) estimated by censored quantile regression (CLAD; Powell 1984) at the (1−α)=80th percentile of peer valuations; α=0.2 calibrated so that the median target's cum-premium value (pretakeover value + 38% median premium, from Andrade-Mitchell-Stafford 2001) sits at the 77th industry percentile. Two X-sets: (i) 3-digit SIC industry only; (ii) firm characteristics (size rank, age, asset turnover, R&D, market share, growth, unlevered asset beta) in tercile ranks so acquirers can move characteristics within but not across terciles. Valuation metrics: Q and EV/EBITDA.
- *System:* Discount = γX + γ₁Z₁ + γ₂Z₂ + δξ + η′ (eq. 8); latent Takeover* = μ₁Discount + μ₂X + μ₃Z₁ + ξ (eq. 9); ρ = corr(η,ξ) = δσ²ξ < 0 under anticipation, so naive estimates of μ₁ are attenuated. Z₁ = variables affecting both (leverage, payout, HHI, institutional ownership, Amihud illiquidity); Z₂ = MFFlow instrument. Estimated by FIML / Rivers-Vuong (1988) control-function approach; anticipation coefficient δ recovered by regressing residual discount on Gourieroux et al. (1987) generalized residuals.
- *Instrument:* MFFlow = hypothetical proportional selling by funds with ≥5% quarterly outflows, scaled by dollar volume; based on *previously disclosed holdings*, not actual trades; sector funds excluded. CAAR evidence: no pre-trend, depressed prices for >1 year, full reversal by month 24 (Coval-Stafford-style temporary price pressure).
- *Data:* SDC M&A bids 1980–2007, CRSP/Compustat nonfinancial nonutility targets; 6,555 deals; ~100k firm-years; unconditional annual takeover probability 6.2%.

**Key results (all estimated, not proven).**
- Naive trigger effect: 1pp higher Discount → 1–3 bp higher takeover probability (Table II).
- IV trigger effect: 12–16 bp per 1pp of Discount; interquartile Discount change → 5.7–7.6pp higher takeover probability (vs 6.2% base rate) (Table III). Weak-instrument (Stock-Yogo) and exogeneity Wald tests both reject.
- Anticipation effect: δ < 0; 1pp higher takeover probability reduces the discount ~1.2pp for the average firm, of which ~0.4pp is attributable to the anticipation of next-year's takeover at the average 40% premium; the rest attributed to multi-year anticipation or omitted variables — **the authors are honest that the anticipation effect is only partially identified**.
- Decomposition: ~20% of Amihud illiquidity's effect on valuation operates indirectly through reduced takeover probability.
- Discount correlates positively with target premium (r≈7.2%) and total takeover return; trigger effect is *stronger* for "financially driven" takeovers (LBOs/sponsor deals): interquartile response 2.2pp vs 1.3–1.45% base rate.

**Institutional facts used.** SDC deal classification; median takeover premium 37–39%; bidder announcement returns ≈ 0 (target captures gains); closed-end funds ~80% trade at discount to NAV (Bradley et al. 2010); LBO value-creation literature (Kaplan 1989; Smith 1990).

**Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses.** Strengths: first IV identification of a price→corporate-event causal effect; the trigger/anticipation decomposition is a template. Weaknesses a referee would press: (i) the exclusion restriction on MFFlow is defended but not bulletproof (fund flows correlated with industry conditions; Wardlaw 2020 later criticized the volume normalization — see §6); (ii) "frontier value" is a statistical construct, sensitive to α and X choices; (iii) the mechanism (who learns from the price — acquirer vs target shareholders) is *not* modeled — the conclusion explicitly says standard theory with symmetric information + Grossman-Hart free-riding predicts prices should NOT matter for bid likelihood, so the empirical fact demands new theory; (iv) single average trigger/anticipation effects, no cross-sectional heterogeneity by governance.

**Implications for repositioning our paper.**
- *Cite as the empirical anchor* for price→takeover entry: our bidder's price-contingent entry rule is exactly the "trigger effect." The 5.7–7.6pp interquartile sensitivity gives an order-of-magnitude target for calibration.
- *Borrow the trigger/anticipation language*: our model's disclosed vs inferred branches map onto EGJ 2012's simultaneity — the price both triggers and reflects takeover odds. Framing R1 (hump-shaped minority gains) against their anticipation effect strengthens the motivation.
- *Differentiate*: EGJ 2012 take the price as (partially) exogenous via IV and cannot speak to *premia* endogeneity, disclosure regimes, or the blockholder's governance choice; our model supplies the missing microfounded asymmetric-learning channel they call for in their conclusion.
- Note: their measure of the stakes is the *probability* of takeover, not the premium split; our focus on minority takeover gains is complementary.

## 2. Edmans, Goldstein & Jiang (2015, AER) — "Feedback Effects, Asymmetric Trading, and the Limits to Arbitrage"

**Access:** published AER PDF from Goldstein's Wharton page; read in full (32 pp. main text; proofs referenced to online appendix).

**Research question.** When firm value is endogenous to trading (feedback from price to a real decision), how do speculators' incentives to trade on good vs bad news change, and what does that do to price informativeness?

**Summary.** A strategic speculator who sells on bad news pushes the price down, reveals information to a manager, who then takes the *corrective* action (disinvest), raising firm value and reducing the short-seller's profit. Buying on good news likewise improves decisions but raises firm value and thus *increases* the buyer's profit. Feedback therefore makes trading asymmetric: "buy—not sell" (BNS) is the unique equilibrium over a strictly positive parameter range, while "sell—not buy" (SNB) is never unique. Bad news enters prices slowly; overinvestment occurs even with a fully aligned manager.

**Model architecture (this is the exact framework our draft builds on — reproduce precisely).**
- Dates t = 0, 1, 2. State θ ∈ {H, L}, prior 1/2. Firm value v(θ, d) with d ∈ {−1, 0, +1} (disinvest / status quo / invest), adjustment cost c ≥ 0, value increment x > 0: v(H,1) = R_H + x − c; v(H,0) = R_H; v(H,−1) = R_H − x − c; symmetric for L. Deliberately symmetric payoffs so all asymmetry comes from feedback.
- **Driving inequality:** v(H,1) − v(L,1) > v(H,0) − v(L,0) > v(H,−1) − v(L,−1) (investment increases the sensitivity of firm value to the state). Case 1: R_H − x > R_L + x (value monotone in state); Case 2: R_H − x < R_L + x (non-monotone; corrective action can overturn the state). **Case 2 is where takeover/major-restructuring applications live** (their example: d=1 proceed with acquisition, d=−1 sell assets for cash).
- Manager's cutoff beliefs: γ₁ = 1/2 + c/(2x), γ₋₁ = 1/2 − c/(2x); invests iff posterior q > γ₁, disinvests iff q < γ₋₁.
- Market: speculator present w.p. λ < 1, perfectly informed, **zero initial stake** (online appendix B.2 extends to stake α > 0 — blockholder — and the asymmetry survives; the width of the BNS-unique region is *independent of α*). Noise trader z ∈ {−1, 0, +1} uniform. Speculator trades s ∈ {−1, 0, +1} at cost κ per unit traded. **Total order flow X = s + z ∈ {−2,…,2} observed by both market maker and manager** (not just the price — this kills self-fulfilling price-based equilibria); market maker sets p(X) = E[v|X].
- Equilibrium: PBE + Cho–Kreps intuitive criterion for off-path X = ±2.

**Key results (all analytical, closed-form; numerical work is nil).**
- Lemma 1 (no feedback, 1/(2−λ) ≤ γ₁): cutoffs κ_NF < κ_NT; T if κ < κ_NF; NT if κ ≥ κ_NT; BNS *and* SNB coexist for κ_NF ≤ κ < κ_NT — symmetric multiplicity, no refinement separates them (same firm value, same speculator profit).
- Proposition 1 (feedback, 1/(2−λ) > γ₁): cutoffs κ_T < κ_SNB, κ_T < κ_NT with κ_NT = (1/3)(R_H − R_L), κ_T = (1/3)[(1−λ)/(2−λ)·(R_H − R_L − 2x) + (1/2)(R_H − R_L)], κ_SNB = κ_T + (4/3)·(1−λ)/(2−λ)·x. (i) κ < κ_T: T unique; (ii) κ ≥ κ_NT: NT unique; (iii) κ_T ≤ κ < κ_NT: BNS; (iv) SNB also in [κ_SNB, κ_NT) if κ_SNB < κ_NT. **BNS is the unique pure-strategy equilibrium on the strictly positive interval [κ_T, min(κ_SNB, κ_NT)); SNB is never unique.** If x > λ(R_H − R_L)/(4(1−λ)), SNB disappears entirely.
- Real efficiency: firm value is higher in T than BNS by (1/3)(x − c); feedback's net effect on value is still positive — the limit-to-arbitrage reduction in informativeness partially offsets the decision improvement.
- Case 2 (non-monotone): asymmetry is *stronger* — the negatively informed seller can make a gross *loss* (κ_T can be negative), i.e., no selling even at zero transaction cost. **New force: the positively informed speculator may manipulate by selling to induce value-reducing disinvestment; each equilibrium needs an extra no-manipulation condition (sufficient: R_H − R_L > 4x/3).** This is the analogue of Goldstein-Guembel (2008) manipulation entering a feedback model.
- Beliefs/prices (Section III): Lemma 2/3 — unconditionally symmetric (law of iterated expectations / market efficiency). Propositions 2–3, Corollaries 1–2 — conditional on the speculator's presence, good news moves beliefs and short-run prices more than bad news; asymmetry decreasing in λ; long-run drift can also be smaller for bad news in Case 2 ("bad news about the state need not be bad news for firm value"). Jensen-1978-strong-form efficiency holds (no exploitable profits) even though Fama-strong-form fails.

**Uniqueness/multiplicity posture (directly relevant to our draft).** EGJ 2015 do NOT prove global uniqueness. They characterize *regions* of the cost parameter κ in which each pure-strategy equilibrium exists, prove BNS-uniqueness on a sub-interval, and use Cho–Kreps for off-path beliefs. This is the "methodological precedent" our draft cites for numerical uniqueness — **but note the precedent is actually stronger than what we invoke it for**: EGJ prove uniqueness analytically within regions by enumeration of four candidate strategy profiles; they never rely on numerical fixed-point search for the core result. A referee will notice this. Where our model genuinely cannot enumerate (continuous cutoffs, two regimes), the honest framing is: "existence by Brouwer; uniqueness verified numerically at the calibration," as the draft already does — but we must not overstate EGJ's precedent as "numerical uniqueness is standard"; the stronger claim we can make is that EGJ show *uniqueness need not be global to be useful* — characterizing the equilibrium that survives on the economically relevant region is acceptable in this literature.

**Discrete vs continuous order flow.** EGJ use fully discrete volumes s, z ∈ {−1,0,1}; they conjecture continuity preserves the asymmetry and cite Boleslavsky, Kelly & Taylor (2014/2017, JET) for confirmation in a continuous framework, noting two caveats: (i) in BKT the equilibrium is in *mixed strategies*, so the real decision-maker is always indifferent and gains nothing from market information; (ii) in continuous models the feedback changes *how much* the speculator sells rather than whether she sells, so transaction costs are not needed. **Lesson for us: discrete order flow is what buys pure-strategy equilibria and clean comparative statics over trading-cost thresholds; the cost is the appearance of multiplicity (coexisting BNS/SNB) that must be handled by region-by-region analysis.** Our draft's footnote justifying discrete flow for tractability is consistent with this.

**Institutional facts used.** Coca-Cola/Quaker Oats and HP/Compaq episodes (sellers burned when corrective action followed); TAQ order-flow observability; M&A termination fees/irreversibility weakening feedback; short-sale/margin costs as κ.

**Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses.** Strengths: closed form; deliberately symmetric primitives; clear AER-style single mechanism. Weaknesses: (i) manager observes X directly (contrived vs price-only); (ii) speculator perfectly informed, manager totally uninformed (defended in fn. 12 — only incremental information needed); (iii) one strategic speculator (defended: needs only large traders, not monopoly); (iv) zero stake in main text; (v) the key asymmetry is a *range-of-parameters* statement, not an everywhere-statement; (vi) Case 2 needs the extra no-manipulation condition — a reminder that once firm value is non-monotone in the state (takeover-like settings!), manipulation constraints bind.

**Implications for repositioning our paper.**
- *Keep citing as the foundational framework*, but describe it correctly: a managerial-investment feedback model with a speculator who has no stake and no governance choice; our contributions (governance-mode choice, 13D disclosure partition, bidder entry) are all genuinely absent there.
- *Borrow the trick that makes their paper clean*: a payoff table built so that the result comes from the feedback loop, not baked-in asymmetry (our hump-shape R1 currently relies on numerical verification — the EGJ discipline would ask: can we pick primitives so that the hump or the disclosure-attenuation result follows from an inequality like their (1), provable in closed form?).
- *Borrow the region-wise uniqueness posture*: state existence/uniqueness per regime with the parameter region named explicitly, rather than a global uniqueness aspiration.
- *Heed Case 2*: a takeover is precisely a non-monotone-value corrective action; in that region EGJ show manipulation incentives appear and must be ruled out. If our bidder-entry feedback makes firm value non-monotone in the blockholder's private signal, we need an analogous no-manipulation condition or an argument that the blockholder's stake makes manipulation unprofitable.
- Their result that the BNS-unique region's width is *independent of the initial stake α* (online appendix) cuts against any claim that block size is the key driver of feedback asymmetry — if we want stake size to matter, it must come through disclosure thresholds, not through the EGJ force.

## 3. Bond, Edmans & Goldstein (2012, Annual Review of Financial Economics 4: 339–360) — "The Real Effects of Financial Markets"

**Access:** published Annual Review PDF from Philip Bond's faculty page (also on Goldstein's page); read in full.

**Research question / summary.** Survey of the feedback-effect literature: secondary-market prices affect real decisions because decision-makers learn from prices (or contract on them). Two main points: (1) the right efficiency concept is *revelatory price efficiency* (RPE — do prices reveal decision-useful information), not *forecasting price efficiency* (FPE — do prices forecast cash flows), and the two diverge precisely because of feedback; (2) feedback changes financial-market equilibrium itself, rationalizing manipulation, asymmetric trading, frenzies, etc.

**Structure/content (methodology = conceptual review).**
- Three reasons prices have real effects: learning, incentive contracts tied to prices, and irrational anchoring (argued to reduce to informational roots).
- RPE-failure taxonomy: (i) corrective actions make firm value non-monotone in the state → same price maps to multiple states → RPE fails even with perfect FPE (Bond, Goldstein & Prescott 2010; Bernanke-Woodford 1997 inflation-targeting analogue); (ii) the firm's response to price information destroys speculators' incentive to produce it (Dow & Gorton 1997 two-equilibrium story; DGG 2011 amplification); (iii) using the price changes trading incentives and can reduce aggregation (Bond & Goldstein 2015).
- Implications sections: Goldstein-Guembel manipulation (uninformed short-selling profits from inducing bad disinvestment; only works on the sell side); EGJ 2015 endogenous asymmetric limit to arbitrage; Hirshleifer-Subrahmanyam-Titman survival of irrational traders; GOY trading frenzies (strategic complementarities from capital providers watching prices); Bond-Eraslan information-based trade without noise traders; blockholders — governance through trading/exit (Edmans 2009 optimal finite block; Admati-Pfleiderer 2009; Edmans-Manso 2011 multiple blockholders; **Maug 1998 and Kyle-Vila 1991 / Kahn-Winton 1998: LOW price efficiency/liquidity facilitates blockholder formation and intervention — the liquidity-control trade-off our paper inherits**); disclosure policy and security design.
- Empirical review: Luo 2005 deal cancellations; Chen-Goldstein-Jiang 2007 investment–price sensitivity; Kau-Linck-Rubin 2008; Fang-Noe-Tice 2009 decimalization; Norli et al. 2010 liquidity and proxy contests; Bradley et al. 2010 closed-end fund activism.

**Institutional facts used.** None new; it catalogs mechanisms and cites the empirical base above.

**Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses.** As a survey: canonical citation for the feedback paradigm and the FPE/RPE distinction; contains no new theorems. Referees use it to check that a new theory paper is actually adding something beyond "feedback exists."

**Implications for repositioning our paper.**
- *Adopt the FPE/RPE framing for our contribution statement*: our disclosure-attenuation result (R2) is fundamentally a statement about RPE — a disclosure threshold changes what the price reveals to the bidder, not what it forecasts. Saying "we study revelatory, not forecasting, efficiency of prices in the market for corporate control" is a crisp, literature-native repositioning.
- *Position the hump-shaped liquidity result (R1) inside their RPE-failure taxonomy*: mechanism (ii) — the bidder's use of the price changes the blockholder's incentive to trade/disclose, and liquidity (κ) modulates both — gives the hump a known intellectual home rather than a free-standing numerical curiosity.
- Their catalogue shows the *corrective-action non-monotonicity* (RPE failure i) is the known hard case; our takeover entry is a corrective action, so we should pre-empt the BGP-2010-style critique (price can't both reflect and guide the action) by showing what our disclosure partition adds.

## 4. Dow, Goldstein & Guembel (2017, JEEA 15(4): 877–909) — "Incentives for Information Production in Markets where Prices Affect Real Investment"

**Access:** published JEEA PDF from Goldstein's Wharton page; read in full incl. main comparative-static propositions.

**Research question.** When the firm's investment decision conditions on the price, what determines speculators' endogenous *information production*, and how does that amplify fundamental shocks?

**Summary.** Traders produce more information about projects more likely to be undertaken (informational leverage); this creates strategic complementarities in information acquisition when the risky project is ex ante suboptimal, generating equilibrium multiplicity and discontinuous market breakdowns: a small drop in fundamentals can collapse information production, investment, and firm value. Equilibrium information production is misaligned with the social optimum (which peaks at ex ante NPV = 0).

**Model architecture.**
- Firm chooses A ∈ {0, 1}: safe project pays V₀ surely; risky pays V_h > V_l, states ω ∈ {l, h} equiprobable, V_h > V₀ > V_l. Key summary parameter **ω* (rendered "ρ" in the text) = 1/2 + (V̄ − V₀)/(V_h − V_l)** measuring ex ante profitability of the risky project. Markets incomplete: no security pays off on ω unless A = 1 — spanning is endogenous (the merger-synergy example is given explicitly).
- Continuum of risk-neutral speculators i ∈ [0,1]; each can buy a signal s_i ∈ {l,h} of precision > 1/2 at cost c; informed mass α endogenous. Each trades x_i ∈ [−1, 1] (position limit; price-takers). Noise n ~ N(0, σ²). Order flow X = n + α(2φ−1) in state h (φ = signal precision). Market maker sets P(X) = E[V|X]; manager invests iff posterior-based value exceeds V₀, i.e., iff X > X*(α), with X*(α) = [σ²/(2α(2φ−1))]·ln[(1−ω*)/ω*] — closed form from the normal MLRP.
- Equilibrium: continuation-game Lemma 1 (pricing rule + cutoff investment; the "price always V₀" no-feedback equilibria are killed by assuming the firm observes order flow directly — same trick as EGJ 2015); information-acquisition stage: break-even π(α̂) = c, stability requires π decreasing in α at the solution.

**Key results (analytical).**
- Prop 1 (risky ex ante optimal, ω* > 1/2): **unique equilibrium**; α̂ = 0 if c too high, else interior/corner. Profit function monotone decreasing (competitive effect only).
- Prop 2 (safe ex ante optimal, ω* ≤ 1/2): α̂ = 0 always an equilibrium; if c < π_max, multiple equilibria (α̂₁ = 0 stable, α̂₂ unstable, α̂₃ stable interior or corner). **Multiplicity arises from strategic complementarity in information production — the more others learn, the more likely the firm invests, the more sensitive firm value is to information (informational leverage).**
- Prop 3: α̂ increases in ω*. Prop 4: amplification — dV/dω* > ∂V/∂ω* > 0 (information channel amplifies fundamentals). Prop 5: at a critical ω*ᴴ the amplification is *unbounded* and firm value discontinuous in fundamentals (market breakdown). Prop 6: aggregate factor input effects; sign of the indirect effect flips at ω* = 1/2.
- Welfare: social value of information is hump-shaped in ω* with peak at 1/2; equilibrium overproduces "foreknowledge" (high ω*) and underproduces "discovery" (low ω*) — Hirshleifer-1971 wedge.
- Comparative statics use "the most informative stable equilibrium" as the selection — explicitly stated.

**Functional forms that load the results.** Normal noise (MLRP → cutoff rules and closed-form X*); binary state and binary action; position limits ±1 making informed traders bang-bang; ex ante equiprobability; the endogenous-spanning assumption (no payoff on ω under A=0) is essential for the complementarity.

**Institutional facts used.** None directly; motivates business-cycle application via Chari-Kehoe-McGrattan wedges; empirical predictions: price informativeness procyclical and higher for growth firms; acquirer-stock informativeness after deal announcements increasing in announced NPV and break-up fees (links to Luo 2005).

**Referee-facing strengths/weaknesses.** Strengths: clean closed forms; a transparent multiplicity statement (Prop 2 enumerates equilibria); the stability + most-informative-equilibrium selection is explicit and defensible. Weaknesses: price-taking continuum (no strategic price impact — complementary to EGJ 2015); firm observes only price yet they kill bad equilibria via the order-flow-observation tweak; business-cycle section is speculative; multiplicity means comparative statics are selection-dependent (they own this).

**Implications for repositioning our paper.**
- *Their multiplicity is handled by a named selection (stable, most informative) — a cleaner precedent for us than EGJ 2015 for our situation*, because our fixed point is continuous-cutoff like theirs, not enumerable like EGJ's four profiles. If our referee demands a uniqueness story, the DGG playbook is: define stability, state comparative statics on the selected equilibrium, and show robustness across fixed points (which the draft already does).
- *Borrow "informational leverage" logic for the disclosure channel*: in our model, disclosure regime determines whether the bidder's information about the blockholder's presence translates into entry — analogous to how DGG's α governs whether order flow translates into investment. A sentence aligning the two would make R2 legible to feedback-literature referees.
- *The endogenous-spanning assumption is exactly the takeover setting*: takeover synergies are only realized if the bid happens — supports our bidder-entry information structure.
- Caution: DGG's hump-shaped *social* value of information at NPV=0 is different from our hump-shaped minority gains in κ; do not conflate, but the existence of their hump shows referees in this literature accept hump shapes when the two opposing forces are named.

## 5. Corum & Levit (2019, JFE 133(1): 1–17) — "Corporate Control Activism"

**Access:** local `lit/corum-levit-2019.pdf` = August 2016 working-paper version (45 pp. with appendices); read in full. (Published JFE version has same results; our draft's bib cites JFE 133, 1–17 correctly.)

**Research question.** Why do activists, not bidders, launch proxy fights, and how does activist presence interact with takeover likelihood?

**Summary.** A bidder who wins a proxy fight captures both sides of the negotiating table and cannot commit not to low-ball the premium/divert resources, so shareholders rationally never elect bidder nominees — the bidder's commitment problem. An activist is on the sell side (wants the highest premium) and is therefore credible; her proxy-fight threat disciplines the incumbent board. Activism and takeovers are complements: activists profit from takeovers of their targets, and bidders are more willing to pay due-diligence costs when an activist is present ("solicitation effect"), because the activist's stake — revealed via 13D — signals the target is "in play."

**Model architecture.**
- Baseline: incumbent board (owns n₀, private benefits B, per-share b = B/n₀), activist (owns α), bidder (toehold μ₀), passive investors > 50%. Standalone value q; bidder synergy Γ > 0 commonly known. Two bargaining rounds (proposer = board w.p. s, else bidder; TIOLI offers) separated by a proxy-fight stage (cost C to challenger; majority vote; undominated pure strategies). Newly elected directors maximize their patron's value (no commitment); divertable amount arbitrarily small. SPE in pure strategies, backward induction.
- Lemma 1: second-round outcome — incumbent blocks iff Γ/(1−μ₀) < b; expected shareholder value q + sΓ/(1−μ₀) + (1−s)b·1{b ≤ Γ/(1−μ₀)} under incumbent; q + sΓ/(1−μ₀) under activist; q under bidder.
- Prop 1: **bidder never runs a proxy fight; activist runs iff C/α < sΓ/(1−μ₀) < b, and always wins** (shareholder-support and activist-incentive conditions intersect).
- Prop 2: unique equilibrium of the full game; deal price v* = q + sΓ/(1−μ₀) + (1−s)b·1{Γ/(1−μ₀) ≥ b}.
- Extended model (Section 4): viability state θ ∈ {0,1} (prior τ), Γ|θ=1 ~ F; activist gets binary signal y with precision; **activist builds stake via Kyle-style trading: liquidity demand is L w.p. 1/2 or 0 w.p. 1/2 (discrete!); activist buys up to 2L; short sales banned; market maker sets p = E[value|total order flow z ∈ {0, L, 2L, 3L}]; after trading, stake becomes public ("e.g., by filing schedule 13D"); bidder observes activist ownership and synergy, then decides whether to pay due-diligence cost c ~ G to verify Γ.** PBE with two refinements (positive activist profit; bounded off-path beliefs).
- Prop 3: activist with y=0 never invests; an equilibrium with activist entry exists iff [h(2L) − h(L)]/h(L) < 2 (h(α) = expected takeover premium given stake α — the ratio condition limits the activist's temptation to over-accumulate, which the market maker would otherwise fully price); in the entry equilibrium the activist *mixes* between buying L and 2L with an explicitly solved mixing probability ρ*. Price schedule p(z) given in closed form.
- Corollary 2: selection vs treatment regions in primitives. Prop 4 (testable): **if only selection operates, takeover probability is strictly decreasing in board entrenchment b; with treatment it can be non-monotone/increasing in b** — an identification strategy for the treatment effect.
- Extensions: motivated-seller boards (activism substitutes for takeovers — activists block/sweeten deals); activist standalone-value proposals (complement at small ε, substitute at large ε); limited board veto (tender offers possible w.p. 1−φ → substitution between bidder bypass and activist role; explains UK/US-1980s vs modern-US differences). The Grossman-Hart free-rider problem is deliberately abstracted away (they focus on board agency); tender-offer extension reintroduces it with dilution parameter ψ.

Functional forms that load results: binary viability state, discrete liquidity demand {0, L}, TIOLI bargaining with fixed s, arbitrarily small divertable resources (tie-breaking), common knowledge of q and Γ in baseline.

**Implications for repositioning our paper.**
- This is the closest "corporate-control activism" competitor in our draft, and the draft's current description ("activists as catalysts of corporate control **via contracting with bidders**") is *inaccurate* — CL is a bargaining/proxy-fight model with a commitment problem, not contracting; there is no contract between activist and bidder (indeed collaborations fail). Fix that sentence: CL model activists as *credible sell-side challengers who put firms into play*; their market microstructure is minimal (binary liquidity demand, mechanical pricing) and — critically — **their 13D is a perfect public signal of the activist's presence, while our 13D regime partitions inference and interacts with liquidity and premia**. That is the sharp differentiation.
- *Complementarity of modeling*: CL take the activist's stake as exogenous at the control stage and endogenize entry in a pooling-ish Kyle market; we endogenize the governance-mode choice (exit/quiet/public voice) before the control stage. Our Exit option and their due-diligence solicitation effect are two halves of one loop — worth one precise paragraph, not a vague "they abstract from secondary-market inference."
- *Institutional borrowings for our empirics section*: the Boyson et al. 70%-within-2-years and Greenwood-Schor facts anchor our empirics transition; CL's selection-vs-treatment identification logic (Prop 4) is a template for how to turn our comparative statics into testable predictions.

## 7. Cross-cutting synthesis for repositioning

**(a) What "feedback equilibrium" discipline looks like in this literature.** Every theory paper here solves the loop by *enumeration or closed-form cutoff rules*: EGJ 2015 enumerate four strategy profiles and prove existence/uniqueness region-by-region over the trading cost κ; DGG 2017 get closed-form X*(α) from normal noise + binary state and characterize information-production equilibria with an explicit stability selection; CL prove uniqueness of the bargaining SPE outright. Nobody in this strand proves existence via Brouwer over continuous cutoff vectors — our approach is *less* standard than the draft implies. The acceptable repertoire is: (i) region-wise uniqueness with named parameter regions (EGJ); (ii) explicit equilibrium selection among named candidates (DGG: stable + most informative); (iii) full uniqueness under clean game form (CL). Our "existence by Brouwer + numerical uniqueness" is defensible only if the paper's theorems are the ones that *are* proved cleanly (the disclosure-attenuation sign, endpoint symmetry Δ(0)=Δ(1)) and the numerics are labeled exactly as they are.

**(b) Discrete vs continuous order flow.** EGJ 2015 (discrete {−1,0,1}) vs BKT 2017 (continuous, mixed strategies, decision-maker indifferent) show the trade-off: discrete flow buys pure strategies and threshold comparative statics in a trading-cost parameter; continuous flow buys smoothness at the price of mixing and weaker real effects. DGG (continuous trades ±1 bounds, normal noise) shows the other continuous route: keep actions binary so MLRP gives closed-form cutoffs. CL show a JFE corporate-control paper can use binary liquidity demand {0,L} + Kyle pricing without apology. **Our discrete order flow + disclosure partition is squarely within accepted practice; the draft should cite BKT 2017 explicitly as the continuous counterpart and note the mixed-strategy cost.**

**(c) Takeover entry modeling.** Three templates found: (i) bidder enters when price reveals discount (EGJ 2012's empirical trigger; microfoundation left open — asymmetric learning acquirer-vs-target-shareholders is *their* suggested channel); (ii) bidder enters when an activist's disclosed stake signals availability (CL 2019 — 13D as public signal, due-diligence cost); (iii) corrective action taken by a manager/director on order flow (EGJ 2015, BGP 2010). Our bidder entry on (P, D) combines (i) and (ii) and is genuinely unoccupied in the theory literature. **But: EGJ 2015's Case-2 warning applies — takeover entry is a non-monotone-value corrective action, where manipulation/no-trade conditions bind; our appendix should contain an explicit no-manipulation or no-profitable-deviation condition for the blockholder, mirroring EGJ's R_H − R_L > 4x/3 sufficient condition.**

**(d) Provability.** Cleanly provable in this literature: threshold characterizations, region-wise uniqueness, signs of comparative statics given MLRP-style updating, welfare wedges. Routinely *not* proved: global uniqueness with strategic trading + feedback (EGJ don't have it; DGG embrace multiplicity; Goldstein-Guembel restrict to specific equilibria), and magnitudes (all quantitative statements in EGJ 2012 are estimated; DGG's amplification is an existence-of-discontinuity result). Our R1 hump shape is exactly the kind of object this literature would accept as "characterized numerically across all located fixed points" — provided the two economic forces (disclosure attenuation vs inference dilution, or whatever the final mechanism pair is) are named and the endpoint result is proved.

**(e) Institutional hooks for the empirics pivot.** 13D 5%/10-day rule (all papers); Fos 2016 proxy-fight shares; Greenwood-Schor 2009 and Boyson et al. 2016 activist→takeover transition rates; CL's selection-vs-treatment comparative-static identification (takeover probability non-monotone in entrenchment under treatment); EGJ 2012's MFFlow IV and its Wardlaw-2020-robust version; CSRC survey evidence. A credible empirics section: model predicts (i) disclosed-branch premia less liquidity-sensitive than inferred-branch premia (R2) — testable by splitting 13D filers by filing speed/stake; (ii) takeover likelihood hump-shaped in liquidity conditional on disclosure regime.

**(f) One-line positioning sentence supported by this strand:** "We provide the first feedback model of the market for corporate control in which a disclosure threshold — the 13D rule — partitions order-flow inference into disclosed and inferred branches, so that revelatory (not forecasting) price efficiency for a bidder becomes a policy object; the trigger effect documented by Edmans-Goldstein-Jiang (JF 2012) is moderated by the disclosure regime, with a closed-form attenuation theorem and a numerically verified liquidity hump."
