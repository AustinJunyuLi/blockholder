## Paper 1 — Ordonez-Calafi & Bernhardt (JFQA 2022)

**Citation:** Ordóñez-Calafi, G., and D. Bernhardt (2022). "Blockholder Disclosure Thresholds and Hedge Fund Activism." *Journal of Financial and Quantitative Analysis* 57(7), 2834–2859.
**Note:** the parent's label "Beneficial Ownership Disclosure Thresholds" is **not** the actual title. Read as Warwick WP TWERP 1203 (Feb 2021, "updated & accepted for publication in JFQA"), downloaded from https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/economics/research/workingpapers/2019/twerp_1203_bernhardt.pdf (51 pp). Published citation verified via third-party reference lists.

**Research question.** How do blockholder disclosure thresholds shape hedge fund activism, managerial behavior, and real investment, and what threshold does each party (uninformed investors, the activist, a regulator) prefer?

**Summary.** A static trading model in which an activist's sole rents come from secretly acquiring underpriced stock before disciplining a misbehaving manager. A disclosure threshold is a hard cap on the position that can be secretly accumulated; crossing it reveals the activist and eliminates trading rents. The paper derives the optimal threshold for each constituency and shows preferences are *never* aligned except when all prefer a non-binding threshold. It is the **only formal disclosure-threshold design paper** in this literature and is fully analytical.

**Model architecture.** Four dates, risk neutrality throughout:
- t=0: a continuum of dispersed uninformed investors choose capital k; project payoff V = f(k)[1 − δ·1{m=0}], f' > 0, f'' < 0, f'(0) = ∞; marginal cost of capital r; one share per unit, measure-one shares.
- t=1: manager picks business plan m ∈ {0,1}. Bad plan: private benefit φ if undisciplined; privately-observed reputation cost ρ ~ H on [0,R] if disciplined (φ < R). Bad plan destroys fraction δ with probability δ.
- t=2: trading. Liquidity sellers unload l ~ **exponential(μ)** (mean 1/μ; larger μ = more illiquid). An outside activist observes malfeasance with probability λ < 1 and can discipline at privately-observed cost c ~ G on [0,C] with weakly decreasing density g. Activist buys fraction α. A competitive Kyle-style market maker sees only net order flow ω = α − l and sets P(ω) = E[V|ω].
- t=3: payoffs.
Equilibrium concept: perfect Bayesian, solved backwards. The exponential noise distribution is the load-bearing functional form (it delivers closed-form price updating, as in Edmans 2009).

**Key results (all proven analytically; NO numerical results):**
- **Prop 1 (trading equilibrium):** activist position α* = 1/μ; participation cutoff c*_t = [1−Y(α*)](z/μ)[(1−λG)/(1−zλG·Y)]·δf(k); two-tier pricing: P_h = f(k) if ω > 0 (net buy fully reveals the activist), P_l < f(k) if ω ≤ 0. Information rents only in the camouflage region; probability of camouflage = 1 − Y(α) = e^{−μα}. Cutoff unique (RHS decreasing in c_t).
- **Prop 2:** expected payoffs split the pie: E[Π_A] = H(ρ*_t)λG(c*_t)c*_t ≡ π_A f(k); investors get the residual π_I f(k); investment solves π_I f'(k) = r.
- **Corollary 3:** threshold ᾱ binds iff ᾱ < α*; when binding, the activist sets α = ᾱ exactly. Binding thresholds reduce both profits and extent of activism, conditional on the firm.
- **Proposition 4 (the design theorem):** define the activism elasticity of management ε_m < 0 and profit elasticity of activism ε_a > 0. There exist cutoffs ε^A_m < ε^I_m < ε^R_m such that: very responsive managers ⇒ only the activist wants a binding threshold; moderately high ⇒ nobody does; moderately low ⇒ only investors do; very low ⇒ investors and society do, activist doesn't. **Society's optimal threshold always lies weakly between investors' and the activist's.** SOC assumed via quasiconcavity; verified in the appendix only for **uniform** G and H.
- **Corollary 5:** investors benefit from a binding threshold only if g(c*_t)(δf(k) − c*_t) < G(c*_t) — i.e., the conditional marginal profitability of activism must be negative for investors.
- **Corollary 6 (proved by contradiction):** dc_t/dα > 0 for α < α* — the investment-feedback channel is *always* dominated by the direct trading-transfer channel. **The activist NEVER benefits from a threshold because it boosts real investment**; the activist can benefit only because a threshold commits it to intervene less, which encourages the managerial malfeasance that is its source of profits.
- **Prop 7 (stock-picking extension):** if the activist can also trade on good news (probability θ), stock-picking has a **positive spillover** on disciplining: more θ ⇒ negative order flow more likely to signal malfeasance ⇒ lower P_l ⇒ higher trading profits ⇒ more activism ⇒ less malfeasance.

**Institutional facts used:** 2011 Wachtell (WLRK) petition to the SEC to tighten 13D rules, and the Bebchuk–Jackson (2012) / Bebchuk et al. (2013) academic response; Williams Act 1968 purpose ("alert investors in securities markets to potential changes in corporate control…", quoted from Wellman v. Dickinson); threshold levels: US 13D 5%, Canada 10%, Germany reduced to 3% (UK 3%), France 5%; SEC 2020 proposal to raise 13F thresholds (authors wrote a comment letter based on this paper); Brav et al. (2010) median 266 days from disclosure to divestment; Fos & Tsoutsoura (2014) $1.3–2.9M foregone director income; Keusch (2017) +7.4% CEO turnover after interventions; Gantchev (2013) sizable intervention costs; Collin-Dufresne & Fos (2015), Gantchev & Jotikasthira (2017), Edmans et al. (2013) for the liquidity–activism link; La Porta/Djankov for investor protection and capital formation.

**Referee-facing strengths:** fully closed-form; clean welfare ordering; novel and counterintuitive activist-commitment result; direct policy relevance; the managerial-feedback channel is disciplined by Gantchev et al. (2019) evidence.
**Weaknesses a JF/RFS referee would attack:** (i) static, one-shot trading — no accumulation dynamics; (ii) exponential noise and two-tier pricing are stark (any net buy is perfectly revealing); (iii) activist cannot cut-and-run by assumption; (iv) activism cost orthogonal to firm size (increasing returns in reduced form); (v) welfare propositions lean on quasiconcavity verified only under uniform distributions; (vi) no bidder/takeover margin — activism = plan-switching discipline only; (vii) no 13G option — disclosure is a single transparency event, not a menu choice.

**Implications for repositioning:** This is our paper's **closest theory antecedent and mandatory citation**. Differentiate explicitly: OC&B's threshold caps the *stake* and studies *managerial disciplining + real investment*; our threshold splits *order-flow inference* into disclosed vs inferred branches and studies *takeover premia with bidder entry*. Their liquidity result is monotone (more liquidity ⇒ more activism profits); our hump-shaped Δ_min(κ) is a different object (premia, not activism frequency). **Borrow:** their trick that exponential noise yields closed forms (a route to making our R1 provable in a special case); their two-tier pricing is exactly a degenerate version of our disclosed/inferred split. **Kill:** any normative "who prefers which threshold" analysis — OC&B owns it. Position our R2 ("disclosure attenuation") as a *positive* statement about the liquidity→premia transmission, complementary to their normative ranking.

## Paper 2 — Albuquerque, Fos & Schroth (JFE 2022)

**Citation:** Albuquerque, R., V. Fos, and E. Schroth (2022). "Value Creation in Shareholder Activism." *Journal of Financial Economics* 145(2), 153–178 (published version drops the "A Structural Approach" subtitle).
**Access:** local PDF `lit/albuquerque-fos-schroth-2022-wp.pdf` (ECGI WP 685/2020 version, 84 pp) — read in full.

**Research question.** How much of the 13D announcement return is treatment (value creation) vs stock-picking vs sample selection, and what does the 13D-vs-13G filing choice reveal about the cost of activism?

**Summary.** The canonical structural treatment of the 13D/13G margin. The investor's binary filing choice, joint with announcement returns, is estimated by ML; the choice censors each schedule's return distribution, and the model recovers the counterfactual. Headline decomposition: of the 6.34% average 13D announcement CAR, 75.2% is treatment, 12.2% stock-picking, 12.6% selection (WP numbers; the published version reports 74.8/13.4/11.8 — note the discrepancy vs `lit/competitor_scope_tables.md`).

**Model/methodology in detail.**
- Valuation gains Δ_D = μ_D + ε_{ΔD}, Δ_G = μ_G + ε_{ΔG}, both normal; μ_{D,i} = x'_i β_D, μ_{G,i} = x'_i β_G linear in firm characteristics (size, Amihud illiquidity, analyst coverage, B/M, age, sales growth, SIC3 HHI, institutional ownership, idio vol, leverage, ROA, past 12m return), investor experience (# prior 13D / # prior 13G filings), activist-HF dummy, and FF3 factors.
- Due diligence: private signals s_D = Δ_D + ε_{sD}, s_G = Δ_G + ε_{sG}, normal noise; investor files 13D iff E(Δ_D|s_D) − C > E(Δ_G|s_G). C is a **constant scalar** activism cost (net of 13G costs, private benefits, risk-disutility, fake-13D penalty).
- Econometrics: from the econometrician's view, z ≡ EU_D − EU_G is normal; filing 13D = {z > 0}; observed returns are truncated normals with inverse-Mills corrections (Arnold et al. 1983). ML on the joint density of (filing, return). FOCs deliver: β̂_D = selection-corrected OLS (Eq. 12); Ĉ equates precision-weighted average selection (Mills ratios) across the two filing subsamples (Eq. 13); variance parameters pinned by volatility + filing-frequency moments (identification via two crossing indifference curves, their Fig. 2).
- Data: **all** 13D + 13G filings on EDGAR 1996–2017 (universe 50,708 13D + 171,051 13G → final 8,703 13D + 61,234 13G after CRSP/Compustat merge and dropping 28,663 Feb-14 13G filings by exempt investors — these earn −0.08% vs +0.59% for other 13G days, a nice information-content check). CAR window [t−30, t+10], FF3-adjusted (window rationale: Collin-Dufresne & Fos 2015 pre-filing price impact; Brav et al. 2008 no reversal).

**Key results (estimated):**
- Means: 13D 6.33–6.34% vs 13G 0.59%; activist-HF subsample 5.98% vs 1.14%; 13D share of filings 12% (full) / 34% (activist HFs).
- Decomposition: treatment μ_D − μ_G = 4.77pp (75.2%); stock-picking μ_G = 0.77pp (12.2%); selection 0.80pp (12.6%). HF subsample: 92.4% / −4% / 11.6%.
- Ĉ = **4.6% of the ex-post value of the filer's stake ≈ $2.43M**; mean net activist return 1.73–1.74% (vs Gantchev 2013's 0.23% annualized — the difference is attributed to Gantchev's static framework).
- **Treatment is priced off investor identity, not target characteristics:** β_D ≈ β_G for every firm characteristic (unrestricted!), so μ_D − μ_G loads only on investor experience. +10 prior 13D filings ⇒ +19bp on the next 13D return but −25bp on a 13G return. Firm characteristics drive stock-picking only (illiquidity +, B/M +, sales growth −, past returns −).
- Efficiency: **60% of 13G filings have μ̂_D > μ̂_G**; if the activism cost were shared across shareholders, average gain $35.6M per 13G filing ≈ **$60B/year** aggregate (explicitly an upper bound).
- Validation: Treatment_i predicts ΔROA (+2.98pp/sd) and sales-turnover improvements (+7.5pp/sd), subsuming CAR's own predictive power; higher Treatment ⇒ **lower** probability of a subsequent proxy contest (effective campaigns don't escalate).
- Model beats Probit (pairwise correlation with actual choices 0.54 vs 0.25) and two-step Heckman (which understates 13D selection, overstates 13G selection).

**Institutional facts:** 5% beneficial-ownership triggers Schedule 13D (intent to influence) vs shorter 13G (passive/exempt); 78% of investors with ≥1 13D also filed ≥1 13G (grounds the universal-choice assumption); exempt investors file 13G within 45 days of calendar year-end (the Feb-14 screen); activist-HF list from Wei Jiang; stake accumulation and its price impact explicitly left out (cite Back et al. 2018).

**Referee-facing strengths:** first structural decomposition; clean identification narrative; out-of-sample-style robustness on the HF subsample (parameter stability, treatment correlation 0.9955); treatment measure validated on future outcomes.
**Weaknesses:** C constant across all filings/20 years; stake size exogenous (no accumulation, no price impact, no 5%-crossing endogeneity); normality everywhere; "announcement return = anticipated value creation" is assumed, then validated only jointly; treatment is a residual (μ_D − μ_G) with no mechanism; the model is silent on *why* 13D returns exceed 13G returns economically.

**Implications for repositioning:** (i) AFS **own the 13D-vs-13G choice as an estimand** — but they take the stake and the pre-filing trading as given. Our paper can supply the missing block: a model in which liquidity and the disclosure rule determine the (stake, announcement-return, filing-choice) joint distribution that AFS estimate. (ii) Their 6.34%/75.2%/4.6%-of-stake/$2.43M numbers are natural **calibration targets** for our empirical leg. (iii) Note the conflict with Celentano–Levine (91% selection vs AFS 75% treatment) — an open tension in the literature; a model that generates announcement returns from *inference about bidder entry* vs *intervention* can speak to both. (iv) Their assumption that "the market cannot forecast the type of filing before the 5% crossing" (Section 2.6) is exactly the margin our disclosed-vs-inferred branch structure formalizes.

## Paper 3 — Johnson & Swem (JFE 2021)

**Citation:** Johnson, T. L., and N. Swem (2021). "Reputation and Investor Activism: A Structural Approach." *Journal of Financial Economics* 139(1), 29–56.
**Access:** local PDF `lit/johnson-swem-2021-jfe.pdf` (published JFE version, 28 pp) — read in full.

**Research question.** Why do targets settle so often with activists facing large private proxy-fight costs, and why are campaigns so frequent despite the free-rider problem? Answer: reputation for proxy fighting, estimated in a dynamic structural model.

**Summary.** Kreps–Wilson/Milgrom–Roberts chain-store reputation transplanted to activism: a long-lived activist of privately-known type (aggressive = low average proxy-fight cost vs cautious) plays a sequence of three-stage campaign games against short-lived target managers. ML estimation on 2,434 hedge-fund campaigns (1999–2016) produces an evolving reputation measure r_t per activist; reputation roughly doubles activism's value to target shareholders.

**Model/methodology in detail.**
- Stage game: A chooses 13-D (initiate; cost L̃ lognormal(μ_L, τ_L)) or Ignore; M chooses Settle (undertake project worth Δ₁ to shareholders, private cost B > Δ₁ to M) or Refuse; if Refuse, A chooses Fight (payoffs as in settlement minus proxy-fight costs F̃_A, F̃_M; proxy fights assumed always successful) or Fold. Costs: log L̃ ~ N(μ_L, τ_L⁻²); log(F̃_M/(B−Δ₁)) ~ N(μ_M, τ_M⁻²); log F̃_A ~ N(μ_i, τ_A⁻²) with type-dependent mean μ_agr < μ_caut.
- Dynamics: campaign opportunities arrive Poisson (λ_c, fixed at 10/yr); type resets arrive Poisson λ_r (keeps learning perpetual); the single state variable r_t = P(aggressive | observed history) updates by Bayes after each campaign and **decays between campaigns** (silence suggests a cautious activist chose Ignore). Crucially, future targets observe only the same noisy indicators as the econometrician: the 13-D indicator, the Proxy indicator (DFAN14A/DEFR14A/DEFC14A/DEFN14A filings), and a 5-vector a of activist-friendly target actions (Reorg, Payout, CEO departure, Board change, Acq) with P(a_i=1) = â_i + β_i·1{Settle or Fight}, where â_i comes from predictive regressions on the Compustat universe.
- Estimation: closed-form likelihood per campaign L_c = L_gap · L_13D · L_CAR · L_outcome; 10 structural parameters θ estimated by MLE via value-function iteration; 8 parameters fixed/calibrated (δ = 0.9, λ_c = 10, σ_car = 8.99%, β_i from proxy-fight subsample means). Δ₁ is identified off average CAR via E[payoff|r_t,13-D] = Δ₁·P(project occurs|r_t,13-D).
- Data: 35,768 raw 13D filings + 5,910 SharkWatch campaigns → 4,235 non-overlapping campaigns (wolf packs merged to a lead activist; 956 multi-activist) → main sample **2,434 campaigns by 420 hedge funds on 1,889 targets, 1999–2016**; 737,004 activist-days; 14.3% of campaigns feature proxy fights; mean 3-day CAR 2.8%.

**Key results (estimated):**
- Δ̂₁ = **6.62%**; mean campaign cost L̃ = **5.44% of the activist's position**; mean proxy-fight cost 8.68% (aggressive) / 19.44% (cautious) of position; r₀ = 2.05%; λ_r = 0.19/yr; y₀ = 21.82%; f_caut,0 = 11.10%; f_agr,0 = 48.03%.
- High-reputation (r_t > 50%) vs low (r_t < 0.5%): campaigns/yr 3.50 vs 0.58 (model) ≈ 3.48 vs 0.62 (data); settlement 44.11% vs 23.86% (the abstract's "85% more settlements"); fight rates higher.
- **19.92% of campaign initiations and 18.83% of proxy fights are reputation-building** (negative standalone NPV).
- Counterfactuals (25,000 simulated samples): no-reputation world ⇒ initiations 9.34%→5.65% of opportunities, settlements 27.44%→23.81%, fights 16.85%→11.86% of refusals; **target shareholders' average payoff 24.53 → 12.67 bp per opportunity (−48%)** — "nearly double" in the abstract. LR tests reject both no-reputation (χ² = 340.1) and full-information (χ² = 21.0) alternatives.
- Fit is weakest on the r_t–Proxy margin (data 36.87% proxy rate at high r_t vs model 25.55%) and on CAR (only ~1/3 of reputation's total return effect lands in the [−1,+1] window).

**Institutional facts:** 13-D required within **ten days** of initiating a campaign (footnote 13 — the paper predates the 2024 five-day rule and does NOT discuss filing windows beyond this); wolf-pack handling; no comprehensive settlement classification exists in any data provider (motivates the noisy-actions approach); Bebchuk et al. (2020): formal settlements only 13% of their sample, informal ones common.

**⚠ Correction to `lit/competitor_scope_tables.md`:** the skeleton claims J&S "maps the 13D filing window to noise trading via a σ²T isomorphism" and "owns the 2024-acceleration framing (verified claim)." **This is not in the paper.** The JFE text contains exactly one mention of the filing window (footnote 13, "ten days") and nothing on σ²T, window-to-noise mappings, or the 2024 five-day rule (accepted Sept 2019 — it could not). Treat the team's "verified claim journal" entry as erroneous on this point. Separately, the skeleton attributes the "activism lowers bid premia 13.7% (5.2pp)" finding to AFS 2022; that finding is **Celentano & Levine 2025** (see below). AFS report no takeover premia.

**Referee-facing strengths:** first dynamic reputation estimation in activism; internally consistent counterfactuals; the reputation series is published (usable by others); clean nested hypothesis tests.
**Weaknesses:** δ and λ_c fixed off-model (identification concern acknowledged); settlements never directly observed; proxy fights assumed always successful; one-dimensional reputation; type distribution 98% cautious; L̃ folds *all* trading/price-impact costs into one reduced-form lognormal draw — the entire accumulation stage is a black box; no liquidity, no microstructure, no 13G.

**Implications for repositioning:** (i) J&S's reduced-form accumulation cost L̃ is exactly the object our microstructure opens up — cite them as motivation ("campaign costs include the price impact of buying shares", their p. 33) and note our model derives that cost from primitives. (ii) Their fixed disclosure timing (13-D = initiation) contrasts with our **action-triggered** disclosure — a genuine complementarity to highlight. (iii) Their estimated Δ₁ = 6.62% per successful campaign and 2.8% announcement CAR are additional calibration anchors. (iv) Do **not** concede the 2024-acceleration framing to them — it is unclaimed territory (see correction above).

## Paper 4 — Celentano & Levine (2025 wp)

**Citation:** Celentano, F., and O. Levine (2025). "Shareholder Activism, Takeovers, and Managerial Discipline." Working paper, Oct 1, 2025 (SFI RP 25-81; per team notes: R&R at RFS).
**Note:** the parent's tentative title ("…Market for Corporate Influence") is not the actual title. **Access:** local PDF `lit/celentano-levine-2025-ssrn.pdf` (62 pp) — read in full.

**Research question.** Are activism and takeovers substitutes or complements as disciplinary mechanisms, and how much value does each channel (and the mere threat of activism) create in equilibrium?

**Summary.** An infinite-horizon quantitative equilibrium model with a manager, an entrenched board, a potential activist (who gets private signals on project quality and acquirer quality), and a potential acquirer (Nash bargaining with the board). Estimated by SMM on 2001–2019 US data. Headline: activism *facilitates* takeovers (campaign ⇒ +7.7% takeover probability) but *lowers bid premia* (−13.7% = −5.2pp); in equilibrium, activism crowds out ≈0.8% of M&A volume via improved managerial effort while facilitation adds ≈0.6% — net ≈ zero volume effect; the **threat** of activism adds +0.33% to equity value economy-wide; **91% of the 4.1% announcement return is selection** (76% private info about takeovers), contradicting AFS's 75% treatment share.

**Model/methodology in detail.**
- Production: π_t = z_t k_t^α − c_k k_t (α = 0.7); log z_{t+1} AR(1) plus effort term e_t p_t, p_t ~ Exp(λ_p) (project quality).
- Activist: signals s_{x,t} = x_t + σ_x η, x ∈ {p, w} (w = acquirer quality ~ Exp(λ_w)); entry decision ν_t ∈ {0, ν̄} with **ν̄ = 5% set to the statutory 13D threshold**; entry cost ξ·(ex-dividend price)·ν̄ (ξ bundles illiquidity, leakage, campaign costs). After entry, the activist's choice and signals become public; the position is liquidated at takeover or period end.
- Manager: effort e_t at private cost c_e π_t; board turnover decision f_t at private cost c_f(ν_t)·π_t (entrenchment; activist presence lowers it); both choices smoothed by Type-I EV preference shocks.
- Takeover: technology transfer (post-takeover productivity = w_t, q-theory of mergers); board entrenchment c_m(ν_t) as a private cost of selling; Nash bargaining (equal weights) ⇒ price P_t = (1−θ_a)V(w) + θ_a V(z_{t+1})/(1−c_m(ν_t)).
- Estimation: SMM, 12 free parameters, 15 moments (asymmetric sales-persistence coefficients ρ⁺/ρ⁻, turnover frequency and its profit-growth correlation, activist entry frequency + entry CAR, takeover frequency within campaigns, mean bid premium overall and during campaigns, 3-year profit growth after campaigns, etc.). Data: CRSP/Compustat; SDC bids via Ewens mapping (2,194 bids = 4.2% of firm-years); **Audit Analytics Schedule 13D filings only** (2,083 campaigns = 4.0% of firm-years; amendments excluded following AFS; first-filing-only within 12 months against wolf packs; Brav HF classification; Equilar for CEO turnover/comp). Bid premium = bid price / CRSP price 25 days pre-bid (mean **36.6%**); activist entry return = market-adjusted CAR [−30, +1] (mean 4.4%).

**Key results (estimated/simulated):**
- Parameters: signal-to-noise 3.2% (acquirer) / 14.6% (project); board turnover cost = 2.1× profits, activist presence cuts it 22%; takeover entrenchment c_m(0) = 15.1% of firm value, activist cuts it 11% (1.6pp); entry cost ξ ≈ **3.0% of the pre-campaign value of acquired shares**; net activist return 1.1%.
- Campaign effects (holding equilibrium fixed): takeover probability +7.7% (7.2% of campaign deals are marginal to activism); **bid premium −13.7% (−5.2pp) when the activist is present** — facilitation weakens the board's bargaining position; M&A-channel shareholder value +6bp per average campaign, but bimodal (+15.7% in marginal deals, −0.75% in inframarginal ones); effort channel +35bp per campaign, +1.17% productivity, +30.8% project-undertaking probability.
- Equilibrium counterfactuals: effort channel crowds out 0.79% of M&A volume (activism as substitute); facilitation adds 0.64%; net volume ≈ 0; aggregate target M&A surplus falls, acquirer surplus +0.22% net; removing activists ⇒ equity values −0.33% for **all** firms (threat effect ≈ campaign effect in magnitude, and applies to 100% of firms vs 4% of firm-years with campaigns); removing the takeover market ⇒ activists turn to CEO-turnover campaigns (turnover +69% conditional on campaign) but shareholders −26.6%.
- Information counterfactuals: killing the acquirer signal ⇒ entry −88%, shareholder value −0.17%; killing both signals ⇒ activist never enters, shareholders −0.32% — **the information advantage is what overcomes the free-rider problem**.
- Announcement-return decomposition: 91% selection (76% takeover info + 14% project info), ~9% treatment (mostly effort incentives). Direct confrontation with AFS (their Section: "Our finding contrasts with Albuquerque et al. (2022)").
- Extensions: hedge-fund-only subsample ⇒ friction cuts 38.8% (turnover) / 8.3% (takeover); late sample (2010–2019) ⇒ takeover facilitation effect 15.6% vs ≈0 early, acquirer signal-to-noise 3.2%→5.4%; golden parachutes negligible.
- Policy experiment: ±20% entry cost ⇒ higher cost ⇒ less entry, lower productivity, but stronger measured selection (higher conditional turnover/takeover rates, higher announcement returns).

**Institutional facts:** the 5% 13D threshold enters as the stake size ν̄; only 13D filings used (13G absent entirely); campaigns treated as consequences within 2 years of filing (Boyson et al. 2017); risk-arbitrage campaigns within 1 year after bids excluded (Jiang et al. 2018); disclosure mentioned only as motivation in the policy section (block disclosure rules, universal proxy, poison pills).

**Referee-facing strengths:** the only structural paper with activism *and* M&A *and* premia; clean substitution/complementarity decomposition; threat-of-activism quantification is genuinely novel; direct engagement with AFS tension.
**Weaknesses a referee would attack:** no trading or price formation anywhere (announcement return is a model object, prices jump on revelation); stake fixed at 5% by assumption; entry cost ξ reduced-form; equal Nash bargaining weights imposed; the selection/treatment split rests on tiny estimated signal-to-noise ratios (3.2%) — the 91% selection share is model-driven, not data-driven; Type-I EV smoothing + SMM moment selection are discretion-heavy; no disclosure-rule comparative statics despite the policy motivation; bid-premium denominator (25-day-prior price) absorbs activist-runup in a way that mechanically matters for the −13.7% estimate.

**Implications for repositioning:** (i) This is the **most dangerous competitor** (same three nouns; R&R at RFS). But it has **zero microstructure, zero liquidity, zero disclosure design** — our engine is untouched. (ii) Their −13.7% premium interaction is the single best empirical target our model could explain: in our framework, activism-evidence states change the composition of bidder entry and the price denominator; a successful version of the D7 mapping (their low premium ↔ our high-appropriability/low-wedge region) turns their result into an explained quantity. (iii) Their "threat of activism worth 0.33% to all firms" is the welfare hook for why the disclosure *rule* (which governs the threat's credibility) matters. (iv) Their AFS conflict (91% vs 75%) is an open wound in the literature — our model, which generates announcement returns from inference about both intervention and bidder entry, offers a reconciliation route worth one paragraph.

## Paper 5 — Cetemen, Cisternas, Kolb & Viswanathan (JF 2026)

**Citation:** Cetemen, D., G. Cisternas, A. Kolb, and S. Viswanathan (2026). "Leader-Follower Dynamics in Shareholder Activism." *Journal of Finance* (2026). Previously circulated as "Activist Trading Dynamics" and "Activist Manipulation Dynamics"; NY Fed Staff Report 1030 (Sept 2022, rev. July 2025).
**Access:** NY Fed SR 1030 PDF (July 2025 revision, 60 pp) downloaded from newyorkfed.org — read in full. The JF 2026 published PDF exists in the LUISS IRIS repository but is behind a download wall (HTML interstitial); the SR matches it in title and content. An older 2023 Duke version ("Activist Trading Dynamics") was also downloaded and cross-checked.

**Research question.** How do activist blockholders coordinate non-cooperatively when they trade in sequence and can influence each other through market signals (order flow and prices)?

**Summary.** A two-activist sequential Kyle model with endogenous fundamentals (effort = terminal block) and correlated private initial positions. The leader's trades become *predictable*: with positive block correlation she sells on average (creating trading gains for the follower by keeping prices relatively less responsive to her own block-building than the follower's beliefs are), with negative correlation she buys. This overturns the Kyle unpredictability benchmark, amplifies free-riding when correlation is positive, and delivers price-abnormality patterns matching empirical facts around 13D disclosure events. Fully analytical (linear-Gaussian).

**Model architecture in detail.**
- Players: leader (she) and follower (he) with initial blocks X^i_0 ~ N(μ, φ), covariance ρ ∈ [−φ, φ] — the **interdependence** parameter. Three periods: t=1 leader trades θ_L in a Kyle round (order flow Ψ₁ = θ_L + σZ₁); t=2 follower trades θ_F in an identical round after observing P₁; t=3 both simultaneously exert effort W_i at cost ½W_i²; firm value W = W_L + W_F (perfect substitutes). Optimal effort W_i = X^i_T (terminal position), giving payoffs (X^i_T + X^{−i}_T)X^i_T − P_{t(i)}θ_i − ½(X^i_T)².
- Equilibrium: linear strategies θ_L = α_L X^L_0 + δ_L μ; θ_F = α_F X^F_0 + β_F P₁ + δ_F μ; competitive MM prices P_t = E[W_L + W_F | F_t] (affine). Refined to **PBS** (positive block sensitivity: α_L, α_F > 0) for institutional realism. Follower's FOC gives θ_F(2Λ₂ − 1) = Y^F₁ + X^F_0 − P₁; leader's FOC adds the **value of manipulation** X^L_T · ∂E_L[X^F_T]/∂θ_L — the continuation value of influencing the follower's block via the gap between the follower's belief sensitivity ∂Y^F₁/∂Ψ₁ and the price sensitivity Λ₁. Correlation drives a wedge between these two sensitivities.
- Second-order conditions are non-trivial because fundamentals are endogenous (leader's SOC: 0 > 1 − 2Λ₁(1−β_F)) — existence fails for ρ below a threshold ρ̲ < 0.

**Key results (all proven analytically; numerics only for figures):**
- **Theorem 1:** for ρ ∈ (ρ̲, φ] a PBS equilibrium exists. ρ > 0 ⇒ leader **sells on average** (−δ_L > α_K > α_L > 0, E[θ_L|F₀] < 0); ρ < 0 ⇒ leader buys on average; follower trades unpredictable (E[θ_F|F₁] = 0) as in Kyle; α_F = √(σ²/γ^F₁); sign(β_F) = −sign(ρ); ρ = 0 ⇒ both play the Kyle gap strategy θ_i = α_K(X^i_0 − μ), α_K = √(σ²/φ). Uniqueness within PBS proven analytically for ρ ∈ (ρ₀, φ] and numerically for the rest.
- **Theorem 2:** (i) ex ante firm value ≤ 2μ iff ρ ≥ 0 (strict if ρ ≠ 0), monotonically decreasing in ρ — positive interdependence amplifies free-riding (leader offloads activism costs); (ii) multiplayer attacks always beat single-player ones; (iii) liquidity limits: ρ > 0, σ → ∞ ⇒ manipulation persists at a finite limit (the leader's marginal incentive to manipulate, X^L_T, is endogenous — more liquidity ⇒ bigger trades ⇒ more to gain from steering); σ → 0 ⇒ no trade.
- **Prop 1–4 (robustness):** same mechanism with public blocks + private info on exogenous value components or effort productivity; with the leader trading twice; with simultaneous multi-round trading (no correlation needed); with a passive leader (sells for all ρ ≠ 0); with a firm-friendly follower (leader always sells at ρ = 0).
- **Prop 5:** a region around ρ = 0 where **both** activists prefer sequential over simultaneous trading (cost-management motive); **Prop 6:** productive activists prefer to lead; a passive leader is *hurt* by the ability to manipulate; **Prop 7:** with N symmetric followers, α_F rises in N, firm value falls in N, leader's payoff grows like √N; **Prop 8:** for small σ > 0 the PBS equilibrium is the unique linear equilibrium (illiquidity refines).

**Institutional facts (the best-sourced institutional apparatus of the five papers — a free quarry for our empirics section):**
- Blocks below 5% are private information; median hedge fund stake at disclosure is 6.6% (Brav et al. 2021b); ~1% of shares are bought on the trigger day itself (Collin-Dufresne & Fos 2015); hedge funds trade primarily on the trigger date or the day after (Bebchuk et al. 2013; Collin-Dufresne & Fos 2015).
- **2023 SEC amendments**: initial 13D deadline shortened 10 days → 5 business days; amendments 2 business days (SEC press release 2023-219). **2023 SEC guidance on tipping/group formation** (rule 33-11253): a disclosing blockholder who tips others to buy can create a "group" — making *market signals* a safer coordination device. Section 13(d)(3) aggregates group holdings; Rule 14a-2(b)(2) permits limited shareholder communication (Lewellen & Lewellen 2022: reaching 25% of shares requires contacting only ~5 institutions).
- Section 16(b) short-swing profit disgorgement above 10% disciplines the 6%-target block choice.
- Under-5% "under the threshold" campaigns were the **majority** of US campaigns in 2021; Salesforce 2022: five activists, none at 5% of a $130B market cap; mega-caps were 30% of 2024 targets (Barclays).
- 1–5% blockholders collectively own ~22% of the average firm vs ~20% for >5% blockholders (Lewellen & Lewellen 2022); smaller blockholders trade more.
- Becht et al. (2017): ~25% of >1,700 activism events involve multiple disclosing activists and perform "strikingly better"; Brav et al. (2008, p. 1757): other funds acquire stakes "within hours" of learning of the initial fund's position; Wong (2020): trigger-date trading abnormality +36%, the lead filer explains only 25% of turnover (unexplained = 240% of normal), past-relationship investors buy more; Flugum et al. (2023): institutional investors read targets' EDGAR files days before 13D filings (IP addresses), >95% of the activist's purchases precede follower access — trading is sequential; Brav et al. (2021b): disclosure-window return abnormality is highest for small caps and negative for mega caps; Hadlock & Schwartz-Ziv (2019): block interdependence negative overall but positive among "strategic investors" (HF/PE), stronger for smaller blocks; average HF investment duration > 530 days; Chabakauri et al. (2025): corporate insiders detect activist trades from order flow before disclosure.

**Referee-facing strengths:** a genuinely new trading result (predictability vs Kyle's 40-year benchmark); fully closed-form linear-Gaussian analysis; rich, current institutional grounding (2023–24 SEC actions); multiple robustness variants; connects to wolf packs and to measurable price abnormalities.
**Weaknesses:** Gaussian blocks admit negative positions/effort ("negative activism" is real but awkward); ρ is a deep parameter whose empirical sign is contested; existence fails for very negative ρ; effort ≡ terminal block is linear and drives everything; three periods only; and — most important for us — **the disclosure event itself is entirely off-model**: the model lives wholly inside the pre-disclosure window, there is no bidder, no takeover premium, and the 5% rule enters only as the reason blocks are private.

**Implications for repositioning:** (i) CCKV prove the pre-disclosure trading stage is strategically rich; our paper picks up exactly where they stop — at the crossing, the disclosure, and the *bidder* who reads the price. Frame: "CCKV endogenize what happens before the trigger; we endogenize what the trigger does to prices and control outcomes." (ii) Their result that liquidity (σ) does **not** eliminate manipulation (endogenous marginal incentive) is a close cousin of our mechanism and should be engaged head-on. (iii) Cite their footnote apparatus for our institutional facts section; it is more current than any other source on the 2023–24 rule changes. (iv) Their "under-5% campaigns are the majority" fact supports an empirical leg measuring disclosed vs undisclosed (inferred) activism — precisely our two branches.

# Synthesis across the strand

## A. How the literature treats the 13D-vs-13G choice
- **AFS 2022 is the only paper where the choice itself is the object of estimation**: a static binary choice over announcement-return distributions net of a constant private cost. It delivers the decomposition and the $60B/yr cost-sharing counterfactual, but the stake, the accumulation, and the crossing are exogenous.
- **J&S 2021**: 13-D filing ≡ campaign initiation technology; no 13G anywhere; filing timing fixed; the interesting state variable is reputation.
- **C&L 2025**: 13D filings are merely the campaign *identifier* (Audit Analytics); the stake is fixed at the 5% statutory threshold; no 13G, no filing choice.
- **OC&B 2022**: the threshold is a hard cap on secret accumulation with automatic revelation — a *level* margin, not a schedule-choice or timing margin.
- **CCKV 2026**: the threshold is the boundary between private and public information; the choice/timing of crossing is not modeled (the game ends before disclosure).
- **Nobody models the 13D/13G menu (intent-based: passive vs activist intent, 5-day window, Item-4 purpose statement) as an equilibrium choice jointly with stake accumulation and order-flow inference.** That is open territory, and it is the natural interface between our model and AFS-style estimation.

## B. What the literature estimates about takeover premia
- **Only C&L 2025 estimates anything about premia**: mean bid premium 36.6% (bid over price 25 days pre-bid); activism *lowers* premia by 13.7% (5.2pp) conditional on a deal, while raising deal probability 7.7%; the premium is Nash-bargaining output with an entrenchment wedge — **not microfounded in tendering/free-riding**.
- AFS: announcement returns (6.34%), not premia. J&S: campaign CAR 2.8%, project value 6.62%, not premia. OC&B: no bidder. CCKV: no bidder (effort-based value).
- Supporting cast: Greenwood & Schor (2009) — activist returns concentrate in campaigns ending in takeovers; Boyson et al. (2017) — activism mergers; Gantchev (2013) — campaign costs $10M; Jiang et al. (2018) — jawboning in risk arbitrage.
- **Open:** nobody links market *liquidity* or the *disclosure rule* to the size of takeover premia, theoretically or structurally. Our R1 (hump-shaped minority gains in κ) and the D7 microfounded wedge are unoccupied. The −13.7% activism–premium interaction (C&L) and the premium distribution are ready-made empirical anchors.

## C. The disclosure-threshold design literature
- **OC&B 2022 stands alone as the formal design paper**, and it is about the *level* of the threshold: optimal levels for investors/activist/regulator are never aligned (except at non-binding); society's lies between the other two; the activist may prefer a binding cap as commitment to intervene less. Cross-country levels (US 5%, Canada 10%, UK/Germany 3%, France 5%) and the WLRK/Bebchuk debate are the motivating facts.
- **The timing dimension has no design treatment**: the 2023 acceleration (10 → 5 business days; 2-day amendments), the group-formation/tipping guidance, and the 13F threshold proposals postdate OC&B. CCKV document these as institutional facts but do no design.
- **Open:** threshold/timing design interacted with *liquidity* and *takeover* margins. Our R2 (stricter disclosure attenuates the liquidity-sensitivity of premia) is a genuinely new comparative static — but to survive review it should be framed as a positive statement about the price-inference mechanism, not a welfare ranking (OC&B owns the welfare language).

## D. The remaining gap for a theory paper with an empirical anchor
1. **The joint trading–disclosure–premium nexus is unclaimed.** OC&B: disclosure design without takeovers. CCKV: pre-disclosure trading without disclosure or takeovers. AFS/J&S/C&L: estimation without microstructure. Our model is the only one with informed trading + stake-triggered disclosure (disclosed vs inferred inference branches) + bidder entry + a microfounded tender premium.
2. **Provability is the binding constraint, and the bar is visible.** OC&B and CCKV are 100% analytical, achieved via exponential (OC&B) and linear-Gaussian (CCKV) functional forms. A JF/RFS referee will hold our paper to that standard: R1 must be proved at least in a special case. Concretely: exponential noise à la OC&B/Edmans-2009 yields closed-form order-flow inference; endpoint symmetry Δ(0) = Δ(1) plus a single-crossing/concavity argument in a tractable special case is the minimum viable proof strategy.
3. **Ready-made empirical anchors for the repositioned paper:** (i) AFS's 6.34% / 75.2%-treatment / C = 4.6% of stake; (ii) C&L's 36.6% mean premium, −13.7% activism interaction, 7.7% facilitation, +0.33% threat value; (iii) trigger-date and under-threshold facts (1% bought on trigger day; median disclosed stake 6.6%; under-5% campaigns the majority in 2021; multi-activist events perform "strikingly better"); (iv) the 2023–24 rule changes as motivation — **currently unclaimed by any of the five papers** (the team skeleton's claim that J&S own this framing is wrong).
4. **Positioning moves:** cite OC&B as antecedent and differentiate on the takeover margin; engage CCKV on the pre-disclosure stage (we continue where they stop); use AFS/C&L estimates as explained quantities/calibration targets; exploit the AFS-vs-C&L treatment-share conflict (75% vs 9%) as evidence the announcement return's information content is unresolved — our disclosed/inferred branch structure is a way to think about it.
5. **Kill/de-emphasize:** normative optimal-threshold welfare rankings (OC&B); reputation dynamics (J&S); campaign-stage structural estimation ambitions for the current version (AFS/J&S/C&L) — keep it as a stated future leg.
