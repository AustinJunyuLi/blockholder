Noise trading and takeovers

A model of takeovers is investigated in which "noise trading" provides camouflage that
makes it possible for a large corporate outsider to purchase enough shares atfavorable prices
so that takeovers become profitable. Although the model accommodates the possibility of
dilution (Grossman and Hart, 1980) and a large incumbent shareholder (Shleifer and Vishny,
1986), neither dilution nor a large incumbent shareholder is necessary for costly takeovers
to be profitable. Noise trading tends to encourage costly takeovers that otherwise would not
occur and to discourage beneficial takeovers that otherwise would occur.


1. Introduction

* Firms in the risk arbitrage business use the stock market as a vehicle for speculating
about the occurrence of corporate takeovers. Since these firms buy and sell large quantities
of shares, they recognize their own market power by taking into account the depth of the
market. In a takeover situation, the amount held by the largest shareholder influences his
incentives to engineer a takeover, which would increase the value of the firm's shares. In
an efficient market, the price of a firm's stock discounts the probability of a takeover and
its implications for the firm's value. This article develops a simple model that makes it
possible to answer the following questions about such markets: What determines the depth
of the market in which informed traders buy and sell? How can profitable speculation by
risk arbitragers be reconciled with the economist's understanding of efficient markets? How
is asset pricing affected when the size of a large trader's holding of shares influences the
economic value of those shares?
      The model is based on the idea that "noise trading"-uninformative trading for liquidity
or life cycle motives-provides enough camouflage to enable a large outsider to profit by
acquiring a significant stake in a target firm without being noticed.' When there is a great
deal of noise trading, the market for a firm's shares tends to be deep, since the market
attributes changes in the quantity of shares supplied in the market to changes in noise

     We assume that one large informed trader has the ability to declare a takeover by

process, as well as shares owned before the trading process begins, will be worth more if a
takeover occurs than if it does not. The large trader can also speculate against a takeover
by taking a short position. In equilibrium, this large trader trades the quantity (positive or
negative) that maximizes his expected profits, taking into account his own effect on prices.
We use a rational expectations concept to show simultaneously both how the depth of the
market depends upon the trading strategy of a large trader and how the trading strategy of
the large trader depends upon the depth of the market.
     The model provides a perspective on takeovers different from those of both Grossman
and Hart (1980) and Shleifer and Vishny (1986).
     Grossman and Hart ( 1980) argue that takeovers which increase the value of a firm's
shares will not occur if the shares of the firm are initially diffusely held and the shares of
the target firm are worth exactly the same to a successful bidder as to incumbent shareholders.
Once shareholders realize that a value-enhancing takeover is going to occur, they have no
incentive to sell their shares for less than they would be worth after the takeover. To rationali
takeovers, Grossman and Hart suggest that an outside bidder can dilute the value of incum-
bents' shares by excluding incumbent shareholders from some of the gains the takeover
brings about. This dilution makes shares worth more to the bidder than to incumbent
shareholders and thus gives incumbent shareholders an incentive to sell to the bidder.
     Shleifer and Vishny (1986) emphasize that when one of the incumbent shareholders
is large, the prospect of increasing the value of shares he already owns provides an incentive
for the large shareholder to engage in takeovers and other value-enhancing activities, even
though other incumbent shareholders receive a free ride.
     The model discussed here includes both Grossman and Hart's dilution and Shleifer
and Vishny's large incumbent shareholder as motivations for profitable takeovers, but it
adds to these an additional effect arising from the interaction between noise traders and the
large informed trader. When noise traders are heavy sellers, the large informed trader notices
an opportunity to buy a large stake at favorable prices and does so. Having become a larger
shareholder, the large trader has an incentive to declare a takeover (by making a tender
offer for all outstanding shares), even if he incurs a cost in doing so. The large trader makes
a profit, and the noise traders, whose selling precipitated the takeover that increased the
value of the firm's shares, systematically lose out.
     Since trading against noise encourages costly takeovers, neither dilution nor a large
incumbent shareholder is necessary for costly takeovers to be profitable.
     In order to make the analysis tractable, the model makes the restrictive assumptions
that ( 1 ) only one large trader is eligible to declare a takeover and (2) this large trader is the
only trader who observes the aggregate trading activity of noise traders. The second as-
sumption could be relaxed by considering an equilibrium in supply curves (see Vila ( 1986)
and Kyle ( 1989) for an application to another case). The first assumption requires the
existence of some barrier to entry that prevents other potential large traders from engaging
in takeover activity. Such a barrier could be created if the large trader has a lower cost of
declaring takeovers than other large traders or a larger ex ante position in the commodity
(as in Shleifer and Vishny (1986)). Alternatively, a natural barrier to entry might prevent
two large traders from competing. Other restrictive assumptions are that noise trading has

2. A model of takeovers
* There are three kinds of traders in the market for a firm's shares: a large trader, noise
traders, and value-based investors. After one round of trading, positions are liquidated
according to predetermined rules that incorporate a reduced-form model of the takeover
process.
     In the one round of trading, the noise traders sell an exogenous random quantity
denoted i. The large trader observes i and places a "market order" to buy a quantity
denoted X(2). The large trader's strategy is a market order in the sense that he must choose
the quantity he buys before the price at which the order is executed is established. Actually,
the large trader's "trading strategy," X( ), is a mixed buying strategy because we allow the
quantity purchased or sold to be random. Thus for every distinct realization z, the quantity
purchased, X(z), is a random variable (degenerate in a pure strategy equilibrium). The
value-based investors observe the net supply Z - X(fi), but they do not observe Z
separately. They set a price denoted P(z - X(z)), where the "pricing rule," P( * ), is some
real-valued function. In this respect, the model is like the "market order" equilibrium of
Kyle ( 1985 ), with value-based investors here competing away profits and insuring efficient
markets like market makers in that discussion.
     The profits of the informed trader depend upon whether or not he declares a takeover
after trading occurs. The informed trader is assumed to have an option to declare a takeover,
which is modelled as follows:
     Let S denote the number of shares outstanding, including the holdings of the large
trader. If the informed trader does not declare a takeover, then each share is assumed to be
worth p*, so the firm is worth p*S. If the informed trader does declare a takeover, then the
informed trader makes a tender offer for the firm's outstanding shares, and the firm is
assumed to be worth (p* + v)S + G. Here, v is an exogenously specified premium the
informed trader must pay in a tender offer, and G represents gains the informed trader
realizes from diluting the values of existing shares. The total increase in the value of the
firm is vS + G. Of this amount, vS is given to existing shareholders (including the large
trader) in the tender offer, and the balance G is captured by the large trader for himself.
We do not model in this article where the premium v comes from; it is exogenously specified.
     The informed trader is assumed to incur a cost K (representing legal fees and other
expenses) when he declares a takeover. Let X0 denote the initial shareholdings of the informe
trader before trading takes place. Let f3 denote the net gains to the informed trader from
declaring a takeover (relative to not declaring a takeover) if he chooses not to trade. Since
the takeover allows the shares to be liquidated forp* + v instead of p*, the no-trade takeover
benefit fi is given by : = X0v + G - K. In this expression, X0v denotes the gain realized
from being a large shareholder, G represents the gain from dilution, and these two sources
of gains are netted against the cost K. If the informed trader trades, the gain from declaring
a takeover (relative to not declaring a takeover) is given by vx + A, i.e., the relative gain is
increased by vx, the difference between the no-takeover value and the takeover value of
the shares purchased in the round of trading. To maximize profits, the informed trader wil
declare a takeover if vx + fi is positive, not declare a takeover if vx + fi is negative, and b
indifferent if vx + fi is zero (in which case we make the innocuous assumption that a
takeover is declared).

6. Implications and other applications

* Our model can be viewed as an alternative approach to the problem of social cost posed
by Hirshleifer ( 1971 ). He argues that the ability to trade as a perfect competitor in advance
of undertaking a costly action which affects asset prices (like technological innovation)
increases the incentive to undertake such costly actions in the first place. Hirschleifer uses
this argument to suggest that speculation in anticipation of technological changes creates
incentives to undertake the innovations that offset the disincentives to undertake the in-
novations resulting from spillovers of benefits to others. Of course, since the argument does
not depend upon whether the technological change represents a socially efficient or inefficient
action, the same argument also implies that speculative opportunities create incentives to
undertake socially wasteful actions (like destroying capital goods) as well.

      Our approach assumes that when large traders participate in markets to earn a spec-
ulative profit from actions undertaken outside the marketplace, we would not ordinarily
expect the asset market to be perfectly competitive. The adverse selection resulting from
the large trader's participation in the market affects adversely the liquidity or depth of the
market. By increasing the cost of acquiring a large speculative position, this adverse selection
reduces the speculative incentive to undertake costly actions (like takeovers or squeezes).
Our approach ignores the social costs and benefits of such actions, because considering
them would raise different issues that take us beyond the scope of this article. Thus, without
further modelling, we cannot conclude that policies which reduce stock turnover, like a
transactions tax or more widespread use of passive portfolio management techniques, have
favorable or unfavorable welfare implications.

      Our definition of equilibrium involves certain technical assumptions that potentially affect the results in
Proposition 1. We discuss these here. First, the assumption that a takeover occurs when the large shareholder is

a demand curve instead of a fixed quantity. This issue is discussed by Vila ( 1986). Third, our assumption that
there are no restrictions on short sales sometimes modifies the equilibrium but not the substance of the results. A
short-sale restriction is considered in a working-paper version of this article (available from the authors).
