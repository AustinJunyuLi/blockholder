                                        Abstract
                                        Purpose – This study aims to examine the Securities and Exchange Commission’s ﬁnal rule in
                                        Modernization of Beneﬁcial Ownership Reporting, which reduced the time for signiﬁcant public company
                                        shareholders to ﬁle Schedule 13D (effective February 5, 2024). The authors corroborate prior results under the
                                        historic 10-day maximum reporting regime and provide updated academic analysis regarding how the ﬁve-
                                        day deadline between the “triggering” event, accumulating 5% of the outstanding shares and public
                                        disclosure of that event will affect abnormal returns.
                                        Design/methodology/approach – This empirical archival study uses publicly available data.
                                        Findings – The analyses show that changing from a 10-day to a 5-day Schedule 13 disclosure window will
                                        reduce activist investors’ opportunity to proﬁt by legally delaying the ﬁling of Schedule 13D. These excess
                                        returns for delay exist regardless of the proﬁtability or size of the target ﬁrm or the shareholder’s disclosed
                                        reason for ﬁling. The authors conclude that accelerating the timing of the disclosure window is an
                                        improvement that is in the best interest of the general investing public.
                                        Originality/value – To the authors’ knowledge, this is the only academic study of Schedule 13D ﬁlings to
                                        include the postpandemic period. As such, the authors establish an updated “baseline projection” for
                                        expectations regarding how the Modernization ﬁnal rule will impact activist investors and stock returns
                                        under a ﬁve-day reporting regime. In addition, the authors measure and test abnormal returns after
                                        considering differences between “triggering” events and ﬁling dates of Schedule 13Ds in the sample rather

                 1. Introduction
                 Section 13 of the Exchange Act requires activist investors who qualify as “beneﬁcial                                                            517
                 owners” of greater than 5% of a class of equity securities to ﬁle Schedule 13D. In this study,
                 we follow and extend prior research related to Schedule 13D ﬁrst-time ﬁlers because these
                 investors potentially have, and often clearly express in their ﬁlings, interest in “changing or
                 inﬂuencing control of the issuer” of the securities [1]. We focus on the timing implications of
                 the Securities and Exchange Commission’s (SEC) Modernization of Beneﬁcial Ownership
                 Reporting ﬁnal rule (Securities and Exchange Commission [SEC], 2023a, 2023b, 2023c),
                 which decreased the Schedule 13D reporting deadline from 10 to 5 days once 5% ownership
                 (or greater) is achieved [2]. The change became effective February 5, 2024. Critics of the 10-
                 day window assert that it reduces market transparency, allows activist investors to ambush
                 public companies, undermines market participants’ ability to prudently manage their
                 positions and inhibits capital formation (SEC, 2023a, 2023b, 2023c, pp. 17–24).
                     We estimate the economic impact of reducing the 13D ﬁling period from 10 to 5 days on
                 non-activist investors who are assumed, at best, to learn about a change in ownership when
                 Schedule 13D appears in the SEC electronic data gathering, analysis, and retrieval (EDGAR)
                 system (i.e. the public ﬁling date). Speciﬁcally, we compare excess returns associated with
                 initial ﬁlings made by investors ﬁling a Schedule 13D within ﬁve days (i.e. those that meet
                 the modernization requirement) with those ﬁling between 6 and 10 days (i.e. those that meet
                 the historic requirement). By comparing the two groups of ﬁlings, we can quantify the
                 portion of excess returns that accrues during the “quiet period” of time prior to required
                 public disclosure.
                     The SEC adopted Modernization of Beneﬁcial Ownership Reporting noting that the historic
                 10-day ﬁling deadline “contributes to information asymmetries that could harm investors”
                 (SEC, 2023a, 2023b, 2023c, p. 16). Indeed, research has shown that relatively sophisticated
                 investors obtain abnormal gains before public 13D ﬁlings (e.g. Flugum et al., 2023; von
                 Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler, 2020; DiMaggio, Franzoni, Kermani, and Sommavilla, 2019).
                 Given recent signiﬁcant market changes (see Cascaldi-Gargia et al., 2023), we corroborate key
                 prior ﬁndings and extend the academic analysis to the postpandemic time horizon.
                     Like von Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler (2020), we ﬁnd that most excess market returns
                 associated with new-owner 13D ﬁlings are obtained after the purchase date that requires the
                 ﬁling of the Schedule 13D (hereafter, “trigger date”) but before public disclosure of the
                 ownership stake. Notably, we ﬁnd that, on average, activist investors enjoy an increase in
                 abnormal returns for each additional day between the trigger date and the 13D ﬁling date.
                 Speciﬁcally, the excess returns increase from 1.34% for those ﬁling on the trigger date to
                 5.09% for those ﬁling on the tenth day following the trigger date. This evidence is consistent
                 with the contention that the 10-day maximum ﬁling deadline incentivized activist investors
                 to delay ﬁling as long as legally possible. Given the ease of ﬁling Schedule 13D and the fact
                 that many investors have historically ﬁled within ﬁve days, we argue that the historic 10-
                 day maximum was an unnecessary delay, providing an opportunity for informed (Flugum
                 et al., 2023) and activist investors to derive gains from an information asymmetry that
                 harmed other investors.

                                     2. An example Schedule 13D ﬁling
                                     As noted by recent investigations of 8-K accelerated ﬁling changes (Watkins, 2022, Rawson
                                     et al., 2022), the timing of disclosure and information issued around the time of the disclosure
                                     matters to investors. The Modernization of Beneﬁcial Ownership Reporting rule reduces the
                                     maximum delay after the “trigger date,” when a new owner meets or exceeds the 5%
                                     ownership level, from 10 business days to 5. We presume most investors who own signiﬁcant
                                     percentages of public companies’ shares track the number of shares they own in those
                                     companies and are aware of the 5% ownership reporting rule. Public companies report their
                                     numbers of outstanding shares quarterly and these reports are easy to access. Therefore, the
                                     additional burden for ﬁling within ﬁve, instead of ten, days seems small, especially compared
                                     to the resulting reduction in information asymmetry that we estimate below.
                                         Our Schedule 13D example, discussed below, further demonstrates that any additional
                                     burden based on accelerated ﬁling associated with modernization appears limited to
                                     resolving confusion regarding when a shareholder has obtained more than 5% of the issuing
                                     company’s stock based on the issuer’s most recent quarterly or annual report [3]. A
                                     relatively high-proﬁle example is Ryan Cohen’s Schedule 13D ﬁling disclosing his initial
                                     beneﬁcial ownership disclosure of Bed Bath & Beyond Inc (BBBY) [4]. In Appendix 1, we
                                     provide a brief timeline of the events and deadlines surrounding a typical 13D ﬁling,
                                     including Mr. Cohen’s. Furthermore, as shown in Appendix 2, the 13D cover page provides
                                     the “trigger date” when the reporting person (jointly, Mr. Cohen and Mr. Cohen’s RC
                                     Ventures LLC) ﬁrst exceeds 5% ownership of Bed Bath & Beyond.
                                         Like most Schedule 13Ds, this ﬁling’s cover page begins with information regarding
                                     the security and the issuer (Item 1). Next, it includes the identity and background of the
                                     purchaser (Item 2) and the source of funds (or other consideration) used to make the
                                     purchase (Item 4). The cover page of Mr. Cohen’s ﬁling, shown in Appendix 2, states that

                                     ownership in BBBY common stock. By purchasing 1,667,833 shares for $23.7m on February

                 some provide detailed descriptions of changes that new beneﬁcial owners would like to see
                 occur at an issuer company. The relatively descriptive nature of Mr. Cohen’s 13D ﬁling
                 allows one to see the timing issue related to the SEC’s Modernization of Beneﬁcial Ownership
                 Reporting rule. We struggle to see additional burden based on accelerated ﬁling.

                 3. Literature review
                 3.1 Abnormal returns and potential insider trading
                 To determine if speciﬁc investors can realize positive abnormal returns around the ﬁling of a
                 Schedule 13D, von Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler (2020) examine 13,413 initial 13D ﬁlings in
                 the center for research in security prices (CRSP) universe from 2001 to 2016. They ﬁnd that
                 excess returns and trading volume spike at the trigger date. Notably, they do not ﬁnd any
                 differences between the type of activist investor and abnormal returns, which they show
                 persist beyond the ﬁling date of Schedule 13D. Overall, the authors provide evidence of
                 excess returns between the trigger date and ﬁling date of original Schedule 13Ds and
                 persistent returns beyond the ﬁling date. This suggests a combination of positive
                 externalities from activist investors to stockholder value, as well as the possibility of insider
                 trading.
                     The SEC deﬁnes “insider trading” as “buying or selling a security, in breach of a
                 ﬁduciary duty or other relationship of trust and conﬁdence, on the basis of material,
                 nonpublic information about the security” (SEC, 2023a, 2023b, 2023c). Using EDGAR search
                 activity and investor internet protocol addresses, Flugum et al. (2023) provide powerful
                 evidence that activist investors pass material nonpublic information (the presence of an
                 activist investor) to investment management ﬁrms (or investment banks) during the “quiet

                                     and the confederate ﬁrm receiving the information is to establish a symbiotic relationship

                                     acquires shares before the market reacts to the activist’s ﬁling of its Schedule 13D, usually

                                     3.2 SEC modernization and abnormal returns
                                     Quantifying the potential beneﬁts of a reduced “quiet period” must be weighed against the
                                     need for shareholders and wealth managers to invest in new technologies to gather necessary
                                     information [7]. To analyze the proposed regulation requiring the initial ﬁling of a Schedule
                                     13D within ﬁve days of the trigger date, the SEC staff (2023b) assesses the economic effects of
                                     the modernization proposal using 2011–2021 data for noncorporate action ﬁlings [8]. They
                                     ﬁnd signiﬁcant positive excess returns on each day from the “trigger date” (day 0) to day 5
                                     following the last legal ﬁling date (day þ15). In other words, as the authors of the SEC
                                     Division of Economic and Risk Analysis report note, shortening the deadline may ultimately
                                     reduce information asymmetry and improve market efﬁciency (SEC, 2023a, 2023b, 2023c).
                                         This evidence, however, does not address two issues. First, the prior literature and SEC
                                     analyses do not examine excess returns and trading volume while accounting for the time
                                     difference between the ﬁling date and trigger date, inherently grouping all shareholder
                                     ﬁlings together. This is notable because approximately 45% of original Schedule 13D ﬁlings
                                     in 2022 were ﬁled before the ﬁnal day to ﬁle [9]. Second, given the relative age of the sample
                                     in prior academic literature, it is an open question if improved technology and
                                     communication has mitigated or exacerbated the impact of information asymmetry between
                                     activist and other investors. Here, we speciﬁcally note the popular press discussion of
                                     “gambling on meme stocks” (Michaels and Thomas, 2023) as a recent phenomenon that
                                     could beneﬁt from updated academic analysis as we transition to the new ﬁve-day reporting
                                     regime. Formally, we state our research question as the following:

                                         RQ1. Do activist investors increase their excess returns by delaying the ﬁling of Schedule
                                              13D?

                 4.2 Returns and trading volume during the ﬁling window
                 While the abnormal returns around the trigger date provide evidence of activist investors
                 and other insiders receiving positive returns before the public disclosure of their
                 investments, many investors ﬁled well before the 10-day maximum delay. Therefore, we
                 analyze the abnormal returns based on the days between the trigger and ﬁling dates.
                 Speciﬁcally, we analyze the sum of the daily abnormal returns until the actual ﬁling date.
                 For example, if an investor crosses the 5% threshold on Monday, March 1, and ﬁles on
                 Friday, March 5, we take the sum of the abnormal returns from day 0 (March 1) to day 4
                 (March 5).

                                     5. Additional analysis
                                     5.1 Firm characteristics
                                     To examine if the results are consistent when considering other ﬁrm characteristics, we test
                                     various subsamples of the population in an attempt to capture activist investors who might
                                     target speciﬁc types of businesses. For example, our results may be limited to a subset of
                                     investors who seek to invest in ﬁrms reporting quarterly losses with the intent to inﬂuence
                                     management to make speciﬁc changes that will change the level of proﬁtability. To test this
                                     possibility, we compare excess returns in the quiet period among ﬁrms reporting quarterly
                                     losses and proﬁts. In an untabulated analysis, we ﬁnd a consistent pattern of returns in the
                                     quiet period among ﬁrms reporting quarterly losses and quarterly proﬁts but do not ﬁnd a
                                     statistically signiﬁcant difference in abnormal returns between the two groups (Figure 5).
                                         Additionally, it is possible that the returns may be concentrated among activist investments
                                     for speciﬁc reasons, such as those where the investor disagrees with the current company
                                     strategy. As shown in Figure 6, Panels AG, the return pattern does not signiﬁcantly deviate
                                     by reason, with abnormal returns, on average, increasing as the number of days in the quiet
                                     period increases.
                                         Furthermore, prior literature suggests that abnormal returns are greater for smaller
                                     ﬁrms (Easterday, Sen, and Stephan, 2009; Kleim, 1983). Therefore, the increase in excess
                                     returns following the ﬁling of Schedule 13D may be concentrated among investments in
                                     ﬁrms that are smaller in size. To test this possibility, we compare the returns around the
                                     ﬁling of Schedule 13Ds disclosing investments in large accelerated and accelerated ﬁlers
                                     versus other ﬁlers given that such ﬁrms experience different information environments. As
                                     shown in Table 4, activist investors reporting investments in both groups of companies
                                     realize increasing abnormal returns as the number of days in the quiet period increases.

                                     6. Discussion and conclusion
                                     In this study, we provide evidence in support of the timing aspect of Modernization of
                                     Beneﬁcial Ownership Reporting. The main drivers of this conclusion are (1) Schedule 13D is a
                                     simple and easy form to complete, (2) a large percentage of 13D ﬁlings historically have
                                     occurred in compliance with a ﬁve-day reporting deadline (i.e. this truly is “easy to ﬁle”) and
                                     (3) a large portion of excess returns is earned during the “quiet period” between the new
                                     reporting deadline (ﬁve days) and the historic deadline (10 days after the trigger date).
                                     Speciﬁcally, our analyses show that activist investors increase their excess returns from
                                     1.34% to 5.09% by legally delaying their Schedule 13D ﬁlings to 10 days.
                                         With that said, we note a few limitations with our analyses. First, our analyses are unable
                                     to rule out idiosyncratic changes to regulations, court decisions or other market forces that

      JFRC                           Appendix 4. Collection of the trigger date and sample selection
      32,4                           The date that requires ﬁling a Schedule 13D (“trigger date”) is not available from traditional data
                                     aggregators (like Audit Analytics) or the SEC directly. Therefore, to obtain the trigger date, we
                                     download each Schedule 13D available in the EDGAR database and scrape the trigger date from the
                                     corresponding tab delineated ﬁle.
                                          To complete this process, we ﬁrst use the R package getFilings to download available Schedules
      538                            13D within our sample period. Next, we use Python to parse the available ﬁlings, obtaining the ﬁrst
                                     date preceding “Date of Event.” Due to inconsistent formatting among ﬁlings (some underline “Date
                                     of Event,” some include extra spaces, many provide the date in a format inconsistent with most
                                     ﬁlings), we lose 23,820 Schedules 13D. As described in our conclusion, standardizing a date format in
                                     Schedule 13D would eliminate such data loss (which is also experienced by SEC staff in their
                                     analyses). However, we retain 14,635 ﬁlings between 2001 and 2022 after also removing records
                                     where the ﬁling date is listed as before the trigger date and where the difference between the trigger
                                     date and ﬁling date is over 30 days. Appendix Table A1 summarizes the sample selection process.
