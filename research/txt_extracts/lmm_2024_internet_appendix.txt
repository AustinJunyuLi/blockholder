D.3. Voting to Delegate to the Board
    In this section, we consider the decision of shareholders to delegate power to the board. Due
to shareholder heterogeneity, even the optimal board, which maximizes aggregate shareholder
welfare, may harm some of them. Those shareholders may prefer to retain their voting rights.
This raises the question of whether the majority of the initial shareholders would give up their
right to vote and leave the choice to the board that improves aggregate welfare. In other words,
can we expect shareholders to reach a consensus on delegation?
    To answer this question, we analyze the following extension. Suppose that at the outset

                                                                 32
of the game, before the trading stage, shareholders choose between two alternatives: (i) all
shareholders retain their voting rights, as in the baseline model, and (ii) shareholders delegate
decision-making authority to a board with an exogenously given bias bm , which then decides
on the proposal. Decision-making is delegated to the board if and only if a fraction of at least
  of the shareholders support delegation.
    In Proposition IA.9 we show that the optimal board, as characterized by Proposition IA.7,
cannot always garner support from at least of the initial shareholders. The main reason
behind this coordination failure corresponds to short-term trading considerations that distort
shareholder votes on board delegation. To understand the intuition, consider the activist
equilibrium and suppose that the median voter is more activist than the average post-trade
shareholder (i.e., a < qa ), that is, there are welfare gains from delegating decision rights
to a board that is more conservative than the median voter. However, notice that in general,
shareholders who expect to buy shares (b > ba ) would like to reduce the share price pa .
Since the share price is given by the valuation of the marginal shareholder ba , shareholders
with bias b > ba have incentives to support boards that the marginal shareholder dislikes.
This consideration ampli…es their incentives to support activist boards. Essentially, buying
shareholders support boards that are more activist than they are, since they internalize the
negative e¤ect that such boards will have on the value of the marginal shareholder and in turn
on the price at which they buy. For that reason, even when there are more than of the initial
shareholders that are more conservative than the median voter, they cannot agree to delegate
their voting rights to even a marginally more conservative board, and in particular, to the
optimal board.
    Overall, we show that when voting occurs prior to trading, short-term trading considerations
may push shareholders to make suboptimal delegation decisions in order to gain from trading.

C. Trading with Partial Sales of Endowments
    Our basic model assumes that shareholders can sell their entire endowment. In this section,
we relax this assumption to incorporate scenarios in which trading frictions are particularly
high and do not allow initial shareholders to exit the …rm completely. Speci…cally, we assume
