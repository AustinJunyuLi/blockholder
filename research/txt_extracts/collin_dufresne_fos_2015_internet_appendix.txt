This appendix is in five sections. Section I provides further details on the sample of
Schedule 13D trades, trading strategies of Schedule 13D filers. In addition, it gives
a detailed description of a specific case. Section II shows that Schedule 13D filers
posses valuable private information. Section III describes liquidity measures used in
the analysis. Section IV shows robustness of main results. Section V presents further
evidence on the selection and limit order mechanisms.

A. Sample Description

   Figure IA.1 presents the time distribution of the Schedule 13D filings with infor-
mation on trades in common stocks during 1994 to 2010. During the sample period,
on average 184 events take place each year. Importantly, the sample covers a 17-year
period, during which several changes in microstructure took place.

   Table IA.I reports the industry classification of Schedule 13D targets. Firms are
classified into 48 Fama-French industries. Column (1) reports the percentage of Schedule
13D targets for the 12 most represented industries among Schedule 13D targets. Column
(2) reports the percentage of public firms in those 12 industries. The evidence indicates
that the population of Schedule 13D targets does not differ from the universe of public
firms as far as the industry classification is concerned.

   Next we examine how the characteristics of targets correlate with the likelihood of a
Schedule 13D filing. We use probit regressions to estimate the partial effects of several
covariates on the likelihood of a Schedule 13D filing. Results are reported in Table IA.II.

   Activist shareholders do not target firms randomly. Potential targets have smaller
market cap (lower MV), are more mature (higher CRSP AGE), invest more in capital
expenditures (higher CAPEX), and have lower market valuation (higher BOOK-TO-
MARKET). Potential targets are also characterized by high institutional ownership
(higher INST), which indicates a more sophisticated shareholder base, whose support
dissident shareholders often need to succeed with the activism. Targets’ shares are
more liquid (lower ILLIQUIDITY) and have higher idiosyncratic volatility (higher
IDIO VOLATILITY). Overall, the evidence is consistent with the notion that activist
shareholders select to target firms with more severe agency problems (e.g., Brav, Jiang,
, Partnoy, and Thomas (2008), Fos (2014)).

   It is important to emphasize that our results are robust to any bias in terms of
industry and firm characteristics. In contrast to several empirical papers in corporate
finance and microstructure, the main evidence in this paper comes from comparing days
with and without informed trading for the same firm within a 60-day period prior to the
filing date. The key point is that firms’ industry classifications and main characteristics
are not likely to change back and forth at a daily frequency.


B. Trading Strategy of Schedule 13D Filers

   In Section I we analyze the trading strategy of Schedule 13D filers as a function of
the distance to the filing date. Figure IA.2 presents similar analysis, but presents the
strategy of Schedule 13D filers as a function of the distance to the event date.

   Figure IA.2 indicates that the probability that a Schedule 13D filer trades at least one
share on a given day increases gradually closer to the event date, reaches the 100% level
on the event date, and then gradually declines. Figure IA.2 also suggests that a Schedule
13D filer gradually increases the percentage of outstanding shares purchased on every
trading day until the event date. For example, the average percentage of outstanding
shares purchased on every trading day by the Schedule 13D filers increases from (0.03%
to 0.05%) to (0.10% to 0.15%) closer to the event date. On the event date it reaches
0.92% of shares outstanding. It then gradually decreases.

C. Profits

   We calculate three measures of profits. First, we calculate Schedule 13D filers’ profits
from purchasing shares at the pre-announcement prices,


                            T rading P rof it = q0 (ppost − p),                     (IA.1)


where q is the vector of trades (purchases are positive and sales are negative) during the
60-day period, ppost is the post-announcement price, and p is the vector of transaction
prices. The post-announcement price is the average stock price during the week that
follows the Schedule 13D filing.

   If Schedule 13D filers do indeed own valuable private information, they would be
expected to purchase shares at a discount relative to the post-announcement price. Of

                                            11
course, by purchasing securities Schedule 13D filers may also push up prices. Thus, their
cumulative profits also depend on the price impact of their trades. If price impact is
large, then we expect realized profits of informed traders to be lower than if price impact
is low. Thus, the trading profits of Schedule 13D filers depend both on the value of their
private information and stock price liquidity.

   Second, we calculate the total profits realized by informed investors,


                   T otal P rof it = T rading P rof it + (ppost − p0 )w0 ,          (IA.2)


where p0 is the price of the first transaction and w0 is the initial ownership, which is
established before the 60-day period. This measure assumes that a Schedule 13D filer
purchases the initial stake at the price of the first transaction. This assumption is most
likely to cause a downward bias in estimated total profits.

   Finally, we report the total value created for the shareholders of a company that
experience a Schedule 13D filing,


                         V alue Created = (ppost − p0 )SHOU T,                      (IA.3)


where SHOU T is the number of shares outstanding.

   Table IA.IV presents the distribution of trading profits. We split the sample into five

suggests that informed traders make significant profits. For example, a Schedule 13D
filer who acquires a $22 million stake in a $293 million market cap company (i.e., a 7.51%

   The realized spread is the temporary component of the effective spread. It measures
the revenue to liquidity providers assuming that the liquidity provider is able to close
her position at the midpoint prevailing five minutes after the trade. For a given stock i
and day t, the realized spread on the kth trade is defined as


                            rspreaditk = 2qitk (ln(Pitk ) − ln(Mitk+5 )),            (IA.12)

volume-weighted average of the realized spread rspreaditk computed over all trades on
day t.

                              D.1. Regression Specification


   To check the robustness of our findings, we adopt the regression methodology used
in Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011) and estimate

are event fixed effects. The sample is restricted to the (t − 60, t) period around the
filing date. Event fixed effects absorb differences in levels of liquidity measures between
events. Therefore, the estimated coefficients exploit only the within-event variation in
liquidity measures.

   Panel A in Table IA.IX reports the results. Odd columns report results for unmatched

                         D.3. Order vs. Quote-Driven Markets


   In our sample, 62% of companies are listed on the NASDAQ and 29% are listed on the
NYSE (the remaining 9% are listed on the Amex). To test whether adverse selection and
liquidity measures perform differently across NASDAQ and NYSE market structures,
we split the sample into NASDAQ- and NYSE-listed stocks. Garfinkel and Nimalendran
(2003) suggest that there is a difference in the degree of anonymity between NASDAQ
and NYSE market structures. Specifically, they find evidence that is consistent with less
anonymity on the NYSE specialist system compared to the NASDAQ dealer system.
After Reg NMS was implemented, however, post-Reg NMS U.S. exchanges have become
more similar in structures and trading mechanisms.


                            D.2. Crisis vs. Noncrisis Period


   Almost 25% of events in the sample take place during the financial crisis. Since the
financial crisis period was characterized by higher trading volume and volatility, we want
to test whether adverse selection and liquidity measures reveal the presence of informed
trading in the pre-crisis period (i.e., pre-2007 period). To perform the test, we restrict
our sample to the pre-crisis period. Panel B in Table IA.IX reports the results. The
evidence suggests that results are not affected by removing the financial crisis period
from the sample.

C. Matching Schedule 13D Trades and TAQ Data

   In general, Schedule 13D requires disclosure at the daily level and not at the
transaction level. Therefore, our sample of 292,551 Schedule 13D transactions consists
of both aggregated (to the daily level) transaction data and specific transactions. We
match transaction data with TAQ data on the following dimensions: transaction date,
transaction price, and transaction quantity. The matching procedure leaves us with

                                           28
108,706 transactions, or 37% of the sample, which suggests that often Schedule 13D
filers disclose aggregated transaction data.

   Next, we require a Schedule 13D transaction to have a unique match to TAQ data.
The match on date, quantity, and price is not unique because Schedule 13D filers do not
disclose the exact timing of each transaction. The matching procedure leaves us with
12,576 trades that have a unique match to the TAQ data.

   There is one final concern, however, about the quality of the match. While 12,576
reported trades have a unique match to TAQ data on transaction date, transaction
price, and transaction quantity, it is possible that Schedule 13D filers report aggregated
information about a series of transactions and that the aggregated “transaction” has a
unique match to the TAQ data. To mitigate this concern, we restrict the sample to the
filings in which Schedule 13D filers disclose at least five transaction on a given day. Our
assumption is that in these cases Schedule 13D filers are likely to disclose information
on true and not aggregated trades. There is almost no change in the results.

trade, order imbalance is more negative. However, γ2 is positive and significant, which
shows that on days when the average price paid by Schedule 13D filers is higher than
vwap buy, order imbalance increases. This is consistent with the usage of market orders
by the filers on these days.

    When we analyze excess stock returns, we find that prices appreciate more when
Schedule 13D filers are likely to use market orders. While on a typical trading day with
informed trading the excess returns are 0.52%, when Schedule 13D filers are likely to


                                                     30
use market orders the excess returns increase by an additional 0.21% to 0.73%. Overall,
when our proxy indicates usage of market orders by Schedule 13D filers, informed trades
have a positive and significant impact on order imbalance and excess stock returns. This
suggests that our proxy for the usage of market orders by Schedule 13D filers is likely
to be valid.


E. Autoquoting on NYSE

   We perform a test on the role of limit orders on NYSE. The test exploits the start
of autoquoting on NYSE (Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld (2011)).            Previously,
specialists were responsible for manually disseminating the inside quote. This was
replaced in early 2003 by a new automated quote system whenever there was a change
to the NYSE limit order book. Presumably, the ability of Schedule 13D traders to rely
on limit orders must have been enhanced by the reform. Therefore, the limit order
mechanism implies weaker results during the pre-reform period. Table IA.XI reports
the results. The evidence suggests that the main result of the paper holds prior to the
start of autoquoting on NYSE as well, indicating that the limit order mechanism is not
the only mechanism that explains the main result.
