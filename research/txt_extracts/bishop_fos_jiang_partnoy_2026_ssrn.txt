                                                ABSTRACT

         We explore the intersection of shareholder activism and antitrust regulation.
         Congress established antitrust laws to address monopoly behavior, stifled
         competition, and economic concentration. However, antitrust regulation has had
         unanticipated impacts. In this paper, we show that the premerger notification
         requirements of the Hart-Scott-Rodino (“HSR”) Act affect the behavior of activist
         investors. Exploiting the HSR notification threshold, we provide causal evidence
         that activists are less likely to target firms when a “toehold” position would trigger
         HSR disclosure requirements prior to a Schedule 13D filing. Contrary to some
         suggestions by policymakers, we find no evidence that activism increases industry
         concentration. Instead, concentration appears to decrease after activist
         intervention. Our results support policy changes to the HSR premerger notification
         rules, including its threshold and investment-only exception.

                                         1. INTRODUCTION

        For more than a century, Section 7 of the Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 (“Clayton Act”)

has prohibited mergers and acquisitions where “the effect of such acquisition may be substantially

to lessen competition, or to tend to create a monopoly” (15 U.S.C. § 18). According to the Supreme

Court, the “dominant theme” motivating the Clayton Act and its subsequent amendments was

Congressional wariness of rising economic concentration (see United States v. Brown Shoe, 370

U.S. 294 [1962]; United States v. Philadelphia National Bank, 374 U.S. 321 [1963]; United States

v. Baker Hughes Inc., 908 F.2d 981 [D.C. Cir. 1990]). In accordance with this theme, a majority of

mergers subject to enforcement actions by the Department of Justice (“DOJ”) and the Federal Trade

Commission (“FTC”) (together, the “Agencies”) in the past forty years have been horizontal

mergers that increase concentration within a certain geographic and product market (Salop 2018).

        Congress has amended the Clayton Act periodically, in response to perceived weak spots

in Section 7 enforcement. One of the most significant amendments was the premerger notification

requirements of the Hart-Scott-Rodino Antitrust Improvements Act of 1976 (Pub L. 94-435, 90

Stat. 1383, codified at 15 U.S.C. § 18a) (“HSR Act” or “HSR”). The HSR requirements

significantly impacted both merger enforcement and investing activity. (Baer 1997; Sims and

Herman 1997)

        We examine a previously-unstudied potential HSR impact: the effects of HSR premerger

notification rules on activist investors. In particular, we observe that the HSR rules, and the so-called

“investment-only” HSR exception, impose direct monetary costs on activist compliance and

acquisition costs, and increase uncertainty about future costs. We examine the effects of these costs

empirically. By exploiting the HSR notification threshold, we provide causal evidence that activists

are less likely to target firms when a “toehold” position would trigger HSR disclosure requirements.

We also test whether the HSR rules achieve antitrust law objectives regarding competition and
3                                                                                        Journal Name



concentration. We find no evidence that activism increases industry concentration; instead,

concentration appears to decrease following activist intervention.

                                    2. INSTITUTIONAL SETTING

        Activist investors acquire shares against a backdrop of various overlapping regulatory

regimes, including antitrust law. In this part, we detail the legislative and regulatory antitrust rules

that relate to activist behavior.

                                2.1. The Premerger Notification Laws

        Congress enacted the HSR Act in 1976 to improve the efficiency and effectiveness of the

enforcement of Section 7 and to “advance the Clayton Act’s goal of preventing undue consolidation

or stopping it in its incipiency” (89 Fed. Reg. 89216). The HSR Act requires parties that agree to

mergers or acquisitions above certain size thresholds, in absolute dollar terms, to submit a

premerger notification filing to the FTC and observe a waiting period before the transaction can be

lawfully consummated (15 U.S.C. § 18a[a]). The filings must disclose the persons involved, the

structure of the transaction, financial data, certain documents filed with the Securities and Exchange

Commission, and documents that pertain to the planning and evaluation of the proposed transaction

(FTC 2024a, p. 6). During the waiting period of thirty days from the date of filing (fifteen days for

an all-cash tender offer or an acquisition in bankruptcy), the FTC or DOJ conduct a preliminary

review of the proposed transaction for potential antitrust issues. This review involves analyzing the

disclosures submitted by the parties and, in some cases, contacting third parties with knowledge of

the target market for their opinions and insights on the transaction (15 U.S.C. § 18a[b]; FTC 2024a,

p. 2). Those who fail to comply with the HSR Act are liable for civil penalties which accrue for

each day the violation is ongoing (15 U.S.C. § 18a[g][1]). Effective January 17, 2025, the maximum

civil penalty for failure to comply is $53,088 per day (Adjustments to Civil Penalty Amounts, 90

Fed. Reg. 5,580 [January 17, 2025]).

        One of the key measures of economic concentration that regulators use to identify

potentially illegal horizontal mergers, including at the premerger review stage, is the Herfindahl-

Hirschman Index (“HHI”). HHI defines concentration as the sum of the squares of the market shares

of each firm in a given market (Herfindahl 1950; Hirschman 1945; FTC and DOJ 2023, pp. 5-6).

Since 1962, the Agencies have regularly published non-binding merger guidelines stating the HHI

metrics that they consider to raise the probability of an antitrust issue (DOJ and FTC 2023, p. 5 n.

15). HHI accordingly is a key determinant of further investigation into a proposed merger.

        Premerger review has three potential outcomes. First, parties may request “Early

Termination,” whereby the reviewing agency terminates the review period prior to its 30 day

expiration (15 U.S.C. § 18a(b)(2); 16 C.F.R. 803.11(c)). Notice of Early Termination is provided

to the parties submitting the transaction and one day later is published on the FTC’s website.

Second, the review period may simply expire at the end of thirty days, with the reviewing agency

taking no further action. Finally, the FTC or DOJ may initiate a “second request” for additional

information to further investigate the proposed transaction (15 U.S.C. § 18(e); FTC 2024a, p. 10).

A second request extends the waiting period another thirty days (ten days in the case of a cash

tender offer or bankruptcy sale) beginning from the date all parties have substantially complied

with the second request. The Agencies may then seek a negotiated settlement that resolves concerns

about potential anticompetitive effects, or they may sue to block the deal (FTC 2024a, p. 12).

        The HSR Act requires reporting not only for whole-company acquisitions, but also partial

stock purchases, such as cash tender offers or purchases of an issuer’s shares on a national

exchange. This is because HSR mandates reporting for any acquisitions of voting securities above

the notification thresholds prescribed by the Act and its implementing rules (with certain

exemptions listed in 15 U.S.C. § 18a[c]; 16 C.F.R. pt. 802). The minimum notification threshold is

$50 million (as adjusted) if the parties meet certain size requirements. (If the transaction is valued

                                         2.2. HSR Legislative History

           Congress enacted HSR to capture “very large mergers and acquisitions” that may present

anticompetitive problems, as measured by the standard set out in § 7 of the Clayton Act (H. Rep.

No. 94-1373 (1976)). The legislative history supports a conclusion that Congress was aware of the

salutary effects of investment activity on competition and hoped HSR would not discourage

procompetitive investments (see, for instance, H. Rep. No. 94-1373 at 12 (1976)). The legislative

history does not support a conclusion that HSR targeted investors purchasing relatively modest

minority stakes in companies. Instead, HSR represents Congress’s careful balancing of the need

for more effective enforcement of the Clayton Act with its desire to minimize the burdens of

premerger notification on market participants (S. Rep. No. 94-803 pt. 1 [1976], p. 65).

        HSR was enacted to deal with three problems that hindered effective enforcement of the

Section 7 (Baer 1997; S. Rep. No. 94-803 pt. 1 [1976], pp. 64-65). The first was the problem of the

“midnight merger,” in which a transaction was consummated before the Agencies had adequate

time to seek to enjoin it. The second was the long delay the Agencies experienced in obtaining

relief, given merged companies’ incentivizes to delay litigation that could result in a divestiture

order. Even when relief was granted, the Agencies encountered a third problem known as

“unscrambling the eggs”: once merged companies integrated their operations and assets, it was

difficult to obtain a divestiture that could create viable businesses capable of competing effectively

(S. Rep. No. 94-803 pt. 1 [1976], pp. 7-8, 64-65; H. Rep. No. 94-1373 [1976], p. 11). In accordance

with this purpose, Representative Rodino, one of the architects of the HSR Act, wrote in a House

Report that the Act would apply only to mergers that were most likely to “substantially lessen

competition” and are “the most difficult to unscramble” (H. Rep. No. 94-1373 [1976], p. 11).

        Given that shareholder activism launched by professional money managers (notably, hedge

funds) is a relatively modern phenomenon, it is unsurprising that the topic of shareholder activism

did not feature in the legislative history of the HSR Act. However, there was extensive debate

regarding the potential adverse consequences of premerger notification and waiting period

requirements on other more common forms of investment activity then. As a Senate Report points

out, the Senate Judiciary Committee believed the Act would “represent[] a careful balancing of the

need to detect and prevent illegal mergers and acquisitions prior to consummation without unduly

burdening business with unnecessary paperwork or delays” (S. Rep. No. 94-803 pt. 1 [1976], p.

65). These concerns resulted in amendments to the bill that shortened the waiting period for cash

tender offers and expanded exemptions to reporting requirements (S. Rep. No. 94-803 pt. 1 [1976],

pp. 65-66). Regarding Congress’s view of tender offers, the House Report acknowledged that “the

very possibility of a successful cash tender offer may exert a pro-competitive influence in the

                 2.3. Agency Interpretations of the “Investment-Only” Exception

        The HSR Act permits the Federal Trade Commission to (1) define terms in the Act, (2)

make additional exemptions to the reporting requirements for transactions which are not likely to

violate the antitrust laws, and (3) prescribe “such other rules as may be necessary and appropriate

to carry out the purposes of this section.” (15 U.S.C. § 18a[d][2]). Pursuant to this authority, the

FTC promulgated regulations for the premerger notification program in 1978 (16 C.F.R. pts. 801–

803).

        Since 1978, the FTC has interpreted the “investment-only” exemption of 15 U.S.C. §

18a[c][9] narrowly (Bruno and Schmidt 2006; Feinstein, Libby, and Lee 2015). Per HSR’s

implementing rules, the investment-only exemption applies only if the investor has “no intention

of participating in the formulation, determination, or direction of the basic business decisions of

the issuer” (16 C.F.R. pt. 801.1[i][1]). The implementing rules do not define “basic business

decisions” or otherwise elaborate on what conduct will trigger a reporting obligation. Per the

                                      2.4. Activist Investors

        Our empirical analysis focuses on the effects of the premerger notification regime on one

subset of minority shareholders: activist investors. Activists are shareholders that invest in a

company seeking financial or operational changes to improve the target company’s performance,

without attempting to acquire control of the firm (Gilson and Gordon 2013). Due to agency costs,

large investment funds with highly diversified portfolios, such as pension funds and mutual funds,

have little incentive to actively monitor management of portfolio companies (Hamdani and Hannes

2019; Gilson and Gilson 2013). Instead, certain hedge funds fill this monitoring gap (Partnoy and

Thomas 2006; Gilson and Gordon 2013). Hedge funds have important advantages over other types

of institutional shareholders that enhance their ability and incentive to actively monitor portfolio

           A robust academic literature examines hedge fund activism.13 Activists generally target

underperforming firms (Brav et al. 2008b). Prior work, including some of our own (Brav et al.

2008a; Klein and Zur 2009), demonstrates that share prices increase in a statistically significant

and economically meaningful fashion when an activist announces an engagement at a targeted firm.

Later studies, including Bebchuk, Brav, and Jiang (2015) and Brav et al. (2008b), find no evidence

that these stock price gains dissipate over time. Some (Cremers et al. 2021) attribute the returns to

skilled stock picking by activists, and others (Strine 2017; Coffee and Palia 2016; Lipton 2013)

argue that the short-term financial returns come at the cost of long-term operational soundness.

Many other studies, however, document positive operational—in addition to financial—effects of

activism. For instance, firms targeted by activists experience increased return on assets (Brav et al.

2008a; Boyson and Mooradian 2011; Clifford 2008), increased firm productivity (Brav, Jiang, and

Kim 2015), increased innovation (Brav et al. 2018), increased board diversity (Jiang 2022),

improvements in social and environmental actions (Dyck et al. 2019; Chu and Zhao 2019; Hwang

and Nili 2020), and reductions in plant emissions (Akey and Appel 2020; Naaraayanan et al. 2021;

Chu and Zhao 2019).

           One mechanism by which shareholder activists often seek to improve performance is

gaining board representation, either through a proxy fight or by negotiated settlement (Fos 2017,

Bebchuk et al. 2020; Coffee et al. 2019; Hamdani and Hannes 2019). Unlike private equity firms,

hedge fund activists normally do not seek control of the firms in which they invest (Gilson and

        Activists’ returns depend on stock price appreciation, which are shared pro rata with all

shareholders. Overhead and other costs, however, are borne by the activist alone. Yet once an

activist discloses its plans, the target’s share price can move significantly—studies have found that

the filing of a Schedule 13D by an activist investor is associated with large, positive average

abnormal returns (Brav et al. 2008a; Albuquerque, Fos, and Schroth 2022). The activist’s cost

recovery and profits therefore derive from returns on the equity position it builds before disclosure

(Gilson and Gordon 2013). If the fact that an activist is acquiring stock leaks prior to the activist

acquiring its full desired stake, the activist often must proceed on the campaign with a smaller stake

over which to spread overhead costs (Bebchuk and Jackson 2012). As such, the ability to purchase

an initial block of shares at prices below the post-disclosure level is an important incentive for

activists, as it enables them to capture a fraction of the expected benefits of their performance

improvements (Bebchuk and Jackson 2012).

        An array of federal laws mandate disclosure of blockholdings in public companies.

Existing rules under Section 13(d) of the Williams Act mandates disclosure of blockholdings above

5% within five business days of surpassing the five-percent-threshold (17 C.F.R. § 240.13d-1).

                                     3. EMPIRICAL ANALYSIS

        As detailed above, HSR imposes costs on activist investors. In this section, we test whether

these costs affect activist behavior. Exploiting the HSR notification threshold, we examine whether

HSR affects activists’ choice of firms to target, and we show that it does: activists are less likely to

to target firms if a toehold position would trigger HSR disclosure requirements. Further, we test for

whether industry concentration increases following activist intervention. We find no evidence of

that. Instead, we provide evidence that concentration appears to decrease following activist

intervention.

        Again, we are careful not to make causal claims about the effects of activism on industry

concentration. Instead, we simply note that our results appear to be inconsistent with the claim that

activism increases industry concentration. All else equal, industry concentration appears to

decrease following activist intervention, particularly in light of the general rise in market

concentration over time (Autor et al. 2017).

        Several (non-exclusive) explanations may explain these results. First, it may be the case

that the HSR requirements discourage activists from pursuing campaigns that would increase

industry concentration. Although this explanation may address why we do not observe increases in

industry concentration following activism, it does not explain why we observe statistically

significant drops in concentration following activism. Another possible explanation goes to the

underlying activist strategy. As noted above, activists targed poorly performing firms and attempt

to improve their performance. One way to rephrase this strategy is they take underperformers and

try to make them close to average. If successful, such a strategy could decrease industry

concentration as measured by HHI. Consider an industry with three firms. Firm A has 70% market

share, firm B has 25% market share, and firm C has 5% market share. This industry has an HHI of

5,550. If an activist intervenes at firm C and increases its market share to 10% by taking 5% of firm

A’s market share, then industry HHI falls to 4,950. And a third (related) answer is that activist

intervention at one firm causes other firms in the industry to improve their own performance and

compete more aggressively with each other, as shown by prior scholarship (Aslan and Kumar 2016;

Gantchev, Gredil, and Jotikasthira 2019).

                                          4. IMPLICATIONS

        As demonstrated above, the HSR premerger notification requirements affect the behavior

of activist investors: activists are less likely to target firms if a toehold position would trigger HSR

disclosure requirements. This result carries important implications for those who care about
35                                                                                      Journal Name



corporate oversight, given HSR’s deterrent effects on activists, particularly at companies that are

near HSR thresholds.

        Given that antitrust regulation imposes costs on investor activism, it is interesting that we

do not find evidence that activism overall is accompanied by a rise in industry concentration, and

instead appears to be followed by a decrease in concentration. Of course, the overall result is simply

a statement of averages, and certain types of activist engagements might implicate antitrust issues.

Activists sometimes take stakes in public companies in order to influence merger and acquisition

activity. One goal of activists may be the sale of all or part of the target firm (see Swanson, Young,

and Yust 2022; Boyson, Gantchev, and Shivdasani 2017). Activists may also intervene for the

purpose of curbing merger and acquisition activity by the target firm (see Jiang, Li, and Mei [2019],

showing activists reduced value-destroying mergers and acquisitions activity; Gantchev, Sevilir,

and Shivdasani [2020], same). But it is important to note that, in the cases where activists attempt

to cause or intervene in a potential merger, the FTC and DOJ already may weigh in on the merger

itself. It is not clear that flagging the potential merger a second time, so to speak, through the

activist’s HSR filings provides the FTC or DOJ with additional information or leverage about the

merger itself and its potential effects on industry concentration.

        Moreover, as certain FTC Commissioners have pointed out, aggressive HSR enforcement

against activists may chill shareholder advocacy, while expending the agencies’ resources on

investigating transactions that are unlikely to raise substantive antitrust concerns (see, for example,

Ohlhausen and Wright 2015). HSR enforcement targeting activists has the added cost of policing

the boardroom dynamics of individual companies, as the Agencies have based their determinations

of whether an activist’s engagement meets the “investment-only” exemption by looking into the

activist’s communications with management (see, for example, FTC [2024b], describing HSR

enforcement action against activist investor who communicated with management of issuer to
Antitrust, Anti-Activism                                                                           36


advocate for a board seat). Accordingly, our results provide support for the continued discussions

surrounding potential changes to the HSR premerger notification rules, including its threshold and

investment-only exception.

        Beyond the antitrust implications, our findings contribute to two related debates in finance

and securities regulation. First, they speak directly to the longstanding question of whether

“toeholds matter” in both takeover markets and activist campaigns. In the takeover literature, earlier

work demonstrates that bidders with pre-bid toeholds face different incentives in tender offers and

mergers, with implications for bid premia, competition, and deal outcomes (see Betton and Eckbo

2000; Betton, Eckbo, and Thorburn 2009). The ability toeholds provide to reduce acquisition costs,

signal commitment, or influence contest dynamics has long been central to understanding the

market for corporate control. However, whether toeholds bind is not clear (see Goldman and Qian,

2005). In parallel, in the activism literature, a large body of work (e.g., Brav et al. 2008a, 2008b;

Klein and Zur 2009; Bebchuk et al. 2013) shows that the ability of activists to accumulate

meaningful stakes prior to disclosure on Schedule 13D is critical both for financing costly

campaigns and for capturing a share of the value created. Our results add a novel dimension by

showing that the antitrust-driven HSR premerger notification regime can bind even before the

traditional 5% 13D threshold, thereby constraining activists’ ability to establish effective toeholds

in a manner analogous to how disclosure or ownership rules constrain bidders in takeovers.

        More broadly, our analysis is particularly relevant given the SEC’s recent rule change

shortening the 13D disclosure window from ten calendar days to five business days (SEC 2023). A

shorter disclosure window compresses the time during which activists can accumulate shares

quietly, limiting feasible toeholds above 5%. Taken together, the dual effects of HSR thresholds

and the accelerated 13D timeline highlight how overlapping regulatory regimes jointly curtail the

economics of activism. These findings suggest that policies outside the conventional corporate
37                                                                                 Journal Name



governance domain, including antitrust and securities disclosure regulation, can materially shape

activists’ incentives and strategies, with implications for both capital markets and firm-level

oversight.
