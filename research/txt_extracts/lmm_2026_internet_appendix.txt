                      II.     Microfoundations of the Model
In this section, we provide a microfoundation of the preferences in our model via four applications:
(i) heterogeneous beliefs about the value of the proposal in Section A; (ii) heterogeneous investor
time horizons and a choice between a short-term and long-term investment strategy in Section
B; (iii) extraction of private benefits of control by the blockholder in Section C; and (iv) costly
monitoring by the blockholder in Section D.

A.   Microfoundation: Heterogeneous Beliefs
In the baseline model, shareholders’ biases b and β are interpreted as capturing heterogeneous
preferences. Alternatively, the model can be recast in terms of heterogeneous prior beliefs. To see
this, suppose that the state θ is given by θ = θ1 + θ2 , where θ1 and θ2 are independent of each
other. Suppose that shareholders do not receive any information about θ1 , but differ in their prior
beliefs about it: shareholder b believes that E [θ1 ] = b. In addition, after shareholders trade and
before they vote, they all observe a public signal s about θ2 such that E [θ2 |s] = q. Finally, suppose
that each shareholder’s valuation of the share is
                                    v (d, θ , b) = v0 + (θ1 + θ2 ) d,

as in the baseline model. It follows that each shareholder would like the proposal to be approved if
and only if q > −b, and that his valuation of the shares at the time of trading is given by equation
(6), exactly as in the baseline model. Therefore, all the analysis and results of the paper apply to
this application.
    Note that in models with differences of opinion, such as those with heterogeneous priors out-
lined above, shareholders do not update their beliefs even though they are fully aware of the fact
that other shareholders do not share their views. For example, shareholders who buy (sell) because
of a very high (low) sentiment b will initiate large trades and will not update their priors from
knowing that others do not share their extreme views.
    We could model differences in beliefs differently and assume that shareholders all observe the
same public signal θ1 , but now before the trading stage, whereas they observe signal s about θ2
after the trading stage (unchanged from above) . Furthermore, now assume they have the same
priors, but have different models according to which they interpret signal θ1 , as in Kandel and
Pearson (1995) and Boot, Gopalan, and Thakor (2006), such that the posterior expectations differ
across shareholders. If we then set the expectation of shareholder b, Eb [θ1 ] = b (slight abuse of
notation here), we again obtain a mapping into our framework. Hence, our model is sufficiently
flexible and robust to accommodate alternative strategies to model beliefs.

B.      Microfoundation: Heterogeneous Time Horizons
In this section, we show how our model can describe the heterogeneity in shareholders’ preferences
coming from their different time horizons. In doing so, we build on the literature on short-termism
– a widely-debated issue (e.g., Harford, Kecskés, and Mansi (2018) and Giannetti and Yu (2020)).
     Suppose shareholders are expected to vote on a proposal to pay out some cash flows in the
short-term, but potentially at the expense of long-term value. For example, the firm could be
going through a proxy contest organized by an activist investor, and the proposal to be voted on is
whether to approve the activist’s board nominees, who are likely to push for the implementation of
short-term projects, for example, selling part of the assets.4 The incumbent manager is suggesting
a long-term project, which, if successful, pays off in the long run, at time tL . The activist is
suggesting a more short-term project, which always pays out 1 per share in the short-run (at time
tS < tL , for example, because part of the assets are sold), but only pays off in the long-run (at time
tL ) if it is successful. If the activist’s slate is approved (d = 1), the more short-term project is
chosen, and if the incumbent management wins the proxy contest (d = 0), the long-term project is
chosen.
     The success of the activist’s vis-a-vis the manager’s proposed project depends on the unknown
state θ ∈ {0, 1}. If the state is good (θ = 1) the project proposed by the activist is successful: it not
only pays 1 per share at time tS but also pays 1 − w at time tL , and the long-term project is unsuc-
cessful and pays 0 in both periods. If the state is bad (θ = 0), the manager’s project is successful:
it pays 0 at time tS and w per share at time tL , whereas the activist’s project is unsuccessful and
only pays 1 per share at time tS , but pays 0 at time tL . Parameter w thus captures the quality of the
strategy proposed by the incumbent management relative to the activist’s proposed strategy.
     All shareholders have a higher value from the activist’s project if the state θ is more likely to
be good; this captures their common value from the decision. However, shareholders have differ-
ent horizons (e.g., Bushee (1998) and Gaspar, Massa, and Matos (2005)) and hence put different
weights on the tS vs. tL cash flows. In particular, the utility of a shareholder with bias b from cash
flows CS at time tS and CL at time tL is

where the cross section of shareholders’ biases b is given by a differentiable cdf G. Hence, share-
holders with a higher b have a shorter horizon, in that they value the short-term cash flows more.

to derive the conditions under which the proposal is approved, we characterize the identity of the
median voter, who is defined as the shareholder whose individual vote always coincides with the
collective decision on the proposal. To characterize the median voter, we first analyze the votes of
small shareholders. Denote by s (q; y, q∗e ) the number of votes cast by small shareholders in favor
of the proposal if signal q is realized, the blockholder traded y shares, and the expected decision
rule is q∗e . Then,

if s (q; y, q∗e ) ≥ τ, which is equivalent to q ≥ s−1 (τ; y, q∗e ), then the value of the proposal is expected
to be sufficiently high, so there is enough support among small shareholders to approve the pro-

equivalent to q ≤ s−1 (τ − α − y; y, q∗e ), then there is enough opposition among small shareholders

proposal whenever he is pivotal, β ≥ w − s−1 (τ − α − y; y, q∗e ), then the small shareholder with

pivotal, then the median voter is the small shareholder with bias w − s−1 (τ; y, q∗e ). In the remaining
cases, the blockholder is the median voter, that is, bMV (β , y, q∗e ) = β .

        III.     Supplementary Analysis for the Baseline Model
This section presents several additional results and proofs that are either used in the proofs of the
baseline model or are mentioned in some of the discussions of the baseline model.

A.   Exit and a Positive Voting Premium
In the context of our baseline model, we show that, although the blockholder can gain influence
over the voting outcome by buying additional shares and moving the median voter closer to his bias
β , he may nevertheless choose to do the opposite: sell shares to small shareholders and thereby
give up his influence over the voting outcome, while demanding a positive premium from the
small shareholders. Thus, the tension between exit and voice (e.g., Hirschman (1970)) also exists

in our model, which shows that the incentives to exit can prevail even when the voting premium
is positive. Hence, a positive voting premium does not necessarily indicate a more concentrated
ownership structure.

D.      Visual Illustration of the Median Voter
Figure IA.1 plots the median voter (vertical axis) as a function of the blockholder’s trade y (horizon-
tal axis). The figure shows that function βH (y) (βL (y)) is upward (downward) sloping, starting at
β ∗ for y = −α and reaching a maximum of b (minimum of −b) as y approaches τ − α (1 − α − τ);
these properties are shown in Lemma IA.1.
    The left panel of the figure considers the case in which the blockholder’s bias satisfies β > β ∗ ,
where β ∗ is defined by (A10). For any trade y < yH ≡ βH−1 (β ), the median voter is a small
shareholder whose bias is strictly increasing in y, whereas for any y ≥ yH , the median voter is the
blockholder himself. Hence, the median voter is given by min {β , βH (y)}, shown as the bold line in
the figure. It follows that by choosing the appropriate trade in the interval [−α, yH ], the blockholder
can change the identity of the median voter to be any point in the interval [β ∗ , β ]. In particular,
by buying more (or selling fewer) shares, the blockholder can push the bias of the median voter
closer to β . However, the blockholder cannot choose a median voter outside of the interval [β ∗ , β ].
Intuitively, the median voter cannot be larger than β because the blockholder’s optimal voting
strategy (to support the proposal whenever q > −β ) prevails when his stake becomes sufficiently
large. Likewise, the median voter cannot be lower than β ∗ because the collective preferences of
small shareholders prevail when the blockholder exits his position.
    The right panel of the figure considers the case in which the blockholder’s bias satisfies β < β ∗ ,
which is a mirror image of the left panel. In particular, the blockholder can influence the identity
of the median voter to be any point in the interval [β , β ∗ ] by choosing the appropriate trade in the
interval [−α, yL ], where yL ≡ βL−1 (β ).

                                         IV.      Extensions
In this section, we discuss several extensions of the baseline model. Section A considers the ex-
tension to a dual-class share structure. Section B describes three versions of a dynamic trading
model with two rounds of trade. Section C analyzes a separate market for votes. Section D con-
siders the case of multiple blockholders. Section E studies the role of proxy advisors. Section F
introduces index funds, who vote but not trade. Section G considers an extension where the block-
holder does not know the distribution of small shareholders’ biases. Section H examines the setting
where shareholders affect corporate decisions not directly by voting, but indirectly, by influencing
management. Section I discusses selective vote participation. The proofs are in Section J.

A.    Dual-Class Shares

B.      Dynamic Trading
In this section, we extend the baseline model to study dynamics. In particular, we introduce a
second round of trade, which takes place after the record date, whereas the first round of trade
occurs before the record date, as in the baseline model. We show that the economic forces behind
the voting premium in the baseline model are relevant in a dynamic setting as well, and we also
highlight several new forces that arise.
    In particular, we consider three different versions of this extension. In Section B.1, the second
round of trade occurs right after the record date and before the public signal is revealed, and
we show that the difference between the share prices around the record date captures the voting
premium from the baseline model. In Section B.2, the second round of trade occurs after the public
signal is revealed, which allows us to explore the implications of public information revelation
(e.g., proxy advisors’ recommendations) on trading volume and price patterns. In Section B.3, the
second round of trade occurs right after the vote. This extension allows us to relate to empirical
evidence on post-vote trading volume and to study how concerns about post-vote trading profits
distort shareholders’ voting incentives.
    For all three versions, we introduce the following notations. Let xt (b) and yt denote the trades
of small shareholders and the blockholder in round t ∈ {1, 2}, and let π2 (b) and Π2 be the second-
round trading profits for small shareholders and the blockholder, respectively. Denote by pt the
market clearing share price in round t. We assume that the second round of trade features the same
frictions as the first round of trade, namely, the same trading costs.5 As in the baseline model,
we assume that γ is large enough such that small shareholders and the blockholder maintain a
long position in the firm after both rounds of trade. Since, in all three versions of the extension,
the second round of trade is scheduled post-record date, these additional trades do not reallocate
voting rights between investors.

C.    Vote Trading

Passive Shareholders and Trading Costs. To interpret (IA.100), abstract for now from the impact

      market declines and trading becomes harder for the blockholder. In equation (IA.100), the
      parameter for trading costs, γ, is replaced by ϕγ , that is, a lower ϕ (more passive sharehold-

      vestors unequivocally increases the blockholder’s trading costs and reduces the size of the
      blockholder’s trades.

   2. The active small shareholders may be more or less conservative than the passive small share-
      holders, and the implications of this difference depend on whether it increases (decreases) or

      decreases (increases) the price at which small shareholders are willing to sell to (buy from)

      blockholder buys from small shareholders for cash-flow reasons, but smaller amounts (hold-

      actively trading small shareholders have a higher reservation price compared to the passive

Direct and Indirect Effects on the Shareholder Base. Equation (IA.96) above suggests that the
blockholder’s trades can make the post-trade distribution more conservative. This can happen in
the following two scenarios:

   1. E p [b] < Ea [b] < β and K (b′ ) > M (b′ ) for all b′ . Then the actively trading shareholders have
      a stronger preference for the proposal than the passive investors, and the blockholder buys
      from the actively trading small shareholders (y > 0). Hence, the blockholder’s purchases
      increase the weight of the more conservative passive shareholders and thus make the small
      shareholder base more conservative.

   2. β < Ea [b] < E p [b] and K (b′ ) < M (b′ ) for all b′ . Then the actively trading shareholders are
      more conservative and buy shares from the blockholder (y < 0), thereby making the small
      shareholder base more conservative.

    In the baseline model in which all small shareholders trade actively, the direct effect of the
blockholder’s trades (he increases his own weight when he buys and decreases it when he sells) and
the indirect effect (he buys more from those small shareholders least aligned with his preferences
when he buys and sells to them in the opposite case) always have the same sign. Hence, the indirect
effect on the small shareholder base amplifies the direct effect. By contrast, in the presence of
passive investors and a conflict of interest between actively trading and passive shareholders, the
indirect effect and the direct effect may have opposite signs: If the blockholder is more activist
than both (case 1 above), then he may increase his own weight, but make the small shareholder
base more conservative. Note, however, that the direct effect is always stronger than the indirect
effect, since on average, the blockholder buys from small shareholders who are less activist than
he is.

G.    Uncertainty about Biases

the case in which the distribution of b depends on an unknown state. Under weak assumptions, the
equilibrium is fully-revealing and small shareholders learn the unknown state from the stock price

H.    Influencing Management
Blockholders may be able to exert influence on the firm’s decisions if decisions are made by man-
agement rather than in shareholder meetings, and the blockholder can influence management (e.g.,
McCahery, Sautner, and Starks (2016)). Hence, we analyze a version of the model in which deci-
sions are taken by management and management puts some weight on the welfare of its current
shareholders, represented by the preferences of the average post-trade shareholder. Then the block-

the shareholder base. We show that this ability to influence decisions may give rise to a premium
on the share price that shares many properties of the voting premium in the baseline model.

by the company’s management, which observes signal q before making its decision. All other
aspects of the baseline model remain unchanged. We assume that the management trades-off its

the blockholder’s stake, leading to a negative influence premium.

difference between them is the decision rule (i.e., the cutoff q∗ on the public signal above which the
proposal is accepted): the voting-based cutoff q∗ (y) is now replaced by the management’s cutoff
q∗m (y), which depends on the bias of the average post-trade shareholder, b post trade (y, q∗m ). Intu-
itively, while in the baseline model the blockholder’s trades affect the decision on the proposal by
changing the identity of the median voter, −q∗ (y), here this occurs through changes in the identity
of the average shareholder. Since the median voter and the average shareholder are different from
each other, there is a wedge between the voting premium and the influence premium. In particu-
lar, if decisions are made by voting, then once the blockholder buys sufficiently many shares, he
becomes the median voter and cannot further influence the median voter’s identity (Proposition 1),
so the voting premium is zero. By contrast, if decisions are made by the management, then as long
as β ̸= E [b], and even if the blockholder’s stake is quite large, his incremental buying pushes the
average shareholder bias closer to β , and as such, further influences the management’s decision.
As a result, generically, the influence premium is different from zero.

(see equation (26)) arise in two very different settings: In the baseline setting, shareholders vote
and managers have no influence, whereas in the second setting, managers make decisions and
their preferences have an impact on the final decision. Yet, both settings share several generic
properties, which give rise to a premium - IP or V P - in the share price: (1) the blockholder changes
the composition of the shareholder base, directly and indirectly; (2) the equilibrium decision rule
can be represented as the choice of a specific shareholder, the median voter q∗ (equation (16)) in
the baseline model, representative shareholder q∗m (equation (IA.108)) in this extension; (3) the
blockholder’s trades can move the representative shareholder and this is valuable to him.

I.      Vote Participation
In this section, we demonstrate how selective vote participation can be captured in our analysis. We
assume that a small shareholder with bias b participates in the vote with an exogenous probability
ρ (b) ∈ [0, 1], where ρ ′ (b) > 0 if and only if b > 0. That is, shareholders with larger |b| are
