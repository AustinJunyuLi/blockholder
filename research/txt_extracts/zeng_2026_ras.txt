before public disclosure?

Abstract
I investigate whether managers learn about who is buying and selling their firm’s
stock before public disclosure. I examine insider trading prior to the disclosure of
Schedule 13Ds and 13Gs, which are required when activist or passive investors
acquire more than 5% ownership, respectively. These disclosures trigger a significant
price jump of 6.1% for 13Ds and 1.7% for 13Gs. I document elevated insider net
purchases before the disclosures, peaking on the day blockholders cross the 5%
threshold. Price and volume signals cannot fully explain the pattern. Cross-sectional
analyses reveal that pre-disclosure insider net purchases are higher when investors
report pre-filing communication with the management, when investors and target
firms are geographically proximate, or when companies have invested in investor
relations. Collectively, my results reveal that managers learn about their firms’
ownership changes by interpreting market signals and communicating with investors,
and they subsequently trade on this information before public disclosure.

Keywords Managerial learning · Insider trading · Blockholder formation · Schedule
13D · Schedule 13G · Investor relations

JEL classification M41 · G1 · G14

to track ownership changes. A 2007 joint survey by CFO.com and the National

surveillance firms aggregate fragmented trading data from legitimate sources,
supplementing it with market analysis, regulatory filings, and proprietary databases

direct engagement with investors also plays a crucial role. A 2015 National Investor
Relations Institute survey found that 49% of investor relations officers first learn about

1 Panel D). This evidence indicates that public companies and managers do not
passively receive ownership information but instead seek to learn about ownership
changes by integrating stock surveillance with direct investor engagement.

   My results are consistent with the hypothesis that managers learn about
blockholder purchases before public disclosure and adjust their personal trades in
response to this information. Univariate analyses indicate that, compared to control
period observations, insiders at target firms sell approximately 25% fewer shares
between days −20 and −6 before the trigger date, and they purchase over 68% more
shares starting from five days before the trigger date until the filing date. Notably,
insider purchases peak precisely on the trigger day on which blockholders cross
the 5% threshold. The coincidence is intriguing, given that the timing of the trigger
date is determined by blockholders and remains private until the filing date. This
indicates that managers monitor blockholders’ trading signals and have inferred that
a significant position is being built. In contrast, I do not find significant differences
in other time windows immediately prior to the filing date. In the multivariate
regression analyses, I control for contemporary return, volume, momentum, and
other common determinants of insider trading. The coefficient on contemporary
stock market return is positive and significant, indicating that insiders respond to
information embedded in stock prices. However, the abnormal insider purchases
persist even with these controls, suggesting that managers can decode investors’
trading signals using cues that are not fully captured by prices and volumes. The
baseline results are stable across firm size, persist over time, and are robust to
multiple alternative specifications.
   I attribute my findings to insiders’ learning of information about investors’ trading.
However, there are two potential alternative explanations: (1) reverse causality, in
which 13D filers base their trades on insiders’ abnormal trades, or (2) joint causality,
in which both 13D filers and insiders trade independently based on shared private
information of target firms’ undervaluation. The reverse causality explanation predicts
that insiders trade before 13D filers, as the 13D filers should have observed abnormal
insider trading when conducting their trades. The joint causality explanation suggests
that insiders trade evenly throughout the period when 13D filers accumulate their
shares because the firm’s state of being undervalued is unlikely to change over a short
period. However, my findings show that insider purchases rise sharply five trading

    Next, I conduct additional tests to examine the mechanisms through which
managers learn about ownership changes. Anecdotal evidence suggests that
insiders acquire this information primarily through investor relations efforts,
integrating stock surveillance with direct investor engagement. Note that managers
are better positioned than other market participants to interpret market signals
due to their regular communication with investors. For instance, managers may
better identify an investor’s trades if that investor has expressed interest in the
firm. To capture pre-filing direct communication, I use two proxies: (1) voluntary
disclosures by 21% of 13D filers who report direct interactions with management,
even though these likely understate actual communication, and (2) geographic
proximity between investors and target firm headquarters, as research suggests that
closer distances facilitate the exchange of soft information (Costello 2013; Ahern
2017; Chu et al. 2019; He et al. 2022). Cross-sectional analyses show that pre-
disclosure insider net purchases are significantly higher when blockholders disclose
pre-filing communications with management or are closer to the target firm. These
results reinforce anecdotal evidence that direct interactions help managers decode
investor trading signals and extract ownership change information. While I cannot
directly observe firms’ use of stock-surveillance services, I proxy for firms’ investor
relations investment by counting the number of investor conferences attended by
the company’s management in the year before a 13D disclosure. Because attending
investor conferences involves significant financial and managerial resources, firms
that participate more actively in these events likely dedicate greater attention to
investor relations and are more inclined to hire shareholder-surveillance services.
I find that insider net purchases are more pronounced for firms that invest more in
investor relations, which further supports the notion that investor engagement helps
managers detect ownership changes.
    I also extend the analyses to the context of 13,882 13Gs. Unlike 13Ds, which
are filed by activist investors, 13Gs are filed by passive investors who cross the 5%
threshold without intending to influence management. In some cases managers
may be more attentive to activists due to potential concerns about control rights
(Chabakauri et al. 2022), but it is less clear whether they would monitor passive
blockholders with similar intensity. Nevertheless, I find a similar pattern: i​nsider

and respond to information about blockholder accumulations, regardless of investor
intent. This demonstrates a broader pattern of learning from outside investors
beyond activism contexts.
    To further assess the generalizability of managerial learning, I examine whether
insider trading responses to blockholder accumulations vary across insider roles
and investor types. I first compare executives and independent directors and find
that both groups increase their net purchases prior to blockholder disclosures. This
suggests that learning about ownership changes is not limited to senior management

    My study contributes to the managerial learning literature (e.g., Chen et al. 2007;
Luo 2005; Ye et al. 2023; Zhang 2023; Zuo 2016). Prior work largely centers on
managerial learning from prices (Goldstein 2023). However, this literature faces two
challenges: it is difficult to rule out confounding variables correlated with both prices
and managerial actions, and it remains unclear how managers extract insights from
noisy, aggregated price signals. As Goldstein (2023) and Gelsomin and Hutton (2023)
note, a key direction for the literature is to provide more direct evidence about what
managers actually learn. My study addresses this gap by providing credible evidence
that managers learn about block purchases preceding public disclosure. Importantly,
this learning cannot be fully explained by price and volume movements. I find that
the effect is stronger at firms with active investor relations, suggesting that direct
engagement with investors helps managers decode blockholders’ trading signals.
These findings highlight a complementary mechanism of managerial learning, in
which direct interactions with investors and market signals jointly help managers
acquire information from outsiders. My study adds nuance to the learning-from-price
literature and highlights the importance of investor engagement as a complementary
means by which managers acquire information from investors.
    My study also adds to the insider trading literature, which emphasizes insiders
exploiting private firm-specific information for trading gains (e.g., Arif et al. 2022;
Chawla et al. 2023; Cheng and Lo 2006; Dechow et al. 2016; Jagolinzer 2009;
Ke et al. 2003; Larcker et al. 2021). From an informational perspective, this line
of literature views insider trades as signals that convey firm-specific information to
the markets, emphasizing the unidirectional flow of information from insiders to
markets (Chen et al. 2020; Manne 1966; Meulbroek 1992). In contrast, my study
provides novel evidence that insiders also trade based on advance knowledge of
ownership changes initiated by outside blockholders. This setting is novel in that the
relevant information originates externally, highlighting a reverse flow of information
from investors to managers. Additionally, prior literature documents that insiders are
contrarian investors who trade against current investor sentiment (Lakonishok and
Lee 2001; Piotroski and Roulstone 2005; Rozeff and Zaman 1998; Seyhun 1992;
Sias and Whidbee 2010). My study provides one of the first evidence that insiders
can and do follow blockholders’ trades.
    My study relates to but differs from two concurrent papers, Chabakauri et al.
(2022) and Duong et al. (2025). Chabakauri et al. (2022) show that corporate
insiders detect activist trades ahead of the public and retain ownership to defend
control rights. Duong et al. (2025) focus on a smaller set of hedge fund activists and
document that insiders trade ahead of the public to make personal gains. While the
three studies are complementary, they differ in focus, mechanism, and implications.

First, mine asks a different question: whether managers learn about their firm’s

and 13Gs (passive investors) and find elevated insider net purchases before both
13D and 13G filings. These findings suggest that managers acquire ownership

extract information about ownership changes. This focus on what managers learn
(ownership changes) and how they learn it from outside investors (via interpreting
market signals and investor engagement) forms the conceptual foundation that
distinguishes my study and advances the understanding of managerial learning.


2 Background and setting

2.1 Monitoring investor base

Firms monitor their shareholder base to stay current on who holds their shares and
how positions are changing, using this information to guide investor engagement.
This monitoring helps IR officers interpret market reactions to corporate disclosures
and strategic decisions. When monitoring reveals that a key shareholder is building or
trimming a position, managers may follow up to understand the investor’s rationale.
Furthermore, monitoring helps firms anticipate voting outcomes and detect potential
activism or control contests early.
   Anecdotal evidence suggests that managers monitor trading in their firm’s stock.
A 2007 CFO magazine/National Investor Relations Institute survey indicates that
as many as 94% of public firms monitor changes in their investor base and that 57%
subscribe to external stock-surveillance services (Appendix 1 Panels A, B, C).7 In
2015, National Investor Relations Institute surveyed investor relation officers to
discern how they first learned of activist investor involvement. The survey revealed
that only 20% of them obtained this information through public SEC filings, while
80% learned from nonpublic sources. Among the latter group, 49% were directly
informed by the activist investors, and 20% received the information from stock
surveillance firms (Appendix 1 Panel D). While direct contact with investors may
help identify who is trading, stock-surveillance firms can help detect the intensity
and timing of transactions. In practice, managers likely use both sources together to
identify when and how much investors are trading in the company’s stock.

2.1.1 Stock‑surveillance firms

The stock-surveillance industry arose during the corporate takeover battles of the
1980s. Proxy-solicitation firms were hired to identify shareholders and appeal to
them for their votes. In the early 1990 s, some of those firms began to add stock
monitoring services and marketed them to public companies that wanted to ascertain
their shareholders’ identities. The stock-surveillance business has since grown, partly
fueled by the upsurge in shareholder activism and companies’ demand to track the
effectiveness of their investor relations efforts. Notable providers have included Ipreo,
CapitalBridge, Q4 Web Systems, and Ilios Partners.
    Surveillance firms obtain bits of trading information from legitimate sources,
which they supplement and synthesize through market analysis and data analytics
to identify investors. They draw heavily from the Depository Trust and Clearing
Corp.’s daily report of where shares are held in custody by which custodian banks.
Public firms can access the daily reports about their own stocks and may turn them
over to third parties, like stock-surveillance firms, who in turn rely on this relay
to determine shareholders’ identities.9The Depository Trust and Clearing Corp.’s
report serves as a road map for stock-surveillance firms, and they combine the
listings with several other sources: a proprietary database containing information
regarding which custodian banks the major investors use, regulatory filings, and
data regarding major investors’ investment strategies. In most instances, the bits of
information they obtain and collate are sufficient to determine investors’ identities.

2.2 The setting of 13Ds and 13Gs

The current rules governing investor disclosure of ownership and holdings consist of
five overlapping parts: 13D for large (above 5%) active shareholders, 13G for large
(above 5%) passive shareholders, 13 F for quarterly disclosure of holdings required
for institutional investment managers, N-CSR and N-Q for quarterly or semiannual
disclosure of holdings required for mutual funds, and Section 16 for ownership
by insiders. While these public filings offer only partial snapshots of public firms’
ownership structures, studies document their impacts on the stock market (Agarwal
et al. 2013, 2015; Brav et al. 2008; Brown and Schwarz 2013).
   I use the setting of 13Ds and 13Gs to examine my research question for the
following reasons. First, it is well documented that blockholders are informed
investors and the disclosure of blockholder formation on either a 13D or 13G has
a high price impact (Brav et al. 2008; Collin-Dufresne and Fos 2015; Greenwood
and Schor 2009; Albuquerque et al. 2022). In contrast, institutional investors report
their entire portfolio holdings on Form 13 F, N-CSR, or N-Q, and it is empirically
challenging to identify which trades reported on these forms are informed trades.
Second, blockholders must disclose their holdings within 10 days after crossing
the 5% threshold, while Form 13Fs need only be filed within 45 calendar days of
the quarter-end dates. Theoretically, 13 F filers may have made trades at any point
during the quarter; therefore I cannot identify a narrow insider trading window.
Even if I find abnormal insider trading during the quarter, it is difficult to link it to
13 F filers’ trades. Additionally, activist blockholders must disclose their daily stock
transactions within the 60-day window before they cross the 5% threshold, and their
trading information is essential to identify the timeline and address reverse causality
concerns in the later analyses.

    An important question is whether the positive returns reflect merely the

firms’ governance and value through either direct influence in firms’ operations
(i.e., voice), as in the case of activist blockholders, or by selling their shares if the
manager underperforms (i.e., exit), as in the case of passive blockholders. Several
studies support that abnormal returns surpass stock-picking of undervalued firms
and reveal the market’s expectation of value added by blockholders (Albuquerque
et al. 2022; Edmans et al. 2013). Furthermore, the information blockholders trade
on seems long-lived: Brav et al. (2008) document that the median duration from an
initial 13D filing to the filer’s exit is 369 days.


3 Main hypothesis

The anecdotal evidence discussed in Section 2.1 suggests that managers acquire
information about who is buying and selling their companies’ shares. Given this,
I hypothesize that managers are aware of blockholders’ accumulation of their
companies’ stocks before public disclosure, and I propose that managers trade on
this information.
    Managers may have several potentially overlapping motives to trade before the
filing of 13Ds and 13Gs. First, they may revise their expectations about the firm’s
future fundamentals based on perceived value added by blockholders. Theoretical
and empirical research (e.g., Edmans and Manso 2011; Edmans and Holderness
2017) suggests that blockholders can enhance corporate governance and firm value.
Consequently, insiders may trade based on updated beliefs about firms’ future
performance.11 Second, given the substantial price appreciation following these
filings—6.1% for 13Ds and 1.7% for 13Gs—managers, upon learning of blockholder
accumulation, may be incentivized to trade in anticipation of these price increases.

4 Data and sample construction

I compile data from several sources. I collect all 13Ds and 13Gs filed between 2002
and 2022 from the SEC’s EDGAR database. I obtain insider trading data from the
Thomson Reuters Insider database, stock returns from the Center for Research in
Security Prices (CRSP), and financial items from Compustat Quarterly database. To
classify blockholders as either hedge funds or nonhedge funds, I use the list of hedge
fund activists provided by Professor Wei Jiang, the Hedge Fund Research (HFR)
database, 13 F filings from EDGAR, and a general Internet searching.

4.1 The sample of 13Ds

4.3 Data on insider trading

Section 16a of the 1934 Securities and Exchange Act requires insiders to report trans-
actions on Form 4 within two business days. I draw data on insider trades from the
Thomson Reuters Insider database. I define insiders as officers and directors, and I
restrict the sample to open-market purchases and sales. I aggregate purchases and
sales by insiders on the same trading day to obtain a daily total. Insider purchases
and sales are measured as the daily fraction of shares outstanding bought and sold by
insiders presented in basis points. Because these measures can have extreme outliers,
I winsorize nonzero values of daily insider purchases and sales at the 1% level.
   The literature indicates that insiders’ trades can be driven by liquidity needs or
diversification, rather than solely by information (Ahern 2020; Cohen et al. 2012).
To address this, I adopt the methodology of Lie (2005), Dechow et al. (2016), and
Even-Tov and Ozel (2021) to control for the expected level of insider purchases and
sales. I take all trading-day observations for the same firm between seven and three
months prior to the 13D filing date and between three and seven months afterward.
I refer to these as “control period observations.” The assumption is that the level
of insider trades driven by liquidity needs or diversification is relatively stable over
time. Unexpected pre-disclosure insider purchases (sales) are thus calculated as the
average daily insider purchases (sales) during the window surrounding the filing of
13Ds and 13Gs minus the average daily insider purchases (sales) during the control
period observations.

5.2 Unexpected insider trading prior to 13d disclosures

5.2.1 Univariate analyses

To test my hypothesis, I first analyze 13D filings and then extend the analysis to
13Gs. Figures 2 and 3 visualize the pattern of insider trading prior to the disclosure
of 13Ds. Panel A of Fig. 2 plots the daily insider purchases, relative to the mean
level during the control period, spanning 20 days before the trigger date to 20 days
after the filing date. The figure reveals that insider purchases begin deviating from
the baseline level a few days before the trigger date, peak precisely on the trigger
date itself, and remain elevated throughout the filing date. By the filing date, insiders
have cumulatively purchased 45% more shares than during the control period (Panel
A of Fig. 3). The peak on the trigger date is likely attributable to two factors: (i)
blockholders making large purchases to cross the 5% ownership threshold, thus
sending strong signals, and (ii) insiders learning from these signals and responding
more strongly to the milestone of blockholder formation. Panel B of Fig. 2 plots the
daily insider sales, while Panel B of Fig. 3 shows the cumulative insider sales. It
indicates that cumulative insider sales are 8% less than baseline levels in the window
leading up to the filing date.
   Internet Appendix Table IA.1 complements these visual patterns by quantifying the
magnitude, timing, and statistical significance of deviations from baseline. Notably,
insiders make significant unexpected purchases of 0.040 basis points of shares
outstanding per day from five days to one day before the trigger date and 0.091 basis
1320                                                                               J. J. Zeng


points per day from the trigger date to one day before the filing date—representing
increases of 67.8% and 154.5% relative to baseline, respectively. On the sales side,
insiders significantly reduce selling between 20 and six days before the trigger date by
0.034 basis points per day (a 25.4% decline), with sales otherwise remaining close to
baseline levels in the surrounding windows.
    Overall, the observed trading pattern is consistent with a gradual learning
process in which managers progressively accumulate and interpret blockholders’
trading signals. In the earlier stages, managers reduce their selling as they begin
to detect signs of emerging blockholder interest, and they respond more strongly
as blockholder formation approaches, peaking when blockholders make sizable
purchases to cross the 5% threshold. The decline in sales and rise in purchases at
different intervals could be explained by the cost differential between increasing
purchases and reducing sales. Because purchasing requires additional capital
and effort, insiders may first avoid selling and increase sales only when the 13D
filer approaches the 5% threshold. I acknowledge that trading may also reflect
strategic motives, as insiders seek to benefit from anticipated price movements.
These learning and strategic motives are not mutually exclusive and likely work in
tandem: managers respond cautiously when signals are weak and shift toward active
purchasing as confidence in blockholder formation strengthens.

5.2.3 Addressing alternative explanations

I attribute my results to insiders’ trading based on knowledge of investors’ purchases
and sales. However, two potential alternative explanations present themselves: (1)
reverse causality, in which blockholders base their trades on insiders’ abnormal
trades, or (2) joint causality, in which both blockholders and insiders trade
independently based on private information of target firms’ undervaluation. Under
the reverse causality explanation, insiders would trade before blockholders, since
blockholders should have observed abnormal insider trading when they conducted
their trades. Under the joint causality explanation, insiders would trade evenly across
the period when blockholders are accumulating their shares. This is because the
firm’s state of being undervalued is unlikely to change over a short period. However,
my findings reveal that insiders start to purchase more at trading day −5 prior to
the trigger date, while the average 13D filer has already accumulated a significant
number of shares: 4.02% at trading day −5 prior to the trigger date. These results are
inconsistent with both of the alternative explanations.
   To further refute these explanations, I focus on the days when blockholders make
large trades and compare insider trading during the five days leading up to large
trades with insider trading conducted five days after large trades, inclusive of the
trading date. If insiders trade based on information about blockholders’ purchases
(my hypothesis), I would expect more insider (net) purchases in the post-large-
trade window than in the pre-large-trade window. In contrast, if both parties trade
independently based on private information of target firms’ undervaluation (joint
causality), I would expect a similar level of insider trading in the post- and pre-
windows, given that the firm’s fundamentals are unlikely to change over such a
short duration. Lastly, if blockholders base their trades on insiders’ abnormal trades
(reverse causality), I would expect more insider (net) purchases during the pre-large
trade window. Testing the predictions of the three explanations (my hypothesis and
the two alternative explanations: reverse causality and joint causality) can illuminate
the impetus for my results. Thus, I compare daily insider trading in the five-trading-
day window before and after blockholders’ large trades. I define a 13D filer’s trade
as large if the trade accounts for more than 20% of trading volume on that day.17 To
ensure that the estimation window does not overlap across large trades, I restrict the
sample to 611 large trades that occur at least 10 trading days away from previous
and subsequent large trades. I run the pooled event-day regression model of the
following form:
              Daily Insider Trading = 𝛼 + 𝛽1 Post Large Trade + Controls + 𝜀.                             (2)
   The dependent variable Daily Inside Trading is either the daily insider purchases,
daily insider sales, or daily insider net purchases. The main variable of interest,
Post Large Trade, is an indicator variable equal to one if the daily observation

5.2.4 Mechanism for insider knowledge of ownership changes

Now that the evidence supports the hypothesis that insiders trade based on their
knowledge of blockholders’ activities, I investigate how insiders acquire information
regarding investors’ trading. The anecdotal evidence discussed in Section 2.1 sug-
gests that managers acquire ownership information by integrating (1) stock surveil-
lance with (2) direct interactions with investors.
   While direct data on firms’ use of stock-surveillance services is inaccessible, I
identify three proxies to capture firms’ investor relations efforts and direct commu-
nication between managers and investors. First, I hand-collect voluntary disclosures
made by blockholders in 13D filings. Approximately 21% of filers report having
communicated with company management prior to submitting their filing. Although
this proxy captures confirmed instances of interaction, it likely underestimates the
true prevalence of communication, as disclosure is voluntary. Therefore, 13Ds with
no disclosed communication remain in the comparison group, potentially leading to
conservative estimates of the effect.
   Second, following the literature that highlights the role of geographic proximity
in facilitating the transfer of soft information (Ahern 2017; Chu et al. 2019; Costello
2013; He et al. 2022), I use geographic distance between the filer and the target
firm as a proxy for potential interaction. I extract zip codes for both filers and target
firms from 2,330 13D filings and calculate the Great Circle distance between the
associated post offices. The assumption is that investors located closer to the target
firm are more likely to meet in person with its executives or informally interact in
ways that could lead to early information transmission.
   Third, I use the number of investor conferences attended by firm management
in the year preceding the 13D filing, sourced from CapitalIQ, which offers reliable
coverage from 2008 onward. This proxies for firms’ broader investor relations
efforts. Because attending investor conferences consumes money and time, firms that
participate more actively in these events likely dedicate greater attention to investor
relations and are more inclined to surveil shareholders.

stronger investor relations engagement or greater likelihood of direct investor inter-

5.3 Extension to 13G disclosures

While managers may pay more attention to activist investors due to potential con-

similarly monitor passive blockholders. To further enhance the generalizability of
my findings, I extend the analyses to the context of 13Gs. Unlike 13Ds, which are
filed by activists, 13Gs are filed by passive investors who cross the 5% threshold
without an intention to influence the management or control of the company.
    Panels A and B of Fig. 4 depict the pattern of insider trading. Notably, insider
purchases begin deviating from the baseline level a few days before the trigger date,
peak on the trigger date, and remain elevated throughout the filing date. Internet

purchases and sales around the disclosure of 13Gs with those in the control period.

from the trigger date to one day prior to the filing date. Insiders also increase their
daily sales by 25.6% during the period of (TD, FD-1).
    Next, I verify the robustness of the unexpected insider trading found during the
TD-5 to FD-1 window. To do so, I conduct regression analyses that control for

and (3) other common determinants of insider trading. Table 5 reports the results.

purchases average 0.074 basis points higher during the pre-disclosure period of
(TD-5, FD-1) than during the control period. Given that the average daily insider
purchases are 0.048 basis points in the control period (Internet Appendix IA.8),
this represents an increase of 154% relative to the control period (= 0.074/0.048).

sales during the pre-disclosure period resembles the baseline level of the control

systematically extract information about blockholder accumulations, regardless of

beyond activism contexts.

6 Conclusion

This study examines whether managers learn about blockholder accumulations from
market signals and reflect that learning in their own trading. I document that insider
net purchases increase significantly in the days preceding 13D and 13G disclosures.
1332                                                                             J. J. Zeng


Importantly, the price movement cannot fully explain managers’ learning, indicating
that insiders can decode investors’ trading signals beyond what’s embedded in
the price and volume information. Specifically, I demonstrate that firms’ investor
relations efforts help facilitate the learning process. The findings suggest that
managerial learning operates through a combination of market observation and
investor engagement. This blended learning complements traditional views of price-
based learning by highlighting the role of relational channels.
