## 1. Question

An institution that holds a block in a firm and privately learns the firm is in trouble can do one of two things with that information: **intervene** (pay a cost to try to fix the firm, which raises the value of the whole block) or **speculate** (trade on the information — including selling out, the "Wall Street rule" / "cut-and-run"). Kahn and Winton ask when the *ability to speculate* strengthens and when it *undermines* the incentive to intervene, and they push the answer through to two further questions: which *firm characteristics* predict intervention rather than cut-and-run, and what *ownership structure* an initial owner (entrepreneur, VC, privatising government) should therefore choose. They also ask whether two legal/contractual devices — short-swing profit forfeiture and restricted (non-tradable) shares — help.

The motivating puzzle is that both behaviours are observed: CalPERS/Putnam/J.P. Morgan intervening at Kodak, IBM, Westinghouse, Borden, American Express and GM in the early 1990s, versus Fidelity Magellan dumping its technology block in late 1995 (pp. 99–100).

## 2. Model / data and method

**Type:** one-shot theory. No calibration, no simulation, no data of the authors’ own.

**Primitives.** One firm, shares normalised to 1. Three dates: 0 (ownership structure chosen; speculators decide whether to gather information), 1 (trading), 2 (liquidation). All agents risk neutral. At time 2 the firm returns 0 or X, split pro rata. Absent intervention the chance of X is q ∈ (0,1) (p. 103).

**Players.**
- **The institution** (the blockholder): holds n shares from time 0; the remaining 1 − n is held by a continuum of infinitesimal small investors (p. 104). Before time-1 trading it learns whether the firm is "distressed". If distressed, the time-2 return is 0 *unless* it intervenes at cost m; intervention converts 0 into X with probability δ. Hence with certain intervention the ex-ante success chance is q_max ≡ q + δ(1 − q) (p. 104).
- **Speculators**: a large number of potential entrants; by paying γ each learns the firm’s situation before time-1 trading, *including whether the institution has intervened*, and whether the intervention succeeded (p. 104). Free entry; S is the equilibrium expected number of active speculators. *(added by verifier)* The institution’s own information is **free** by assumption; n.5 p. 104 records that earlier drafts charged it a cost and that "As long as the institution can acquire information more cheaply than can other investors—perhaps by virtue of its stake in the firm—our basic results are unchanged."
- **Market makers**: competitive, no private information; following Admati–Pfleiderer (1989) and Easley–O’Hara (1992) they post bid and ask P_B, P_A *independent of realised order flow*; Bertrand competition ⇒ zero profits, so P_B is the expected value conditional on a sale and P_A conditional on a buy (p. 105).
- **Noise / liquidity trading**: small investors get a liquidity shock with probability μ of each sign, buying or selling the whole of their holding. Total expected liquidity volume on each side is **L = μ(1 − n)** (p. 105).
- **Position limit**: each large informed investor (institution and each speculator) can buy or sell at most w shares — a stand-in for a margin/short-sale wealth constraint (p. 105).

**Timing** is given in Table I, p. 105.

**Equilibrium notion.** Rational-expectations/Nash: market makers price correctly given the equilibrium intervention probability α and speculator entry S; the institution mixes optimally; speculators enter until expected profit is zero. Existence via Kakutani’s fixed-point theorem applied to the equilibrium price correspondence (Proposition 2, proof p. 122 — "The details are available on request").

**The central object.** Define F ≡ (R_I − R_D)/(1 − q), the normalised net benefit of intervening. Equation (4), p. 108:

> F = δnX − m + δw(X − P_A − P_B)

- **Direct impact** δnX − m: intervention raises the value of the *existing* block.
- **Trading impact** δw(X − P_A − P_B): with probability δ the intervention succeeds, so the institution *buys* w shares (earning X − P_A each) rather than *selling* w (earning P_B each).

And equation (5), p. 108, shows the trading impact has the same sign as (1 − 2q′), where q′ = q + αδ(1 − q) is the equilibrium ex-ante success probability. Intuition: the institution’s informational edge is worth the ex-ante return variance q′(1 − q′)X²; intervention raises q′, so it raises the value of the edge iff q′ < ½ (p. 108).

**Functional forms that buy tractability:** binary firm return {0, X}; perfectly informed institution and speculators; order-flow-independent quotes; identical γ across speculators (this is what makes liquidity neutral — see R10); fixed trade size w.

## 4. Institutional facts used

Very few, and **none of them is a stake-disclosure rule**. In full:

- **Short-swing profit forfeiture (US insider-trading regulation)**: "U.S. insider trading regulations classify institutions with 10% or more of a firm’s shares as insiders, so that they forfeit any “short-swing” profits earned by buying or selling shares and then reversing the trade within a six-month period" (p. 118). This is §16(b) of the Exchange Act, though the paper never names the statute. The **10% stake threshold** and the **six-month horizon** are the only quantitative legal parameters in the paper. In the model this is generalised to an arbitrary threshold n̄ (p. 118).
- **SEC Rule 144A**: "In the United States, SEC Rule 144A allows private resale of such shares subject to certain conditions, but this resale market is still relatively illiquid" (n.17, p. 119) — cited to justify treating restricted shares as illiquid.
- **The one other securities-law statement in the paper** *(added by verifier)*, n.19 p. 120: "Note that the legal prohibition on using “inside” information would not apply to information such as in-person appraisals of the CEO’s motives and ability." This is what licenses the whole model — the blockholder is allowed to trade on exactly the soft, access-derived information its block buys it. It is the only place the paper says anything about what a blockholder may lawfully trade on, and it is the natural hook for a disclosure-rule extension.
- **Votes appear once** *(added by verifier)*, n.16 p. 119: "if larger stakes make intervention cheaper or more likely to succeed (because the institution controls more votes outright), the short-swing rule is even more inefficient; by reducing the institution’s stake, it makes whatever intervention occurs more costly and ineffective." Voting power is otherwise not in the model — intervention costs a flat m regardless of the stake. Also relevant: p. 113 notes that "effective control can be obtained with a fraction of the firm’s shares (particularly through the use of different classes of voting rights, or pyramiding schemes)".
- **Institutional anecdotes**: the early-1990s activism wave (CalPERS, Putnam, J.P. Morgan; targets Kodak, IBM, Westinghouse, Borden, American Express, GM) (pp. 99–100); Fidelity Magellan — "by the end of March 1995, 49 percent of Magellan’s holdings were stakes of 5 percent or more in the target firms’ shares", and in late 1995 Magellan "reduced its technology holdings from 45 percent to 8.5 percent just in advance of a downturn in those stocks" (p. 100). Note: the "5 percent" here is a *descriptive* statistic about Magellan’s block sizes, **not** a reference to the Schedule 13D filing threshold.
- **Institutional environment abroad**: interlocking shareholdings in Japan (Sheard 1994) and the limits on US corporate pension fund activism (Roe 1994) (p. 114); combined debt-and-equity stakes in Germany and Japan (n.11 p. 114).
- Zeckhauser–Pound (1990), Silber (1991), Hertzel–Smith (1993) evidence as above.

So: **the blockholder in Kahn–Winton is never flagged.** Its intervention is observed by the informed speculators (by assumption, p. 104) and never by the market makers, who "only know the ex ante equilibrium probabilities that these events have occurred" (pp. 105–106; the sentence runs across the page break). There is no public revelation of the stake or of its purpose, no filing, and no window.

**Necessary qualification** *(added by verifier)* — the card's flat "never flagged" holds for the *time-1 intervention* but not for the *time-0 acquisition*. In §III.B the observability of the block purchase does work: small shareholders' selling price P0 depends on whether they can tell the institution is buying, and the paper's modification (2), pp. 117–118, turns on liquidity trading **masking** the purchase — "Because liquidity trading masks the institution's share purchases, P0 is an average of the marginal shareholder's value when the institution is present (and subsequently intervenes) and this value when the institution is not present." The word used elsewhere is "anonymously" (p. 114: "When the institution buys shares anonymously on the open market …"; p. 102, on Shleifer–Vishny, "an active and anonymous stock market"). So Kahn–Winton *do* have a pooled-versus-separating margin on the accumulation, sketched in one paragraph and never modelled, and it is exactly the margin a 13D filing closes. This does not weaken our position — it strengthens the citation, because they name the channel, credit it to Kyle–Vila (1991), and then set it aside.

**Takeovers and bidders.** The word "takeover" occurs **four** times in the body (pp. 104, 113, 114, 117–118) plus twice in reference titles on p. 128 (Grossman–Hart 1980, "Takeover bids …"; Kyle–Vila 1991, "Noise trading and takeovers") — *count corrected by verifier; the p. 113 hit is hyphenated across a line as "take-over" and is easy to miss on a naïve grep*. Every body occurrence is in passing and none is an outcome:
1. As one unmodelled *form* of intervention: "In practice, getting management to make the change might involve threatening (and possibly carrying out) a takeover or proxy fight, but we abstract from these details" (p. 104). The other forms named are spinning off "a money-losing, unrelated division to an acquirer with the right managerial expertise", replacing "a somewhat below-average CEO" (p. 101), "removal of takeover deterrents" (p. 113), and fixing "focus" or "agency" problems — "extraneous money-losing divisions, or excessive layers of management" (p. 120).
2. As an *analogy* for the block-acquisition holdout: "these externalities have effects analogous to the holdout problem analyzed in Grossman and Hart’s (1980) paper on takeovers" (p. 114).
3. As a cited related result: Kyle and Vila’s (1991) "analysis of how liquidity trading can mitigate holdout problems in takeovers" (pp. 117–118).

There is **no bidder**, no bid, no tender offer, no premium, and no control contest anywhere in the model. Intervention is the *only* channel and its payoff is δX, an increase in the firm’s own cash flow, not a premium paid by an outsider.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- The core insight is clean, general, and stated in one line: intervention and speculation are complements **iff** intervention raises the value of the blockholder’s information relative to the market’s — which in a binomial world reduces to q′ < ½ (pp. 108–109). This survives well beyond the binomial specialisation.
- Everything of substance is *proved*: seven numbered analytic results plus two appendix propositions, with existence (Kakutani) and uniqueness both established under stated sufficient conditions.
- It endogenises **speculator entry**, and that single addition produces the paper’s most surprising result — the neutrality of liquidity (R10) and of the concentration-induced liquidity loss (R11). *(Verifier: the card originally said "which no earlier paper in this line did". The paper itself makes the weaker and checkable claim, at p. 103, that relative to Maug (1998) "we incorporate additional large traders, and find that trading effects are of little concern for firms where costs of gathering information are relatively low and competition among informed traders is high." Use that form; the stronger priority claim is not made in the paper and was not checked.)*
- It converts the theory into a *falsifiable cross-section* (§V) rather than a comparative static on an unobservable, which is why it became the theory the empirical activism literature tests.
- It is explicit and even-handed about its own caveats: the delayed-resolution extension (p. 113), private control benefits (pp. 113–114), the deadweight-loss treatment of speculators’ information costs — "These considerations suggest important caveats to any results that depend on increases or decreases in the number of speculators" (p. 116).

**Relation to Maug (1998) — the biggest omission in the reader's draft, added by verifier.** The card as first written never mentioned Maug (1998), even though Kahn–Winton devote three paragraphs of the introduction (pp. 102–103), a footnote (n.12, p. 114) and a sentence of §III.B (p. 117) to the relationship. For a card whose whole point is liquidity, this cannot be left out. What they say:

- "Most closely related to our work is that of Maug (1998), who also analyzes an institutional investor’s intervention decision when the institution’s private knowledge of its decision may allow it to earn trading profits at the expense of liquidity traders. Although the two papers begin with the same problem, the modeling assumptions are somewhat different, reflecting a difference in focus." (p. 102)
- Maug's result, as Kahn–Winton state it: "He shows that increased liquidity trading may actually increase the institution’s desire to acquire a large initial stake and intervene." The mechanism is the Grossman–Hart holdout — "Fear of trading at a loss to meet future liquidity needs reduces small shareholders’ reservation price, making it more attractive for the institution to acquire shares." (p. 103)
- Their own differentiation: "we focus on how differing firm characteristics affect the institution’s intervention decision … we incorporate additional large traders … We also make firm outcomes partly independent of intervention"; and "To illustrate his main point, Maug focuses on the case where the holdout problem is most severe … Because private placements of shares can alleviate the holdout problem, we analyze ownership structure choice under both private placements and open market purchases." (p. 103)
- And decisively for us, n.12, p. 114: "As noted in the introduction, our results when the institution buys shares on the open market are essentially analogous to those of Maug (1998)"; restated at p. 117, "As in Maug (1998), the institution gains no direct benefit from intervention."

**Consequence for the card's positioning.** Kahn–Winton's liquidity *neutrality* (R10) is a statement about the **exogenous/private-placement** branch of their paper. In the **open-market** branch (§III.B, pp. 116–118) liquidity is *not* neutral: it depresses P0, it masks the accumulation, and it is what makes acquiring a block profitable at all. So "Kahn–Winton say liquidity does nothing" is only true of one branch and must be said that way. See the revised point 2 in §7.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **The liquidity result is a knife-edge.** μ is neutral only because every speculator has the *same* cost γ, so entry exactly absorbs any extra noise trading. The authors themselves say that with a distribution of costs "higher liquidity volume would attract less efficient speculators, raising equilibrium trading profits" and the neutrality breaks (p. 112, repeated p. 122). Any paper that wants liquidity to *do* something has this door held open for it by the authors.
- **Quotes are order-flow-independent** (Admati–Pfleiderer / Easley–O’Hara pricing, p. 105). There is no Kyle-style price impact, no learning from order flow, and hence no channel by which the blockholder’s *own* trade moves the price. A referee trained on Kyle (1985)/Back would call this the paper’s biggest modelling shortcut.
- **The information assumption is acknowledged as "extremely strong"** (p. 104): speculators know exactly what the institution knows, *including* whether it intervened and whether the intervention succeeded. The blockholder’s edge over the market is therefore an edge over *market makers*, not over other informed traders. *(added by verifier)* The authors do defend it in the same breath, and any critique of ours should quote the defence too: "Nonetheless, weakening this assumption would not change our qualitative results—as will be seen, our assumptions already give the institution a trading advantage over the speculators." (p. 104). The advantage in question is that the institution's information is free and the speculators' costs γ.
- **One-shot trading.** The institution cannot wait for the intervention to resolve and trade then; the authors flag this and note that by then "its intervention may well have become public, reducing the value of the institution’s information" (p. 113) — the closest the paper comes to a disclosure mechanism, and it is a single sentence in a robustness discussion.
- **No welfare-relevant role for prices.** Speculators’ information cost is pure deadweight loss by construction (p. 115), which the authors flag (p. 116) as a caveat that could reverse the ownership-structure conclusions.
- **No private benefits of control, and the authors know what dropping them costs** *(expanded by verifier — the card had only pointed at the page)*. p. 113: "Such benefits would reduce the large shareholder’s willingness to sell shares, encouraging intervention over pure speculation, but now the large shareholder would intervene so as to increase some weighted sum of total shareholder value and private control benefits." And p. 114: "although the trade-off between speculation and intervention would loom less large if control benefits were important, the positive relationship between ownership concentration and firm value would also be undermined" (citing Bebchuk–Zingales (1996) and Pagano–Röell (1996)). This matters to us: private benefits of control are the standard ingredient of a takeover-premium model, and Kahn–Winton say plainly that adding them *shrinks* their own exit/voice trade-off. Any model of ours that carries both a control premium and an exit/voice margin has to answer this sentence.
- **No empirical work of their own.** §V is verbal prediction plus three citations to others’ results. No sample, no estimate, no test.
- Existence (p. 122) and Corollary 6(i) (p. 127) both rest on steps "available on request" rather than in the paper.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**Object.** The object is the **intervention/monitoring decision** of a single institutional blockholder and the resulting **ownership structure** (the size of the block an initial owner should sell, and the choice between open-market accumulation and private placement). It is *not* the takeover premium, *not* bidder entry, *not* campaign success rates, *not* announcement returns, and *not* the blockholder’s disclosure choice.

**Margin.** **None of the disclosure rule.** No threshold margin, no window margin — the words "disclosure", "13D", "Williams" and "Schedule" do not occur in the body text at all (see the grep in §4). The two regulatory margins the paper *does* study are (a) the **short-swing profit forfeiture** at a stake threshold n̄, calibrated to the US 10%/six-month rule, and (b) **share restriction** (a contractual, not statutory, ban on resale). Both operate on the blockholder’s *ability to trade*, not on what the market gets to *observe*. In the paper’s information structure the market makers never learn the institution’s action at all; they price on the equilibrium probability α.

**Identification.** **Theory only.** No estimation, no simulation, no calibration, no data of the authors’ own; §V is verbal prediction plus three citations to others’ published evidence.

**Where they say so, verbatim.**
- On takeovers as a mechanism: "In practice, getting management to make the change might involve threatening (and possibly carrying out) a takeover or proxy fight, but we abstract from these details." (p. 104)
- On the whole exercise: "Clearly, the interactions between liquidity demand, speculation, and intervention are complex; our model has examined only a simple case." (p. 122)
- On heterogeneous speculators and the institution’s own liquidity needs, explicitly left open: "As noted in Section II, with heterogeneous speculators, an increase in liquidity demand should attract less efficient speculators on the margin, increasing the institution’s equilibrium trading profits and their importance in the institution’s intervention decision. Also, the institution’s own liquidity needs may affect the intervention decision, particularly if such needs tilt the institution more toward selling rather than buying shares. Detailed analysis of these issues could lead to useful cross-sectional relationships between shareholder composition and intervention." (p. 122)
- On multiple blockholders and coalitions, left open: "However, multiple monitors may be useful if monitoring quality is sufficiently convex in effort (see Winton (1993)), or if the signals received by different monitors are imperfectly correlated. Also, if the effectiveness of monitoring depends on the number of shares controlled, multiple large shareholders might gain from forming coalitions." (n.6, p. 104)
- On the informativeness of prices: "These considerations suggest important caveats to any results that depend on increases or decreases in the number of speculators and their total costs of gathering information." (p. 116)
- On dynamic trading and the block becoming public: "Of course, in a model with repeated trading periods, the institution might be able to wait until the outcome of the intervention is resolved and then trade, but by that point its intervention may well have become public, reducing the value of the institution’s information." (p. 113)

**There is no sentence anywhere in the paper leaving *disclosure rules* to future work** — not because they rule it out, but because the topic never arises. The nearest thing is the single clause at p. 113 above ("its intervention may well have become public"). That is the whole of it. This is a genuine absence, not a hedged omission, and it should be reported that way rather than dressed up as an invitation.

## 7. Implications for our position

**Where Kahn–Winton sits.**
- **OBJECT:** the blockholder’s intervention-versus-speculation decision, and equilibrium ownership concentration. Control outcomes in our sense (bidder entry, takeover premium, campaign success) are absent — intervention pays off as a δ-probability jump in the firm’s own cash flow, never as a premium extracted from a bidder.
- **MARGIN:** none of the disclosure rule. The regulatory margins occupied are the **trading-restriction** margins: short-swing forfeiture at a 10% stake threshold with a six-month horizon (pp. 118–119) and restricted shares (p. 119). Both bind the blockholder’s *trades*; neither changes the market’s **partition** (in CONTEXT.md’s sense, the split between flagged and pooled states). Market makers in Kahn–Winton are permanently in the pooled state.
- **IDENTIFICATION:** theory only.

**What this buys our position (the whitespace it leaves open).**
1. **The partition on the *intervention* is untouched — but not the partition on the *accumulation*.** *(revised by verifier.)* Kahn–Winton’s institution is never flagged when it intervenes: that action is seen by the informed speculators (by assumption) and never by the market makers, who "only know the ex ante equilibrium probabilities that these events have occurred" (pp. 105–106). Our disclosure rule is precisely the device that moves probability mass from the pooled state to the flagged state, and on the intervention margin there is no overlap; the closest they get is one clause about intervention becoming public in a hypothetical dynamic model (p. 113). **But their §III.B does turn on whether the market can tell the block is being bought** — modification (2), pp. 117–118: "Because liquidity trading masks the institution’s share purchases, P0 is an average of the marginal shareholder’s value when the institution is present (and subsequently intervenes) and this value when the institution is not present." So write the whitespace claim as *"the partition is never a policy instrument in Kahn–Winton, and the one place observability matters — the masked accumulation of pp. 117–118 — is sketched in a paragraph and left unmodelled"*, not as *"the partition is untouched"*. A referee who knows §III.B will catch the stronger version.
2. **They pre-authorise our liquidity margin — with two named escape hatches, and only in one branch of their paper.** *(revised by verifier.)* Their headline liquidity result is *neutrality* (R10): μ does not move intervention while speculators are active, because free entry of identical speculators exactly offsets. They then name **two** conditions under which it fails: (a) heterogeneous speculator costs, "if speculators’ costs of gathering information were not identical, but were drawn from a distribution, higher liquidity volume would attract less efficient speculators, raising equilibrium trading profits" (p. 112, restated p. 122); and (b) concentration high enough to drive speculators out entirely, after which "further reductions in the volume of liquidity trading are now internalized by the institution" (n.8, p. 110 — R10a). Moreover neutrality is a statement about the branch where the stake is exogenous or privately placed. In the **open-market** branch it is not neutral: liquidity depresses P0 and is what makes acquiring a block profitable (eq. (9), p. 117), which is why they say "our results when the institution buys shares on the open market are essentially analogous to those of Maug (1998)" (n.12, p. 114). So the honest positioning line is: **Kahn–Winton’s liquidity *neutrality* is a knife-edge property of homogeneous free entry inside one branch of their model, and the authors themselves name the two assumptions that break it** — not "Kahn–Winton find liquidity does nothing."
   - **2b. Maug (1998) is the paper we actually have to beat on the liquidity margin, and Kahn–Winton point at him.** *(added by verifier.)* Kahn–Winton report Maug's result as "increased liquidity trading may actually increase the institution’s desire to acquire a large initial stake and intervene" (p. 103), running through the Grossman–Hart holdout: liquidity lowers small shareholders’ reservation price, so the block is cheaper to build. Our κ result must be stated against *that* benchmark, not only against KW's neutrality. Read `research/cards/maug_1998_jf.md` alongside this one before writing the liquidity paragraph of the intro.
3. **The sign of the trading motive is theirs, and we should not re-derive it as new.** "intervention and speculation are complements only when intervention increases the institution’s information advantage over other traders" (p. 109) is the canonical statement. Any exit-versus-voice comparative static in our core model that reduces to "the blockholder prefers to raise the odds of the *less expected* outcome" is Kahn–Winton, not us. Our claim to novelty must live in the *partition* and in the *control outcome*, not in the exit/voice trade-off itself.
4. **Their threshold n\* is the natural place to hang the disclosure threshold.** They have a stake threshold at which behaviour switches (n\*, endogenous, pp. 109–110) and a *legal* stake threshold that changes trading rights (n̄, p. 118) — but the legal one never changes what anyone *observes*. Slotting an observation-changing 5% threshold beside their trading-changing 10% threshold is a clean, defensible step, and their §IV is the template for how to write it.
5. **The window margin has no antecedent here at all.** The model has one trading date; there is no interval between crossing a stake level and being revealed. Nothing in Kahn–Winton speaks to the 10 → 5 business-day acceleration.
6. **Deliverability caution.** Their result R2 (higher L narrows the spread) and R11 (concentration cuts L but entry offsets) both come from order-flow-*independent* quotes. If our core model uses Kyle-style price impact, we cannot cite their comparative statics as a benchmark without saying the microstructure differs.
