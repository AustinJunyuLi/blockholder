## 1. Question

What determines the depth of a market in which a large outsider accumulates shares ahead of a takeover, and how does that depth feed back into whether the takeover happens at all? The paper's motivating puzzle is the risk-arbitrage desk: how can a large trader make systematic speculative profits in a market that is nonetheless informationally efficient, when the size of his own holding changes the economic value of the shares (p. 54)? The economic point is that "noise trading" — uninformative liquidity/life-cycle trading — is camouflage: it lets an outsider buy a large stake cheaply, and having bought it he then *wants* to declare a takeover he would otherwise have skipped. Kyle and Vila position this as a third route to profitable takeovers alongside Grossman–Hart (1980) dilution and Shleifer–Vishny (1986) large incumbent blocks, and show neither of those is necessary (p. 55).

## 2. Model / data and method

**Primitives (§2, pp. 56–57).** Three trader types: one large trader, noise traders, value-based investors. One round of trading, then liquidation under "predetermined rules that incorporate a reduced-form model of the takeover process" (p. 56).

- Noise traders **sell** an exogenous random quantity z̃ with a **two-state** distribution (p. 58): z = z₀ w.p. 1 − λ (state 0), z = z₁ w.p. λ (state 1), with z₁ > z₀, 0 < λ < 1. Heavier noise selling (state 1) is the state favourable to a takeover; λ is the probability of the favourable state.
- The large trader **observes z̃** and submits a market order X(z̃) (a mixed strategy in general; degenerate under pure strategies). He must fix quantity before price.
- Value-based investors observe **only the net supply y = z̃ − X(z̃)**, "but they do not observe z̃ or X(z̃) separately" (p. 56), and set P(y). They are risk-neutral Bertrand competitors, so the model is the "market order" equilibrium of Kyle (1985) with these investors playing the market-maker role (p. 56).
- **Takeover technology.** S shares outstanding. No takeover ⇒ each share worth p*. Takeover ⇒ the large trader makes a tender offer for all outstanding shares and the firm is worth (p* + v)S + G, where **v is an exogenously specified per-share premium** he must pay and G is his dilution gain (p. 56). He pays a cost K (legal fees etc.). With initial holding X₀, the **no-trade takeover benefit is β = X₀v + G − K** (p. 56). Having traded x, he declares the takeover iff **vx + β ≥ 0** (ties ⇒ declare).
- **Profit** (eq. 1, p. 57): Π(x, z) = [p* − P(z − x)]x + (vx + β)⁺.

**Equilibrium notion (§3, p. 57).** A trading rule X and a pricing rule P: ℝ → [p*, p* + v] such that (1) **market efficiency**: P(y) = p* + vθ(y) where θ(y) = prob{vX(z̃) + β ≥ 0 | z̃ − X(z̃) = y} (eq. 2); (2) **profit maximisation**: for every realisation of z̃, x = X(z) maximises Π(x, z). The large trader trades "like a textbook monopsonist" against the residual demand schedule (p. 57). Range restriction to [p*, p*+v] is justified by a sequential-equilibrium (Kreps–Wilson 1982) argument. **No short-sale constraints** (p. 57).

**Functional forms that buy tractability.** (i) Binary noise: the whole model collapses to the single statistic **z₁ − z₀**, "the appropriate measure of the amount of noise trading" (p. 65) — a common shift in z₀ and z₁ has no substantive effect. (ii) One round of trading, so no time dimension at all. (iii) Linear-in-quantity share value: the takeover adds exactly v per share, so the payoff is piecewise linear in x with one kink at vx + β = 0; that is the *only* non-convexity. (iv) Exogenous v and G: no tendering game, no free-rider problem to solve. (v) Everything is then indexed by **two** numbers: λ and **γ ≡ β / [(z₁ − z₀)v]** (eq. 17, p. 60) — the ratio of the ex-ante takeover benefit to the maximum profit the large trader could ever make trading in an efficient market.

**Solution method.** §4 gives an intuitive first-order-condition derivation; the Appendix (pp. 67–71) gives the rigorous argument, recast in (y, θ)-space on the rectangle B = [z₀, z₁] × [0, 1], via a **pooling point** on a **weak Pareto frontier** (an Edgeworth-box contract curve between the trader's state-0 and state-1 preferences). Lemma A1: (y, θ) sustains pooled profit maximisation ⟺ it lies on the weak Pareto frontier. Lemma A2: that frontier is the graph of Y(θ) = z₀ + θ(z₁ − z₀) (eq. A13) over an interval whose endpoints θ₀, θ₁ are explicit functions of γ given by (A11)–(A12), p. 68. (The `.txt` extract mangles A11–A12; the values that matter are recoverable from the body text: θ(y*) = γ^{1/2} at the mixing-in-state-0 endpoint (eq. 23, p. 62) and θ(y*) = 1 − (−γ)^{1/2} at the mixing-in-state-1 endpoint (eq. 26, p. 63) — both cross-checked here against Table 1's μ₀ and μ₁.)

**No data.** No calibration, no empirics, no numerical solver. Figures 2–5 are plots of the closed forms in Table 1 at λ = ½ (Figs. 2–4 with (z₁ − z₀)v = 1; Fig. 5 with v = 1 and β = ±1, x-axis (z₁ − z₀)v ∈ [0, 10]).

**Where the equilibrium lands (pure-strategy pooling, pp. 59–60).** The trader buys in state 1 and shorts in state 0 by exactly the amounts that make the market see the same net supply y* = z₁ − X(z₁) = z₀ − X(z₀) (eq. 4, p. 58). Then:

**Disclosure: what is in the paper.** Grepping the full text for `disclos`, `13D`, `Williams`, `5 percent`, `threshold`, `reveal`, `announce`, `toehold` returns **exactly one** relevant hit — footnote 1 on p. 54 — plus one incidental use of "revealed" (p. 55, about information revealed by order flow). There is **no** mandatory disclosure, no Williams Act, no 13D, no stake threshold, no filing window, no fixed revelation date, and no announcement event anywhere in the model. The only "revelation" is the market's Bayesian inference from net order flow y.

**Common knowledge vs. learning (the CDF question).** In Kyle–Vila the large trader's **existence, his takeover option, and the value of β are common knowledge**: β is a non-random constant, and the market's belief is defined as θ(y) = prob{vX(z̃) + β ≥ 0 | z̃ − X(z̃) = y} (eq. 2, p. 57) — the conditioning is over the noise state only. What the market must learn from order flow is **not "is there a raider?" but "which state of noise trading occurred", hence how much the known raider traded and therefore whether he will declare**. Value-based investors see only the sum y = z̃ − X(z̃) and "do not observe z̃ or X(z̃) separately" (p. 56). **In the pure-strategy pooling region** (−(1 − λ)² < γ < λ²) the equilibrium is engineered so that exactly one quantity y* is observed in both states, so on path the market's posterior is the single number λ and the price never moves: P(y*) = p* + λv. *(corrected by verifier)* **Outside that region this is not true**: with mixing, the trader chooses y* with probability 1 − μᵢ and a *fully anticipated* quantity with probability μᵢ, at which the market infers the outcome with certainty and the price is p* + v or p* (pp. 62–63; (A16)–(A17), p. 69). So on path the market's information is already a **two-cell partition — pooled or fully revealed —** whenever γ lies outside the pure-pooling window. What is uniquely pooled is the *pooling point*, not the whole equilibrium. Either way the camouflage is of *quantity*, not of *identity*, the flag is generated by the trader's own indifference rather than by a rule, and there is no dynamic learning at all (one round).

## 4. Institutional facts used

Essentially none. The paper is fully abstract: no statute, no threshold, no date, no exchange, no dataset. The only institutional gestures are (i) the risk-arbitrage industry as motivation (p. 54), (ii) K described as "legal fees and other expenses" of declaring a takeover (p. 56), (iii) a tender offer "for all outstanding shares" as the takeover form (p. 56), (iv) "policies which reduce stock turnover, like a transactions tax or more widespread use of passive portfolio management techniques" named as the policies the model cannot evaluate (p. 67), and (v) footnote 1's explicit statement that disclosure requirements are abstracted from (p. 54).

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths**
- Complete characterisation: Proposition 1 covers the whole parameter space in two numbers (λ, γ) with closed forms for probabilities and profits, and the Appendix proves uniqueness of the *outcome* (not just existence) plus uniqueness of the pooling point.
- The mechanism is stated in a way that survives generalisation: camouflage → a cheap stake → an ex-post incentive to declare that did not exist ex ante. This is the sentence every later liquidity-and-governance paper is a variation on.
- The sign reversal (R7) is genuinely two-sided and is the paper's real content: liquidity does not "help governance", it pulls the takeover probability **toward λ from both directions**. That is an unusually honest comparative static for 1991.
- Explicit and repeated about its own limits (pp. 55–56, 67, 71).

**Weaknesses / open flanks**
- **Binary noise, one round.** Everything is z₁ − z₀. There is no notion of a trading horizon, so no window, no dynamics, no price path, no accumulation over days. They flag this and defer it to Vila (1986) (pp. 56, 58).
- **v is a primitive.** The premium is assumed, not derived; free-riding is discussed (p. 55) but not solved — the tender offer is a liquidation rule, not a game. Consequence: the model has **no premium comparative statics at all**, and no announcement return.
- **One raider, by assumption.** "The first assumption requires the existence of some barrier to entry that prevents other potential large traders from engaging in takeover activity" (p. 55). Bidder entry and bidder competition — one of our three control outcomes — are assumed away.
- **Unlimited short selling.** Half the equilibrium (state 0) is a *would-be acquirer shorting the target and then withholding a bid* — the paper's own word for the deviation version of this is "spiking" the takeover (p. 62). *(added by verifier)* This is not an artefact of the pure-strategy case: the Appendix proves it for **every** equilibrium — "since Π₀ = −Θ(y)x₀ ≥ β⁺ (and Θ(y) > 0 at a pooling point), we must have x₀ ≤ 0, i.e., the large trader goes (weakly) short when he does not take over" (p. 67). Legally this is close to the conduct disclosure rules exist to police; the baseline permits it without comment, and the short-sale-constrained case lives only in an unpublished working paper (p. 71).
- **The price barely moves on path — and never over time.** *(corrected by verifier: the original bullet said the price never moves at all.)* In the pure-strategy pooling window the equilibrium price really is the constant p* + λv. Outside it, mixing puts two prices on path — the pooling price p* + θ(y*)v and a fully anticipated p* or p* + v (pp. 62–63, 69–70) — so there is a one-shot revelation event but still no *path*: one round of trading means no price impact over time, no run-up, no announcement window, nothing an event study could be run against.
- **Internal inconsistency at p. 66.** The text there says "When β = 1, takeovers would always occur in the absence of trading… Noise trading increases the probability of a takeover and increases the large trader's expected profits." The top-left panel of Figure 5 on that same page shows the β = +1 probability **falling** from 1.00 to 0.50, and the paper's own italicised summary (p. 64), its abstract (p. 54) and the closing sentence of that same paragraph (p. 66) all say noise trading **discourages** takeovers when they are not costly. Treat the p. 66 sentence as a typo; **never cite p. 66 for the sign** — cite p. 64 or the abstract.
- No welfare analysis, by their own choice (p. 67). No empirical section, no testable-implications section: §6 "Implications and other applications" is about Hirshleifer (1971) social cost and about applying the framework to futures-market squeezes, not about data.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **Disclosure — declared out of scope in a footnote on the first page.** "The model abstracts from disclosure requirements. We do not require the large trader to notify the market if he purchases or sells a significant amount of stock." (p. 54, n. 1). This is the single most useful sentence in the paper for us: the antecedent explicitly hands over the disclosure margin.
- **The premium.** "We do not model in this article where the premium v comes from; it is exogenously specified." (p. 56).
- **Welfare / efficiency.** "Our approach ignores the social costs and benefits of such actions, because considering them would raise different issues that take us beyond the scope of this article." and "Thus, without further modelling, we cannot conclude that policies which reduce stock turnover, like a transactions tax or more widespread use of passive portfolio management techniques, have favorable or unfavorable welfare implications." (both p. 67).
- **Multi-state noise and multiple trading rounds.** "Other restrictive assumptions are that noise trading has a two-state distribution and there is one round of trading. Vila (1986) discusses some implications of relaxing these assumptions." (pp. 55–56). *(added by verifier)* They repeat the deferral where the two-state distribution is imposed: "Consideration of the more complicated case in which z̃ has more than two states takes us beyond the scope of this article, but some aspects of this case are considered by Vila (1986) and Rochet and Vila (1991)" (p. 58) — so the richer noise structure is handed to **two** unpublished manuscripts, not one.
- **Competing bidders.** Ruled out by an assumed barrier to entry (p. 55).
- **Short-sale constraints.** Deferred to an unpublished working-paper version (p. 71).
- **Empirics.** None; no data, no sample, no test.

## 7. Implications for our position

**The cell Kyle–Vila occupy.**
- **OBJECT:** the ex-ante and state-conditional **probability of a takeover**, the **raider's expected profit**, **market depth**, and the **size of the stake accumulated** (X(z₁)). *Not* the premium (v is a primitive), *not* efficiency/welfare (refused, p. 67), *not* bidder entry (a single raider behind an assumed entry barrier), *not* announcement returns (the on-path price is constant).
- **MARGIN of a disclosure rule: none, and explicitly so** (p. 54, n. 1). No threshold, no window, no revelation date, no flagged state. The market's information is the single pooled statistic y = z̃ − X(z̃), and the equilibrium is built so that only one y is ever observed.
- **IDENTIFICATION: theory only.** Closed-form propositions plus four figures that plot those closed forms at λ = ½. No data, no calibration, no structural estimation.
- **DRIVER:** liquidity, measured as z₁ − z₀. This is literally our κ before it had a name.

**What this constrains.** We cannot claim as new: (a) that liquidity is the driver of control outcomes; (b) that camouflage/pooling is the mechanism; (c) that liquidity raises the blockholder's profit and the stake he accumulates; (d) that liquidity's effect on the *probability* of control change is **sign-reversing** — pushing it toward the pooling value λ from above when control is already profitable and from below when it is costly (R7, p. 64). Any non-monotonicity-in-liquidity claim of ours (the "hump") must be stated against this: Kyle–Vila already own the two-sided, sign-flipping comparative static for the takeover-probability object, and they own it as a PROVED result, not a grid.

**What this supports — the whitespace, precisely.** Our disclosure rule is exactly the object Kyle–Vila's structure is missing, and they say so on page one. Concretely, a **stake-triggered disclosure rule with a filing window** added on top of their structure would add five things they do not have:

1. **A rule-driven partition where theirs is an artefact of the trader's own indifference.** *(corrected by verifier: the original bullet said their market's information is "one number, always pooled" — that holds only inside the pure-strategy pooling window.)* Their Appendix proves every equilibrium supports exactly **one pooling point** (p. 69), but outside the pooling window the equilibrium already splits on path into a pooled branch and a fully revealed branch, chosen by the trader's mixing probability μᵢ (pp. 62–63, 69–70). So the useful contrast is not "they have no partition" — it is **whose partition it is**. Theirs is endogenous, un-keyed to anything observable, and available only where the trader happens to be indifferent; a disclosure rule imposes a partition **keyed to the stake and to a date**, present in every parameter configuration, and unrelated to the trader's indifference. Their uniqueness-of-the-pooling-point proof is what fails once the flag is triggered by a threshold rather than by mixing.
2. **A threshold margin where their stake choice is unconstrained.** X(z₁) = (1 − λ)(z₁ − z₀) grows linearly and without limit in liquidity (eq. 11, p. 60). A 5%-type trigger puts a kink in the raider's problem that their payoff does not have — their only kink is the takeover option (vx + β)⁺ — and produces bunching just below the trigger or a discrete jump past it. Their comparative static "more liquidity ⇒ bigger toehold" becomes a *threshold-truncated* one; that truncation is our threshold margin, and it is entirely new relative to them. *(added by verifier, two supports for this bullet.)* (a) The pre-existing stake X₀ enters their model **only through β = X₀v + G − K** (p. 56) — it is never a state variable, so a trigger defined on the total holding X₀ + x has no object to attach to in their formalism at all. (b) They do have one liquidity threshold of their own, and it runs the other way: for (z₁ − z₀)v ≤ \|β\| the trader does not trade at all (eqs. 19–20, pp. 61–62), so their model already says stake-building switches **on** above a liquidity cutoff. Our rule switches it **off** above a stake cutoff. Those two thresholds interacting is a comparative static neither paper has.
3. **A window margin where they have no time.** They have exactly **one round of trading** (p. 56) and defer multi-round to Vila (1986). A filing window is a duration over which the raider keeps buying *after* the trigger has bound but *before* the market is flagged. There is no dimension in Kyle–Vila in which to state a window comparative static at all — so the 10 → 5 business-day change is not a re-parameterisation of their model; it needs a model they did not write.
4. **A premium that can move.** With v exogenous, a disclosure rule in their model could only shift *whether* a takeover occurs, never *at what price*. Our premium wedge (m₁ − m₀ / λ-scaled tender game) is the object that makes "disclosure → premium" a statement rather than a definitional impossibility. Kyle–Vila hand us this too: "We do not model in this article where the premium v comes from" (p. 56).
5. **A welfare verdict they explicitly refuse.** Their p. 67 disclaimer covers precisely the class our rule belongs to — a policy that changes how much informed trading the market absorbs. Quote it to establish that the welfare question on this margin was left open by the antecedent rather than settled against us.

**What we must be careful about.** Their §6 (p. 67) contains the *counter-force*: adverse selection from the large trader's own presence reduces depth, which raises the cost of a position, which reduces the incentive to undertake the costly control action. A disclosure rule is an adverse-selection amplifier, so our disclosure-attenuation claim should be presented as operating through *this* Kyle–Vila channel (raising the effective cost of accumulating), not as a fresh mechanism. Doing so makes the claim harder to attack and cites the antecedent on our side.
