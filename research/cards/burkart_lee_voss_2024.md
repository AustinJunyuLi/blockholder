## 1. Question

Why did the mode of disciplinary control change shift from hostile tender offers (the 1980s pattern) to *takeover activism* — large shareholders who do not buy the firm themselves but broker its sale to an outside bidder? Their answer is not legal: it is the growth of capital at the disposal of control-oriented investors (activist hedge funds, private equity). They ask, at the firm level, when an informed large shareholder prefers to be on the buy side versus the sell side, and, at the market level, how the equilibrium composition of the investor population evolves as the measure of control investors grows.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Firm-level model (Section 2).** One-share-one-vote firm, mass 1 of shares, incumbent value normalised to 0. Large shareholder `L` holds a minority block α ∈ (0, ᾱ); the rest is a continuum of atomistic holders. Restructuring raises value to `V ~ F` on [0,1] with continuously differentiable, full-support density `f`. `L` observes `V` privately and has the restructuring technology; outside bidder `B` has the same technology but learns `V` only once in control. This is Shleifer & Vishny (1986) plus one new option.

Timing (Figure 1, printed p. 12): `L` observes `V`, then chooses either
- **Tender offer**: take-it-or-leave-it cash bid `P` per share, conditional on receiving 1/2 − α shares; no takeover costs; no counter-bid by management; and
- **Merger invitation** ("takeover activism"): `L` negotiates a sale of the *whole firm* to `B` under Nash bargaining — with prob. ρ, `L` posts `P_L`; with prob. 1 − ρ, `B` posts `P_B`. The merger agreement is binding, so free-riding is bypassed.

Equilibrium notion: **Perfect Sequential Equilibrium**, uniqueness bought by three assumptions (printed p. 11): (A1) shareholders tender when `P` weakly exceeds the expected post-takeover share value; (A2) off-equilibrium beliefs satisfy the **credible beliefs criterion** of Grossman & Perry (1986); (A3) **log-concave density** `f` (Bagnoli & Bergstrom 2005). A4 is the market-model parameter restriction `E[Π_B|V*=V₁*] > E[Π_L|V*=V₀*]` (printed p. 24).

The tractability engine: `L`'s merger payoff `α[ρP_L + (1−ρ)P_B]` is **independent of her type**, whereas her tender payoff `αV + (1−α)(V − P)` is increasing in type. Hence one crossing, hence a single cutoff. Log-concavity makes the mean-residual-life function monotone, which delivers uniqueness of the cutoff (proof of Prop. 1, Step 3, printed p. 45).

**Market model (Section 3).** Continuum of ex-ante identical firms of measure 1, i.i.d. `V ~ F[0,1]`. A measure `n` of ex-ante role-less control investors chooses to become `L` (buying the stake **at the current share value of 0** — see the scope note below) or `B`, then is randomly matched to one firm (search friction). Multiple `L`s in a firm: one "lead" is drawn at random and the others sell to her — a modelling assumption that makes `Π_L` independent of the number of `L`s and "substantially simplif[ies] things, and in particular allow[s] analytical solutions" (printed p. 22). Multiple `B`s in a firm ⇒ second-price auction ⇒ zero bidder profit, isomorphic to ρ = 1. Equilibrium = entry measures `(n_L*, n_B*)` with no profitable role deviation (Definition 1, printed p. 23).

**Extensions (Section 4, titled "On the Efficiency Gains from Takeover Activism").** (4.1) Block trade to `B` who then makes his own tender offer — free-riding restored for `B`. (4.2) `B` learns `V` after the block trade — two cutoffs. (4.3) A chain of `n` bidders, each of whom may bid or pass the block along.

**Method:** pure theory. No data, no estimation, no calibration, no simulation. Section 3.4 (actual title: "Disciplinary Ownership Changes: Empirical Patterns") assembles *secondary* empirical trends from other papers and one public data source; it estimates nothing.

*(added by verifier)* Numerical content is confined to **three** items, not two: the Figure 4 example (printed p. 17), the Online-Appendix example (printed p. 57), and one parameter check in footnote 15 — "with V ∼ U[0,1] and α = 0.1, Assumption 4 is satisifed whenever ρ < 0.958851" (printed p. 24). Figures 5–7 carry **no** stated parameterisation and are schematic. Also: the stake α is **exogenous everywhere** — in the market model a control investor "can buy an initial minority stake α ∈ (0, ᾱ)" at "the current share value of 0" (printed p. 21 and its footnote 13) and never chooses its size. There is therefore no stake-accumulation margin in the paper on which a threshold rule could bind.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- Every headline claim is a proposition with a full proof in Appendix A; no result rests on simulation. The honesty ratio is high. *(verifier qualifier: A.1-A.8 cover Lemma 1 and Propositions 1-7 and nothing else. Lemma 1's "proof" is a one-line deferral to Shleifer & Vishny (1986); **Corollary 1 has no proof at all**; Lemma 2's proof sits in the Online Appendix, not Appendix A; and the efficiency decomposition, eqs. (7)-(8), is an in-text derivation with no appendix proof. The count 7 propositions + 2 lemmas + 1 corollary is correct — verified by enumeration.)*
- The mechanism is genuinely new and cleanly stated: an informed party who can choose to be buyer *or* seller faces the **smart-buyer problem** at one end of the type space and the **lemons problem** at the other, and the opposite directions of information rents produce partial separation that neither security design within the smart-buyer problem (Burkart & Lee 2015) nor within the lemons problem (Myers & Majluf 1984) can achieve (printed p. 16).
- Robustness is done properly rather than asserted: Section 4 re-derives the same cutoff `V₁*` when the binding merger agreement is removed (Prop. 4), and Section 4.2 shows what breaks when `B` becomes informed (Prop. 5).
- Section 3.4 openly engages the two rival explanations (takeover defences; "low-cost-low-stakes activism" of Gilson & Gordon 2013) instead of ignoring them.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **The empirical section is narrative, not empirical.** Four trends are matched by eye to figures in other people's papers. There is no test that discriminates their capital-growth mechanism from the takeover-defence story, and they concede the AUM numbers "considerably overstate the growth of the subset of funds that map into the 'control-oriented investors' within our model" (printed p. 33). A referee can ask for one identified test; the paper offers none.
- **The stake is bought at price zero.** Section 3.3 (printed p. 30) concedes that with rational investors and noise traders the entry price would rise with `n` and erode profits, and abstracts from it deliberately. The profit-increasing-in-`n` result — the paper's most striking market-level claim — is therefore not robust to endogenous stake pricing, by their own admission.
- The "lead `L`" device (other `L`s sell to her at no cost) is transparently for tractability, and they say so; Section 3.3 admits dilution would make profits hump-shaped in `n` and that "formalizing them would be rather cumbersome" (printed p. 31).
- Proposition 6 gives existence of a bidder-chain equilibrium, not uniqueness — unlike Prop. 1.
- The Online Appendix is mislabelled: it restarts as "A Online Appendix / A.1 Bidder Heterogenity" (sic, printed p. 56), colliding with Appendix A. There is also a stray forward reference at printed p. 20 pointing to "Appendix A.1" for the heterogeneous-abilities analysis.
- No welfare statement about *shareholders* separate from allocative efficiency; "efficiency" is throughout the fraction of value improvements realised, largely silent on who captures the premium. *(verifier qualifier: there is exactly one distributional statement — Proposition 4's only difference from the baseline is "a redistribution of profits from B to dispersed shareholders", printed p. 38. A sentence, not a welfare analysis.)*
- **(added by verifier.)** The market-level efficiency claim is weaker than the firm-level one. At the firm level control is fully efficient for any alpha > 0 (Corollary 1). At the **market** level, "[w]hile all potential value improvements are realized in Case (a), the market does not attain full efficiency in Case (b)" (printed p. 28) — in Case (b), `n_B* < 1` for all `n`, an artefact of the no-dilution assumption they themselves flag. Anyone citing "BLV get full efficiency" must say *which* level. See Q18.

## 7. Implications for our position

**What they occupy:** object = *mode* of control change and allocative efficiency; margin = none; identification = theory only, with a narrative (non-identified) empirical motivation.

**What this buys us.**
1. **The margin is unoccupied by the current state-of-the-art competitor.** BLV is the most visible recent theory of how the market for corporate control got to its present shape, and it has *no* disclosure rule at all. Neither the threshold margin nor the window margin is touched. Our partition — flagged vs pooled — is orthogonal to their entire construction. This is the cleanest whitespace evidence in the competitor set.
2. **They hand us the liquidity flank in their own words.** Q1 ("Market liquidity plays no role in our analysis") is a competitor's signed statement that the driving variable of our paper is absent from theirs. Q4–Q5 are stronger still: they *name* the Kyle/Kyle-Vila price-formation channel, describe the comparative static it would generate (entry prices rise with the mass of control investors, eroding profits), and set it aside as "orthogonal". Our paper does the thing they deliberately did not do — and, because they characterise the omitted effect precisely, we can position ours as the complement rather than the correction.
3. **They validate the control outcome as the right object.** CONTEXT.md broadened our outcome from takeover premium alone to bidder entry / premium / campaign success. BLV's Proposition 2–3 is precisely a theory of *bidder entry* (`n_B*`) as an equilibrium object, and their Prop. 1 is about who ends up paying the premium. Citing them supports the broadening rather than threatening it.
4. **They pre-empt one of our anchors' rivals.** Their Section 3.4 argument that legal/takeover-defence changes cannot fully explain the shift is useful to us in reverse: if legal change is *not* the whole story for the 1980s→2020s shift, a paper that isolates one dated legal change (the Feb-2024 acceleration) on one clean margin has a stronger claim to identification than a broad "legal change caused the shift" narrative. *(added by verifier: the crispest form of this is the conclusion, printed p. 42 — "This trend is typically attributed to legal changes. But as we show, it emerges endogenously as the capital available to investors who seek to implement control changes in underperforming firms grows" (Q13). Note they concede the point rather than deny it at printed p. 6 (Q19); quote both, or a referee will say we straw-manned them.)*
5. **(added by verifier) They already have endogenous revelation — which makes our rule the marginal contribution, not the mechanism.** Their Prop. 5 and the bidder chain reveal information through *actions* that truncate the dispersed shareholders' posterior (Q17). Our rule does something their machinery cannot: it makes revelation **exogenous, dated, and discontinuous in the stake** — a partition the blockholder does not choose. That is a sharper contribution statement than "they have no disclosure", and it survives a referee who points at Prop. 5.
6. **(added by verifier) The exit margin is a second signed absence.** Footnote 9, printed p. 8: "We abstract here from exit as an intervention mode" (Q14), followed by a citation of the exit/voice literature our four-action model lives in. Together with Q1 this gives us *two* of our three axes declared out of scope by the same competitor, in the same paper. Pair Q1 (liquidity) with Q14 (exit) in the introduction.

**What constrains us.**
- **Do not claim novelty on "informed large shareholder chooses between takeover and brokered sale."** That is their Proposition 1, proved. Our exit/hold/quiet-voice/public-voice machinery must not be sold as first-to-model the buy-side/sell-side choice.
- **Do not claim novelty on "activism displaced hostile tender offers".** Proposition 3 owns that, and Section 3.4 owns the motivating facts.
- **Their premium logic runs opposite to our wedge in the common-value baseline.** In BLV a merger invitation *signals a lower* value improvement, so merger prices and returns are *below* tender-offer prices in the baseline; only with heterogeneous bidder synergies (Online Appendix A.1) does the ranking flip and mergers carry the higher average premium (printed p. 20; Boyson et al. 2017 cited as consistent). If our premium wedge `m₁ − m₀` says an engaged target fetches more, we need to be explicit that our engagement is not their merger invitation, or we will be read as contradicting a proved result.
- **A referee will ask whether our κ is doing what their `n` does.** Both make brokered/engaged outcomes more likely. We must show the disclosure rule (the partition) — not just more capital — is what moves our result, and ideally that liquidity and control-investor mass have separable signatures.
- **Their full-efficiency result raises the bar for our welfare claims.** In BLV, giving the blockholder the sell-side option restores full efficiency *for any α > 0*. Any inefficiency our model generates must come from the partition (disclosure), since the buy/sell choice alone does not generate one in their frictions. *(verifier qualifier: this is the **firm-level** result, Corollary 1. At the market level, full efficiency holds only in Case (a); Case (b) leaves `n_B* < 1` and the market short of full efficiency, printed p. 28 — Q18. Cite the level you mean.)*
- **(added by verifier) There is no stake-size margin in BLV to compare ours against.** α is exogenous throughout, bought at the current share value of 0 (printed pp. 10, 21–22), and the only comparative static is the *sign* of `∂V₁*/∂α < 0` (printed p. 19). If our mechanism runs through the blockholder choosing a stake around a threshold, BLV cannot speak to it — which is whitespace, but also means we cannot borrow their comparative statics as support.
- **(added by verifier) Their premium fact cuts toward us, not against us.** Boyson et al. (2017), quoted at printed p. 21 (Q16), find *larger* premia and returns when an outside bidder rather than the incumbent blockholder takes over. If our wedge `m₁ − m₀` predicts a higher premium on the engaged/brokered path, this is the empirical regularity to cite — but it belongs to Boyson et al., not to BLV, and BLV's own baseline predicts the opposite sign (Q11).

**Bottom line for positioning:** BLV is an *antecedent and a boundary marker*, not a rival for our position. It occupies mode-of-control-change × no-margin × theory-only. We occupy control-outcome × disclosure-rule margin (threshold and window) × liquidity, with an identified empirical leg. Quote Q1 and Q4 in the introduction as the whitespace proof.
