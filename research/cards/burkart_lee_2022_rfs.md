## 1. Question

Which is more profitable, and which more efficient, for a blockholder who wants a widely held firm restructured: buying control (a hostile tender offer) or working for control (an activist campaign)? The literature had compared the two only by *assuming* they differ in the value they create V or the cost they incur C. Burkart and Lee instead hold the restructuring technology fixed across the two modes and let them differ in **one thing only — how control is obtained** — then ask what the dispersed shareholders' free-riding does to each. They then ask a third question: what if an activist uses control not to restructure but to *sell the firm* ("takeover activism")?

## 2. Model / data and method

**Primitives.** A firm with a toehold t < 1/2 held by one investor and a mass 1 − t of shares held by an infinite number of atomistic shareholders with equal, indivisible holdings (p.1874).

- **Restructuring technology, common to both modes.** Effort e ≥ 0 (continuous), value improvement V(e, θ) ≥ 0, private cost C(e), where θ > 0 is the **marginal return to effort** — "firm-specific restructuring potential or investor-specific skill" (p.1874). A controller with stake s solves max_e sV(e,θ) − C(e) (eq. 1, p.1875); write e(s,θ) for the solution and Δ(s,θ) ≡ sV(e(s,θ),θ) − C(e(s,θ)).
- **Assumptions 1–4** (p.1875): V_e > 0, V_ee ≤ 0, V_eθ > 0, V_θ(0,·) = 0; C_e > 0, C_ee > 0; C_e(0) = 0, lim_{e→∞}C_e = ∞, lim_{e→∞}V_e = 0; tV(0,θ) ≥ C(0); lim_{θ→0}V_e(e,θ) = 0.
- **The two free-rider problems (the paper's engine).** *Jensen–Meckling (JM) free-riding*: the controller bears all cost but captures only her share s of the gain, so effort is too low. *Grossman–Hart (GH) free-riding*: dispersed shareholders will not tender below the post-takeover value, so the bidder cannot buy the gain, only pay for it.
- **Tender offer game** (Section 2.2, pp. 1876–1877; follows Burkart, Gromb, and Panunzi 1998 with costly effort in place of costly diversion). Three stages: restricted first-and-final offer of r_b shares at cash price p_b conditional on reaching 50%; atomistic tender decisions; effort. Constraints (3)–(6): free-rider condition p_b ≥ V(e_b, θ); majority requirement r_b ≥ 1/2 − t_b; post-takeover incentive constraint s_bV_e = C_e; s_b = t_b + r_b. Management is assumed opposed but unable/unwilling to counterbid.
- **Activism game** (Section 2.3, pp. 1879–1880). **Reduced-form campaign technology**: a campaign with effort a ≥ 0 succeeds with probability q(a, ψ, s) at private cost K(a), where ψ ≥ 0 is "campaigning efficacy, which may depend on activist skill and institutional factors" (p.1879). Assumptions 5–6: q_a > 0, q_aa ≤ 0, q_ψ > 0, q_aψ > 0, **q_s ≥ 0**, K_a > 0, K_aa > 0; K_a(0) = 0, q(0,·,·) = 0, lim_{ψ→∞}q_a = ∞. The activist keeps her stake t_a; she never buys shares in the published model.
- **Takeover activism game** (Section 3, pp. 1884–1885). The successful activist uses board control to negotiate a merger with a bidder holding t_b ≥ 0; she has all the bargaining power and makes a first-and-final offer (r_m, p_m). **Legal risk ϵ**: with probability ϵ the merger price is revised ex post to the full post-merger share value (following Müller and Panunzi 2004), which restores partial free-riding and reduces the bidder's "effective" stake to ŝ_b ≡ t_b + (1 − ϵ)r_m.
- **Equilibrium notion.** Subgame-perfect equilibrium solved by backward induction, with rational expectations in the tendering stage.

**No market, no trading, no price formation.** There is no market maker, no order flow, no noise trader, no share price process, no toehold accumulation. Prices in the model are *two negotiated/constrained numbers*: the bid price p*_b = V*(1/2, θ) (pinned by the free-rider condition, Lemma 2) and the merger price p_m (pinned by the bidder's participation constraint, Lemma 4). The toehold t is exogenous throughout.

**Data / empirics: none.** There is **no empirical section, no sample, no regression, no coefficient, no standard error**. The paper has Section 1 (equivalence benchmark), Section 2 (takeover or activism), Section 3 (takeover activism), Section 4 (concluding remarks), and an appendix of proofs. *However* — and our prior note that there is "no measurement/empirical-implications section" needs a correction — the paper **does** state a numbered list of four testable patterns (p.1872), an explicit "empirical implications of Proposition 3" paragraph (p.1882), and two subsections (3.1, 3.2) of discussion arguing consistency with published evidence. What it never does is *measure* anything.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths**
- A genuinely clean comparative design: the restructuring technology V, C is held *identical* across modes, so every difference traces to one primitive — how control is obtained. This is the paper's methodological claim and it is honestly executed.
- The **dual free-rider mechanism** (JM ⇄ GH) generates a sharp, sign-opposite comparative static in a single parameter θ, which is unusual and memorable.
- **Takeover activism** as "combining the best of both worlds" is a real theoretical contribution and produces the striking normative inversion: limited, temporary ownership becomes a *strength*, not a shortcoming.
- Predictions are stated crisply and their empirical counterparts are discussed carefully; the authors are explicit about which prediction needs the dual free-rider structure.
- The proofs are complete for everything except Corollary 2 and the model is transparent about what the reduced-form campaign technology does and does not do.

**Weaknesses / open flanks**
- **The toehold t is exogenous.** How the blockholder got to t — the entire pre-disclosure accumulation problem — is outside the model, and the authors say so (p.1891). Nothing in the model prices the toehold.
- **No market, no liquidity, no price formation.** The word "liquidity" never appears in the body of the paper (see §6 below for the exact verification). The bid price is a constraint, not an equilibrium of a trading game.
- **No disclosure rule as a mechanism.** The 5% threshold appears in one footnote as motivation. There is no filing window, no flagged/pooled distinction, no partition of what the market knows.
- **Corollary 2 — the one prediction most directly about institutional change (ψ) — has no printed proof.** A referee could ask for it.
- **The campaign technology q(a, ψ, s) is a reduced form.** Its microfoundations are relegated to the Internet Appendix; the assumption that neither q nor K depends directly on θ is conceded to be "not innocuous" and capable of overturning the result (p.1882).
- **Prop. 3's existence claim is a limit argument plus one numerical figure.** The authors admit sharper conditions need specific functional forms (proof, p.1892).
- **The bidder ends at exactly 50%.** Acknowledged as unimportant for direction (fn. 8, p.1877) but it is the knife-edge that makes the algebra go through.
- The activist's own bargaining power in the merger is assumed to be **complete** (p.1884); alternatives are in the unavailable Internet Appendix.
- Applies only to effort-type governance, not diversion-type; and explicitly not to "friendly strategic mergers" (p.1872).

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**Object.** They study **intervention profits** (bidder profit Π*_b, activist expected profit Π*_a), **social surplus / efficiency**, **campaign success probability q**, and **intervention-mode choice**. They do have a takeover price and hence a premium — but it is a *degenerate* object: by Lemma 2 the premium is exactly the full post-takeover value improvement V*(1/2, θ), forced by the free-rider condition. There is no bargaining over it, no wedge, and no input from anything a trader does.

**Margin of the disclosure rule: none.** The 13D rule is cited once, in footnote 1, as motivation for why free-riding matters, and once in passing ("13-D filing", p.1887). **No threshold margin and no window margin appears in the model, in any proposition, or in any comparative static.** A published-version extension in which activists buy shares after disclosure was *dropped*: "In an earlier version of this paper, we study a model extension in which activists can make post-disclosure share purchases to increase their stake (Burkart and Lee 2019, section 4)" (p.1883).

**Identification.** Theory only. No data, no estimation, no calibration except the illustrative parameter set behind Figure 1.

**Liquidity — what they actually say, precisely.** Our prior note recorded that Burkart & Lee "explicitly say market liquidity plays no role." **That is not literally what the paper says, and the note should be corrected.** A full-text search shows the token "liquid" appears **exactly three times** *(count corrected by verifier from "four … three reference-list entries")*: "liquidate" in the Icahn epigraph (p.1868), and "liquidity" in **two** reference titles — Back, Collin-Dufresne, Fos, Li and Ljungqvist (2018), *Activism, strategic trading, and liquidity* (p.1894), and Maug (1998), *Large shareholders as monitors: Is there a trade-off between liquidity and control?* (p.1896). Verified twice, on the supplied txt and on an independent `pdftotext -layout` pass. There is **no sentence anywhere in the paper asserting that liquidity is irrelevant.** What the paper does — and this is stronger and cleaner for our purposes — is **omit the market entirely and then say so, naming Back et al. (2018) as the paper that owns the omitted problem and leaving the interaction to future work**:

> **"There are three gaps in our analysis. First, we study a post-disclosure choice between different intervention strategies conditional on a toehold, but we do not endogenize the acquisition of the toehold in anonymous, predisclosure markets. This latter problem—in which there is scope for profitable cut-and-run strategies—has been comprehensively studied by Back et al. (2018). We leave an analysis of how predisclosure and post-disclosure decisions interact to future work."** (p.1891)

## 7. Implications for our position

**What of ours this paper already owns.** The *control-outcome* cell under a free-riding friction, with object = intervention profit and efficiency, margin = *none*, identification = *theory*. Specifically: (a) the comparison of activism vs takeover as modes of getting control; (b) the result that activism can be more profitable while being less efficient; (c) takeover activism as a distinct and superior strategy; (d) the prediction that institutional changes easing activism raise total M&A and cut hostile bids (their Cor. 2 — note it is unproved). We must not re-derive any of these as a contribution.

**Where the whitespace is, precisely.**
1. **The joint object.** They have control outcomes but no market; Back et al. have a market but no control outcome. **Neither has both.** Burkart & Lee say this in one sentence at p.1891 and hand the joint problem to future work *by name*. This is the single strongest external warrant our position has, and it should be quoted verbatim in the introduction of draft_v3.
2. **The margin.** Neither paper models the disclosure rule as a rule. Burkart & Lee mention the 5% threshold in a footnote and note that "The free-rider problem is a key issue in the regulatory debate on the level of the disclosure threshold" (p.1869, fn. 1) — i.e. they see the **threshold margin** as economically live and then do not touch it. Back et al. compress the **window margin** into σ²T. A model in which the disclosure rule is a *partition* (flagged vs pooled) and moves a *control outcome* is unoccupied by either.
3. **Identification.** Neither paper has any. A clean estimate around the Feb-2024 acceleration is uncontested ground relative to this competitor pair.

**Where they constrain us.**
- **Our premium wedge must survive free-riding.** Lemma 2 is the standing result: with atomistic, rational shareholders, the takeover price *equals* the full post-takeover value, so the bidder's toehold is her only source of gain. If our m₁ − m₀ wedge is to be non-degenerate, we owe an explicit statement of which of their assumptions we relax (finite/discrete shareholder base? partial tendering? bargaining power? the 50% knife-edge? a bidder-side information friction?) and why that is legitimate rather than convenient. **A referee who knows this paper will go straight here.** The `takeover_game.py` λ = 1 − q(1−γ)ψ appropriability coefficient is exactly the object that has to be defended against Lemma 2.
- **Do not claim "activism vs takeover" or "activists broker takeovers" as our contribution.** Both are theirs, proved.
- **(added by verifier) Two escape routes from Lemma 2 are pre-closed in footnotes on p.1884.** (i) *Freeze-outs*: fn. 12 says that in their setting bidders are **harmed** by having the freeze-out option (Internet Appendix Sec. E). (ii) *Improved merger terms*: fn. 11 says an activist pushing for better deal terms "can be mapped onto our model of regular activism with improved merger terms being the desired value improvement." So neither "the bidder can freeze out" nor "the activist bargains up the premium" is, on its own, a wedge they do not have. Our m₁ − m₀ has to come from somewhere else — the flagged/pooled partition changing what the bidder infers is the candidate, and it should be named as such in one sentence.
- **Do not claim the ψ → M&A prediction.** Their Corollary 2 states it. It is unproved in print, which is a legitimate thing for us to *strengthen* or *condition on a disclosure margin* — but not to claim first.
- **Their efficiency ranking is uncomfortable for a "liquidity helps activism helps welfare" story.** They prove a takeover is *always* socially more efficient than regular activism (R7). If our model says more liquidity produces more (regular) activism and calls that a welfare gain, we are contradicting a proved result in the competitor set unless our control-outcome channel is doing something their model cannot see. State the difference explicitly.
- **Their θ is our productivity, not our κ.** Their comparative statics are all in θ (return to effort) and ψ (campaigning efficacy). Neither is liquidity. There is no overlap in driving variable — which is good news, but it also means we cannot borrow their sign intuitions.
- **Take their honesty as a template.** They name their three gaps in one short paragraph and name the paper that fills the first. Doing the same for our own gaps (in `CONTEXT.md`'s vocabulary: what our partition does not capture) will read well to the same referees.

It says results hold for any bargaining split as long as the takeover activist recoups sunk campaign costs, and it floats — with a pointer to the 2019 working paper, not a proof — the idea that a bidder may hold a *smaller* toehold and "tip off" an activist to build one instead (IA-Q1). The activist's toehold is still an exogenous number, chosen as a coordination device between two parties who already know each other. **There is no trading, no market, no price, no anonymity and no disclosure timing in it.** So it does **not** occupy "blockholder trades before a control contest". What it *does* occupy is *who holds the toehold and why* — which means we must never write "no one has thought about the bidder's interest in the activist's stake". Write instead: "the allocation of the toehold between bidder and activist has been discussed (Burkart & Lee IA §D); how that toehold is *accumulated*, under what disclosure rule, and at what price impact, has not."
2. **They already have a premium wedge — and it is not ours.** IA Proposition 1 (R21) produces a bid price **strictly above** post-takeover value, a "commitment premium" that deters an off-equilibrium freeze-out. Its comparative static runs in **legal risk ε**, and it runs the "wrong" way (more legal risk → smaller premium). This is good for us in two ways: it shows a wedge above Lemma 2's free-rider price *is* obtainable in their frame, so our m₁ − m₀ is not asking for the impossible; and it shows their route to it is a legal-commitment channel with nothing to do with liquidity or disclosure. **Cite R21 when defending the wedge, and say plainly that ours comes from the partition, not from ε.**
3. **The Kyle–Vila hazard, and how to answer it.** IA-Q3 pre-frames our whole channel as "noise traders relax the GH free-rider problem — Kyle and Vila (1991) already made that point". A referee holding this appendix can say the liquidity channel is a known relaxation of an old condition. **The answer is not to dispute it but to route around it:** in Kyle–Vila noise trading relaxes free-riding through *concealment of an unconditional stake*; our object is a **rule-induced partition** — the market's information set jumps at a legal threshold and a legal deadline, and it is the *rule's parameters* (threshold, window), not the *noise variance*, that move the control outcome. If our comparative static is only in κ, IA-Q3 is a serious referee weapon. If the headline static is in the rule's margins with κ as the interaction, it is not. **This is an argument for making the disclosure margin, not liquidity alone, the paper's driving comparative static.**

**Referee risk to pre-empt.** The obvious question is: "Burkart–Lee do control outcomes, Back et al. do liquidity — is your paper just the two stapled together?" The answer must be a mechanism neither has: the **partition** (flagged vs pooled) changing the *bidder's or the market's* inference, and thereby the control outcome — not merely a model containing both a market and a takeover. Write that sentence before anything else.
