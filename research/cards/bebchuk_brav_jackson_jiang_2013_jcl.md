## 1. Question

Should the SEC shorten the ten-day window in Section 13(d) of the Williams Act, within which an investor crossing 5% must publicly disclose? A 2011 rulemaking petition from Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz asked for one day. The petition asserted, without data, that changed trading technology had made pre-disclosure accumulations larger over time, rendering the rule "obsolete". This Article is the **first systematic empirical evidence on those disclosures**, and it is written explicitly as a rebuttal: the authors test the petition's factual premises and find several inconsistent with the data. Secondarily, they argue the **costs** of shortening the window (fewer and smaller outside blocks → fewer activist arrivals → more managerial slack) and raise an interaction the petition ignored — **low-threshold poison pills**.

This is a **policy article that happens to contain original empirics**, not a research paper. The distinction matters for how we cite it.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Primary sample.** All Schedule 13D filings by **activist hedge funds, 1994–2007** — **2,040 filings** (p. 33; Table 1, p. 9). Built by taking every 13D filer in the period and filtering by **Item 2** (filer name, principal business, address) plus internet and news searches to identify hedge-fund managers; then excluding, on **Item 4** (purpose of the transaction), filings whose purpose is bankruptcy/reorganisation, merger-arbitrage risk arbitrage, or closed-end funds (pp. 7–8, p. 33). This is the **Brav–Jiang–Partnoy–Thomas (2008, JF) dataset**, extended to 1994–2007 by Brav–Jiang–Kim. **42 events with sub-5% activist stakes are dropped** from the 13D analysis, leaving "about 2,000" filings (p. 9).

**Which Schedule 13D items they actually use — important.** Item 2 (identity), Item 4 (purpose), the reported **percentage ownership**, the **date of crossing 5%**, and the **filing date**. **They do NOT use Item 5's trade tables.** The string "Item 5" does not appear in the article. Everything about *what was bought inside the window* is inferred from **market-wide abnormal turnover**, not from the filer's own reported transactions. (Our internal notes' guess that the source was "Item 5 trade tables" is **wrong for this paper** — that is Collin-Dufresne & Fos, which BBJJ cite at fn. 38, p. 23 and fn. 47, p. 27.)

**Constructed variables** (pp. 34, and fn. 63 on p. 34): hostility dummy and purpose/tactic dummies coded from Item 4; market cap relative to the median public company at the prior year-end; number of covering analysts (First Call); **relative liquidity, proxied by the Amihud (2002) measure, from daily trading data**; size-adjusted stock return over the year before filing; and market-adjusted buy-and-hold returns over the twenty days before and after. **(added by verifier) Read that last one carefully: the appendix (p. 34) defines it as the excess buy-and-hold return "during the twenty-day period before, and twenty-day period after, **the Schedule 13D filing**" — i.e. around the *filing*. But Figure 3 and the ~6% run-up (R10, p. 20) are measured around the *crossing of 5%*.** The article never says whether these are the same series or two different ones, and it uses "short-run abnormal return" for the regressor in Tables A1–A3 without re-defining it. For a window-margin design the crossing date and the filing date are exactly the two dates whose distance is the object of study, so this is not a pedantic distinction: **do not treat R10 and the "short-run abnormal return" regressor as the same object.**

**Secondary samples.**
- *All* 13D filings 1994–2007: **33,440** filings, of which **9.5%** are by activist hedge funds (Table 7, p. 22).
- A **random draw of 13D filings from June 2011** — **174 filings (20 activist hedge funds, 154 non-activists)** — hand-collected for days-to-file and stake (Table 8 and fn. 38, p. 23; Table 9, p. 24).
- **805 US public companies with a poison pill in place** (FactSet SharkRepellent, accessed April 2012) for pill trigger thresholds (Table 10, p. 29).

**Designs / estimators.** Descriptive tables and two figures, plus multivariate models with **N = 1,398** throughout: **ordered logit** and **two-sided tobit** for days-to-disclose (Table A1, p. 36); OLS-type models for stake at filing (Table A2, p. 37); the same with a year trend and a last-five-years dummy for the time-trend test (Table A3, pp. 38–39). Year fixed effects in A1 and A2. **Tables report coefficients with a bracketed statistic and stars; the text calls them "coefficients, and standard errors" (p. 23) while the sentence introducing Table 5 (p. 17) promises "the estimated correlation coefficients, z-statistics, and significance"** — the bracketed numbers behave like t/z-statistics, and this labelling is inconsistent across the article. **No clustering is stated anywhere.** The significance legend is unusual and itself garbled: fn. 34 (p. 17) defines `"****"` as 99%, `"**"` as 95%, `"*"` as 90% — with no three-star level defined, yet `***` appears in Tables 9 and A2.

**Turnover measure (Figure 4).** Normal turnover for each company is the average over a **200-day window ending ten days before the crossing date**; abnormal turnover is the ratio of actual to normal (fn. 40, p. 25).

**Identification.** **None, and the paper does not claim any.** There is no shock, no control group, no rule change to exploit — the article was written *before* any change, to argue against one. Everything is descriptive or a conditional correlation.

**The sign-convention caveat on R14 (read this before citing it).** The variable is labelled "Amihud Liquidity Measure" in every table, and fn. 63 (p. 34) says "We use the Amihud measure as a proxy for market liquidity." Amihud (2002) is an **illiquidity** ratio (|return|/dollar volume) — higher means *less* liquid. The article **never states which orientation it used, never signs the variable, and never discusses this coefficient in the body text at all**; it appears only in the appendix tables. Under the literal reading (higher Amihud = more illiquid), the result is *illiquid stocks → larger disclosed stakes*, which cuts against the naive Kyle/Maug intuition that liquidity makes accumulation cheap. Under the label's reading (higher value = more liquid), it is the opposite. **We cannot resolve this from the document.** Treat it as a lead worth chasing in a replication, not a fact to cite. The citation in fn. 63 is also **misprinted twice** — it gives "31 J. FIN. MKTS. 31 (2002)" for what is *Journal of Financial Markets* vol. **5**, 31–56, *(and, added by verifier, it renders the title as "Cross-Section and Time-Series **Events**" where Amihud wrote "**Effects**")*. Two errors in a four-element citation is itself a signal about how much care the appendix received.

## 4. Institutional facts used

These are the anchor facts. Cite them from here.

- **Section 13(d) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (the Williams Act):** a beneficial owner of **more than 5% of any class** of publicly traded securities must disclose **within 10 days of crossing the 5% threshold** (p. 33; p. 1). This is the **pre-2024** regime — exactly the state of the world our window-margin design treats as "before".
- **The 10 days are calendar days, not business days.** BBJJ cite the SEC's Compliance and Disclosure Interpretation, Question 103.10, for this; they note that filers appear to count *business* days, and that this alone moves the late-filing rate from **>20% (calendar) to >10% (business)** (fn. 21, p. 10). **This is a first-order fact for any window-margin empirical design** — the day-counting convention is worth roughly ten percentage points of the "violation" rate all by itself, and the 2024 rule is written in **business** days.
- **The petition asked for one day**; other jurisdictions cited by the petition run **two to five days** — UK, Australia, Canada, Hong Kong (p. 25 and fnn. 42–45, p. 27). BBJJ's counterfactual illustration uses **four days**. *(Verifier: the article states the foreign range twice and inconsistently — "between two to five days" on p. 25, "from two days to four days" on p. 27. Use "two to five" and cite p. 25, or cite the underlying rules directly.)*
- **(added by verifier) The cross-country comparison is confounded, and BBJJ say so: **pp. 30–31** (the sentence breaks across the page) — "no other developed economy [/] grants corporate insiders the freedom to cap the ownership of blockholders they disfavor through the use of low-trigger poison pills, as is now permitted in the United States."** Anyone comparing the US ten-day window to the UK's two days is comparing two different *bundles* — window plus takeover-defence law. This is directly load-bearing if our design ever reaches for a cross-country comparison or a foreign placebo, and it is an argument BBJJ have already made for us.
- **Schedule 13D structure:** Item 2 = filer identity; Item 4 = purpose of the transaction, including plans for further acquisitions or a merger (fnn. 17–18, pp. 7–8).
- **The rulemaking history:** Wachtell Lipton petition to the SEC, **7 March 2011** (petn. 4-624); Chairman Mary Schapiro's December 2011 remarks; the SEC's 2013 Unified Agenda entry naming a concept release to "modernize the beneficial ownership reporting requirements ... [including] shortening the filing deadlines" (fnn. 1–3, p. 1). **This is the paper trail whose endpoint is the 2024-02-05 acceleration** our design uses. BBJJ is the academic intervention in the middle of it.
- **Poison-pill thresholds**, FactSet SharkRepellent, 805 companies, accessed April 2012 (Table 10 and fn. 55, p. 29).
- **Not present:** no 13G, no tender-offer rules, no takeover-premium object, no 2024 rule (impossible — this is 2013).

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths**
- **It owns the descriptive facts.** Nobody had put the days-to-file distribution on the record before. R1, R3, R4 and R11 are the citations everyone uses, and they are still the standard reference for what the pre-2024 window looked like from the inside.
- **The 33,440-filing denominator (R11) is genuinely important and under-used.** The activist-hedge-fund literature almost always studies the 9.5%. If our design uses *all* 13D filings, this table is the justification; if it uses only activist filings, this table is the external-validity caveat we must address before a referee does.
- The **June 2011 non-activist hand-collection** (R12) is small but is the only evidence anywhere that non-activists behave the same way in the window.
- The authors are the same team that built the canonical activism dataset (Brav–Jiang–Partnoy–Thomas 2008), so the sample construction is the one the field already accepts.
- They are **candid about scope** (see §6) and about the fact that this is a first step, not a settled analysis.

**Weaknesses / open flanks**
- **It is an advocacy document.** Written to rebut a specific petition by a specific law firm, with a named adversary quoted throughout. Every result is framed as "the Petition's premise is wrong". A referee will discount the *interpretive* claims accordingly — not the descriptive tables, which are fine.
- **R9 — the headline policy inference — is not estimated.** "Shortening to four days would not cut accumulations by 60%" is an inference from a turnover figure. No counterfactual, no elasticity, no bound. **This is the single largest open flank, and it is precisely the flank a window-margin paper can close.**
- **R8 measures market-wide abnormal turnover, not the activist's own purchases.** Total turnover on the crossing day being 400% of normal is consistent with the activist buying a block that day *and* with liquidity-motivated selling by others — indeed BBJJ themselves float the block-seller explanation (p. 27). The step from "abnormal turnover on day 0" to "the activist's purchases are concentrated on day 0" is **an assumption, not a measurement**, and Item 5 data (which they did not use) would test it.
- **No prices anywhere — in the *data*.** The article never reports what activists paid inside the window, and "price" appears in no table. So the "at what prices" half of our question **cannot be answered from this paper**. *(Added by verifier: but it is answered rhetorically, and against us, on **p. 30** — see §6. Do not write "BBJJ say nothing about prices"; write "BBJJ report no prices".)*
- **Figures carry the two most-quoted magnitudes** (the ~20% day-10 mass, the ~6% run-up, the 400% turnover) **with no tables, no standard errors, and no counts**. In a working draft with image-only figures, these are read-offs. Downgrade accordingly.
- **Statistical reporting is loose.** Bracketed statistics are called "standard errors" in one place and "z-statistics" in another; the star legend (fn. 34) defines `****`/`**`/`*` but tables print `***`; no clustering is stated; no clear exclusion of overlapping events by the same fund. A finance referee would send this back; a law review did not.
- **Two internal inconsistencies in this draft.** The abstract says the median stake "has remained stable throughout the **17-year** period that we study" while the body says **fourteen-year** and the sample is 1994–2007 (PDF p. 2 vs. pp. 3, 6, 9). And Table 4's median stake is **6.3%** (1994–2007 activists) while Table 8's is **9%** (June 2011 activists) — different samples, but the article never reconciles them, and fn. 38 (p. 23) has to reach for Collin-Dufresne & Fos's 6.2% to explain the gap.
- **R5/R6 (delay does not buy stake) is the paper's most load-bearing empirical claim and it is a null.** It is a null on a selected sample — we only see stakes of investors who *did* file. Anyone who accumulated and then decided not to cross, or crossed and settled for less, is unobserved. *(Corrected by verifier: the authors raise the **adjacent** censoring problem and not this one. **fn. 19, p. 8** notes that "relying exclusively on Schedule 13D filings might exclude cases in which outside investors maintained significant holdings of stock", which is why the news searches pulled in the 42 sub-5% activist events in the first place — events they then **drop** from every 13D analysis. So they saw that 13D-only sampling truncates from below; they never raise that the delay→stake null is estimated conditional on having filed. The criticism stands, but it must be made precisely, or a referee who knows fn. 19 will say we misread them.)*

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

They say it themselves, and unusually plainly:

> "our analysis is limited to only a few of the empirical questions that an adequate assessment of the rules governing blockholders' acquisitions of public-company stock should consider" (p. 5)

and they explicitly hand off the day-0 turnover puzzle: **"We hope that this question will be addressed by future research."** (p. 28).

In our three coordinates:

- **Object.** They occupy: **days-to-file**, **stake disclosed at filing**, **abnormal turnover around crossing**, **abnormal return around crossing**, **late-filing incidence**, **activist share of all 13D filings**, and **poison-pill trigger thresholds**. They **estimate** no **control outcome**: no takeover premium, no bidder entry, no deal completion, no campaign success, no proxy-contest outcome, no target-firm value beyond the ±20-day return around crossing. The word "premium" never appears anywhere in the article (verified by full-text search), nor does "tender offer"; "takeover" occurs twice, once in a citation. Our "control outcome" object is **empirically entirely open** relative to them.

  **(added by verifier) But they *argue* two control-outcome channels in words, and both cut against a naive premium design. Know these before a referee quotes them at us.**
  1. **p. 28 — faster disclosure speeds up the *defences*:** "tightening the disclosure requirements under Section 13(d) will not only alert the market to the investor's presence but also incumbent directors and executives—who can then put takeover defenses in place more quickly in response to the news that a significant shareholder has emerged."
  2. **p. 30 — faster disclosure need not raise the price selling shareholders get:** on the pill scenario, "the company's response to the immediate disclosure would not enable public investors to capture higher prices for shares they sell to the large shareholder; to the contrary, it would prevent these investors from selling shares to the outside shareholder at mutually beneficial prices."

  Neither is estimated, and both are stated conditional on a low-trigger pill being adopted — so they are **conjectures with a named moderator**, not results. Two consequences for us. (i) If our window-margin estimate finds control outcomes *fall* after the 2024 acceleration, BBJJ predicted the sign in prose and we are confirming them with identification, not surprising them. (ii) Their moderator is a **testable interaction we can run**: split the post-2024 sample by whether the target has a low-trigger pill. That turns their rhetorical hedge into our heterogeneity result, which is the most efficient thing this article gives us.
- **Margin of the disclosure rule.** The **window margin**, and *only* the window margin — and they occupy it **normatively, not causally**. The threshold margin (5% vs. 3%) is taken as given and never varied. This is the one place BBJJ genuinely sit on our ground, so the boundary has to be drawn precisely: **they describe behaviour under a fixed window and argue about a hypothetical change; they do not estimate the effect of a window change on anything.**
- **Identification.** None. No shock, no DiD, no control group, no event study of a rule change, no structural model. The counterfactual in R9 is arithmetic prose. **This is the whitespace.**
- Also not done: **no theory**; **no Item 5 trade data**; **no reported prices**; **no 13G**; **no post-2007 activist sample** (the only post-2007 data is 174 hand-collected June 2011 filings); **no analysis of who crosses 5% and who stops short**; **no clustering** (the string does not occur in the article).
- **(added by verifier) The scope quote continues, and the continuation is the better half.** Q18 stops one clause early. The full sentence at p. 5 goes on: "Any such assessment should include analysis of the benefits conferred on shareholders by outside blockholders as well as **the effects of existing disclosure rules and state law on the incidence and size of block holdings in public companies.**" That is BBJJ naming our research question — the effect of the disclosure rule on blocks — as the thing their article does not do. It is a stronger licence quote than Q17, because Q17 hands off only the day-0 turnover puzzle whereas this hands off the rule-effect question itself. **Use this as the §6 boundary quote in draft_v3.**

## 7. Implications for our position

**What it buys us — it is the "before" panel of our window-margin design.** Our position rests on the Feb-2024 acceleration (10 → 5 business days). BBJJ is the published, peer-cited description of **how the ten-day window was actually used, on 2,040 activist filings and 33,440 total filings**. Four facts are directly load-bearing:

1. **The window bound.** 45.3% of activist filings land in the 8–10 day bucket and ~20% on day 10 itself (R1, R2). A 10 → 5 day cut is therefore **not a marginal nudge**: it moves the deadline past where the modal filer sits. That is the source of statistical power for a window-margin design, and BBJJ supply the number that makes the power calculation credible.
2. **The margin has a floor.** R5/R6 say the extra days do **not** buy a bigger stake, and R8 says the buying is concentrated on day 0. If both hold, the *stake* margin should barely move when the window shrinks. **This is the sharpest testable prediction anyone has left on the table, and BBJJ stated it without being able to test it.** Our design can. If the 2024 acceleration *did* compress stakes, BBJJ's central inference is refuted with the very shock they were arguing about — that is a paper. If it did not, we confirm a strong prior with clean identification — that is also a paper, and a safer one.
3. **The denominator.** 9.5% of 13D filings are activist (R11). Whatever our sample is, this fixes the external-validity conversation up front.
4. **The day-counting trap.** Calendar vs. business days moves the measured "late filing" rate from >20% to >10% (fn. 21, p. 10). **Our parser must get this right**, and it belongs on the referee checklist's "parser validation" line. This is the most operationally useful footnote in the paper.

**Where it constrains us.** BBJJ *own the window margin descriptively*, so we cannot claim to be the first to look at the ten-day window. The claim has to be **identification**: they described the window under a fixed rule and argued about changing it; we estimate what happened when it changed. Say it in exactly those terms and the overlap becomes a strength — we are answering the question they posed and could not answer.

**How it interacts with our model.** Two hooks worth building toward:

- The **liquidity coefficient (R14)** is the only place in this literature where 13D stake size is regressed on an Amihud measure, and it is buried in an appendix with an unstated sign convention. Our CONTEXT.md defines empirical liquidity as inverted Amihud. If we replicate this cleanly with a stated orientation, we get either (a) a stylised fact our κ mechanism must match, or (b) a correction to a widely cited paper. Either is cheap and worth doing early. **Do not cite R14's sign without resolving the convention.**
- The **block-seller conjecture** (p. 27) — activists cross 5% only when a large seller appears, and "then choose to provide liquidity to this seller" — is a *liquidity-timing* story for the crossing decision that our model could microfound. It is exactly the kind of loose end that becomes a modelling contribution.

**The whitespace, stated for the positioning memo.** BBJJ occupies (window-use behaviour + stake at filing) × (**window margin**) × (**descriptive, no identification**). Massa–Xu occupies (takeover premium) × (**no disclosure margin**) × (cross-sectional association). Our position — **control outcome × window margin × Feb-2024 DiD** — takes the *object* from Massa–Xu, the *margin* from BBJJ, and the *identification* from neither. The referee sentence: *Bebchuk et al. (2013) documented how activists used the ten-day window and argued that shortening it would change little; Massa and Xu (2013) showed target liquidity is priced in control transactions. Neither could observe a window change. We do.*
