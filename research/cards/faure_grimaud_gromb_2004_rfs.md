## 1. Question
Does public trading of a firm's stock strengthen or weaken a large shareholder's incentive to make the firm more valuable? The premise is that trading produces a price that is informative *about the insider's own activity*, not just about exogenous shocks. If the insider may have to sell before his effort is publicly visible, a more informative price pays him for that effort — so public trading is an incentive device. From this the paper derives implications for the go-public decision, capital structure and security design. It is written directly against the Coffee (1991) / Bhidé (1993) claim that liquidity, by easing exit, kills monitoring incentives.

## 2. Model / data and method
Four dates (t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4), no discounting, all agents risk neutral. No data — pure theory.

- **Ownership.** An all-equity firm: fraction α held by dispersed outsiders, block (1 − α) held by the insider. α is chosen once and for all at t = 0 ("Founding Fathers" approach, p. 997) to maximise initial firm value.
- **Effort (t = 1).** The insider exerts unobservable effort *e* at private cost c(e) = e²/2. Firm value is V^H with probability (1 + e)/2 and V^L otherwise; ΔV ≡ V^H − V^L > 0. Expected value V̂(e) = V^L + ((1+e)/2)ΔV; first-best effort is ΔV/2. **Note (footnote 2, p. 989): higher effort raises the mean *and lowers the variance* of value — this assumption is what makes price informativeness fall in effort (Lemma 1(ii)).**
- **Trading (t = 2).** V realises but is not public until t = 4. A simplified Kyle (1985) market exists only if α > 0. Liquidity demand d_L ∈ {−d, 0}, each with probability ½, with d = d(α), d(0) = 0, d(α) > 0 for α > 0, d continuous, d′(0) > 0, d′(α) > 0. **d(α) — the volatility of liquidity trade — is this paper's noise-trading intensity, the closest object to our κ.** A single speculator observes a publicly known information cost k (drawn from c.d.f. F on (0, ∞)) and, if he pays it, learns V exactly. A competitive market maker sees the orders {d_L, d_S} but not who sent which, and posts P₂ = E[V | {d_L, d_S}].
- **Price informativeness.** With probability ½ the informed speculator's order is *not* separated from the liquidity order, and P₂ = V̂(e^a); with probability ½ the orders coincide, the market maker reads them, and P₂ reveals V. Speculator profit is π(e^a) = ½ · ((1 − e^a)/2) · d · [V̂(e^a) − V^L], i.e. equal to d·Δ_V·(1 − (e^a)²)/8 (Eq. 4, p. 993). **Price informativeness is p(e^a) = ½·F(π(e^a))** — *(corrected by verifier; the card previously dropped the ½.* Eq. 5 on p. 993 reads p(e^a) = ½F(π(e^a)) = ½F( ((1−(e^a)²)/8)·d·Δ_V ), confirmed by rendering the page as an image. F(π) is the probability the speculator pays k and becomes informed; the ½ is the probability his order then separates, so p is the probability the *price* is informative, which is what enters the insider's payoff in Eq. 7.*) Definition 1 (p. 993): "an increase in the price informativeness function" means an upward shift of p(·) for given α and Δ_V — generated by a larger d(α) or an FOSD shift of F toward lower k.
- **Exit (t = 3).** With probability λ the insider is hit by a liquidity shock and must sell his whole stake; otherwise he may but need not. **Two assumptions do the heavy lifting: (i) the insider cannot trade anonymously — his trade is always observable; (ii) in the baseline the liquidity shock itself is publicly observed.** Buyers have only public information, so the stake sells at P₃ = P₂. Partial sales are said not to change the results (Faure-Grimaud & Gromb 2000). **The insider's action set is sell-all-or-retain — he has no buy action and cannot accumulate.** *(added by verifier: the four-action space we need — exit / hold / quiet buy / flagged buy — is not a restriction FGG relax anywhere; Appendix I adds only a partial *sale* as a signal.)*
- **Observability is asserted four times, not two.** pp. 988, 989, 990 *and* p. 991: "We assume throughout that the insider cannot trade anonymously. This is to focus on our main effect, and to emphasize the contrast with some of the literature on the liquidity-control trade-off. **Our model could accommodate some (limited) secret trading.**" That last clause is an unsupported aside — no version of the model with secret trading is presented — but it is the sentence a referee will quote back at us. *(added by verifier)*
- **Insider's incentive.** e(p^a) = (1 − α)[1 − λ(1 − p^a)]·ΔV/2 (Eq. 7, p. 994). Two channels: the **direct** effect of the stake (1 − α) and the **indirect** effect of price informativeness p^a. Equilibrium is the fixed point p = p(e), e = e(p) — p(·) decreasing in e, e(·) increasing in p.
- **IPO pricing.** P₀(α) = V̂(e*(α)) − (1/α)·π(e*(α))F(π(e*(α))) (Eq. 9, p. 998): outsiders are compensated ex ante for their expected trading losses to the speculator (Holmström–Tirole style).
- **§3 extension — exit as a signal.** Assumption 1: the insider observes V at t = 2. Assumption 2: no liquidity shocks; instead an outside project with return (1 + r), r drawn from a known distribution, with the sale decision and r publicly observed. The insider sells iff r > (V^s − P₃)/P₃ (Eq. 10, p. 1003). Multiple PBE arise; **the authors select the pooling equilibrium, "somewhat arbitrarily", as the one in which exit is most likely** (p. 1004).
- **Appendices.** A–E and G–I prove Propositions 1–6 and Corollaries 2–3. Appendix F specialises the model (discrete effort e ∈ {0, e_H}, k uniform on [0, K], d(α) = d·α) to derive a closed-form optimal float (Lemma 4, p. 1010). *(added by verifier: Appendix F is also the only place the noise trade is **microfounded** — a unit mass of consumers, an equiprobable fraction 0 or d of whom must consume early and liquidate, so that d(α) = d·α makes the float itself the noise-trading intensity. That is the tightest analogue in this paper to our κ, and it exists only inside the specialised model. Corollary 1 has no appendix; Corollary 4 has no appendix of its own either — see §3.)*
- **Equilibrium notion.** Nash / perfect Bayesian (the signalling section uses the Cho–Kreps intuitive criterion, Appendix I, p. 1012).

## 4. Institutional facts used
Essentially none. This is theory with no institutional anchor, no data source, no sample, no period, no jurisdiction.
- Disclosure *rules* appear exactly twice, both as caveats and neither in the model. Footnote 8 (p. 993) concedes that stricter disclosure rules would cut the other way on the information-cost interpretation of a "developed" market. §2.1 (p. 996) explicitly disclaims disclosure requirements as the source of the paper's disciplinary effect. *(verified by grep: "disclos*" has exactly four hits in the whole paper — these two, one in the reference list, and the Fishman–Hagerty sentence below. "13D", "Williams", "window", "premium", "tender", "bidder", "proxy" and "campaign" have **zero** hits in the body.)*
- *(added by verifier)* A **third** disclosure mention, on p. 988, is the one the card should keep in view: FGG cite Fishman & Hagerty (1989) — "Absent incentive issues, they show that a firm may want to increase stock price efficiency by disclosing information." This is the paper's only acknowledgement that *disclosure* and *trading* are alternative routes to an informative price, and FGG's answer is that in their model informativeness has to be bought with float, which carries an incentive cost. It is the nearest thing in FGG to a disclosure-versus-liquidity trade-off, and it is one sentence long.
- Institutional colour is limited to illustrative citation: Brennan & Franks (1997) on UK IPO underpricing and dispersed ownership (footnote 12, p. 997); Lerner (1994) on venture capitalists not liquidating at the IPO; Jegadeesh, Weinstein & Welch (1990) and Welch (1996) on split issues (p. 999). None of these is used as an identifying fact — they are consistency checks quoted from other people's work.
- No stake threshold, no filing window, no 13D, no Williams Act, no dated rule change anywhere in the paper.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses
**Strengths:**
- The central mechanism is a genuine two-way fixed point (p depends on e, e depends on p) and both directions are established as lemmas before the equilibrium proposition — the comparative static in Prop. 1 is a fixed-point shift argument, not a partial derivative.
- Prop. 5 is the honest version of the liquidity–control debate: they show the unconditional exit probability is *ambiguous* rather than claiming exit falls, and still get effort up. That is a harder and more credible result than the alternative.
- Appendix F pays the price of a closed form explicitly (specialised functional forms) instead of asserting the optimal float.
- The security-design section is clearly labelled as a sketch rather than dressed up as a theorem.

**Weaknesses / open flanks:**
- **The insider cannot trade anonymously — by assumption, and the assumption is load-bearing.** Everything that Kyle–Vila / Maug / Kahn–Winton get from stealth accumulation is switched off here, and FGG name those four papers (plus Noe 2002) as the literature they are opting out of (p. 988). The paper restates the assumption **four** times (pp. 988, 989, 990, 991) and never checks how far the results survive relaxing it — *but* p. 991 does assert, in one clause and with no supporting analysis, that "Our model could accommodate some (limited) secret trading." *(corrected by verifier: the card previously said "twice" and did not record the accommodation clause, which is the obvious referee comeback.)*
- **The insider has no buy action.** He sells his whole stake or retains it (Appendix I adds a partial sale as a signal). Accumulation, and therefore any stake-threshold question, is outside the model. *(added by verifier)*
- Two binary states (V^H, V^L) and a two-point liquidity demand ({−d, 0}); price informativeness is a scalar probability, so "how informative" has no continuous grading. In the baseline the price is either fully revealing or fully uninformative.
- Footnote 2 (p. 989) concedes that the mean/variance link in the effort technology is a restriction; Lemma 1(ii) rests on it.
- §3's multiple equilibria are resolved by selecting the exit-maximal (pooling) one "somewhat arbitrarily" (p. 1004). The exit-probability results in Cor. 4 and Prop. 5(i) are therefore selection-dependent.
- The paper cannot derive an optimal float in the general model (footnote 14, p. 998); it needs Appendix F's special case.
- P₃ = P₂ by assumption — the buyers of the block have only public information. The authors give four reasons (pp. 991–992), none of them a model.
- The comparative statics are in a *function shift* (an upward shift of p(·)), not in a single primitive that maps to a measurable market variable. Taking it to data would require deciding whether d(α) or F is what moved.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)
**OBJECT.** The object is the insider's **effort / value-increasing activity**, and derived from it: initial firm value, the go-public decision, the optimal float, capital structure and security design, and the exit probability. It is **not** a control outcome. No takeover, bidder, tender offer, premium or campaign appears in the model. Control transfers are explicitly excluded: "Our model does not deal with control transfers per se, although it can be applied to such events." (p. 999). The market for corporate control is named and disclaimed as a channel: "In our model, this disciplinary effect is not related to the firm facing more stringent disclosure requirements or being on the market for corporate control as a result of going public. Of course, these effects might complement ours." (p. 996). The conclusion leaves the post-exit fate of the block open: "Finally, we have not considered the firm’s fate following the insider’s exit. When this is taken into account, whether exit results in the dispersion of the insider’s block or in its transfer to a new large shareholder may become relevant." (p. 1006).

*(added by verifier — two places where control does brush the model, both worth knowing before we claim the axis is untouched.)* First, the sentence immediately before the Q2 disclaimer sets FGG against a **control-transfer** theory of going public: in Zingales (1995), and Burkart–Gromb–Panunzi (1999), "going public allows initial owner to use the free-rider behavior of dispersed shareholders as a bargaining tool to extract a greater surplus in future sales of corporate control in an imperfectly competitive market" (p. 999). FGG's reply is that they assume a *competitive* sale market, "and so there is no surplus to be extracted. The dispersion of shares yields a more accurate rather than a higher price." That is a direct, explicit renunciation of a *premium* — which is our object — and it is the single most quotable line in the paper for our whitespace claim. Second, one of the paper's own readings of the liquidity shock **is** a control transfer: "an entrepreneur who has to sell out when retiring or transferring control to a more effective party (e.g., a larger firm that will better develop and market the firm’s product)" (p. 991). So a control transfer sits in the model as an exogenous shock λ, never as an outcome.

**MARGIN of a disclosure rule: none.** There is no disclosure rule in the model — no stake threshold, no filing window, no flagged/pooled partition. What the model does have is the *opposite* of a disclosure rule as a modelling primitive: the insider's trade is **always** observable by assumption, so there is nothing for a rule to reveal. "We rule out secret trading by assuming that the insider’s trade is always observable." (p. 990); "We assume that the insider cannot trade anonymously." (p. 989); "To focus on our main point, we assume instead that the insider cannot trade secretly." (p. 988). Disclosure regulation is mentioned once, in footnote 8 (p. 993), only as a caveat on interpreting the information-cost distribution F, and the authors explicitly assume its effect does not dominate: "For instance, more developed markets may have stricter disclosure rules, making private information more scarce and the costs of acquiring it greater rather than lower."

**IDENTIFICATION: theory only.** There is no data, no sample, no estimate anywhere in the paper. Empirical work is cited (Brennan & Franks 1997; Lerner 1994; Welch 1996) as consistency evidence for implications, never as a test the authors run.

**Explicitly left for future work (their words, §4 Conclusion, pp. 1005–1006):** multi-project effort allocation and the resulting myopia / managerial conservatism; a foundation for the assumption that the market mechanism generates unique information; alternative ways of generating information; the insider acting on λ and r themselves — "Also, we have not considered alternative ways in which the insider can deal with liquidity shocks."; and the post-exit fate of the block (quoted above).

## 7. Implications for our position
**What they occupy.** OBJECT: insider effort / firm value, plus go-public, float, capital structure and security design; exit probability as a by-product. MARGIN: none — no disclosure rule exists in the model, and trade observability is imposed by assumption rather than by a rule. IDENTIFICATION: theory only.

**What this leaves open for us.** Our control outcome (bidder entry, takeover premium, campaign success), both margins of the disclosure rule (threshold and window), and any identification are all untouched. The position sits in whitespace against this paper on all three axes.

**Where they constrain us.**
1. **They own the "liquidity helps incentives" direction — with a *proved* comparative static.** This is stronger than Admati–Pfleiderer's transaction-cost assertion. Prop. 1(i) (PROVED) says equilibrium effort rises with the price-informativeness function, and Prop. 5(ii) (PROVED) says effort rises even when exit becomes more likely. Any claim we make about liquidity helping engagement must cite FGG and say what is new. What is new for us: their liquidity works through *speculator learning about the insider's own effort*; ours works through *how much stake can be accumulated inside the pooled state before the flag*. Different channel, different object.
2. **Their d(α) is a noise-trading intensity, and it moves the right way — but it moves informativeness, not the partition.** d(α) enters only through π and hence through F(π): more noise trade ⇒ more speculator profit ⇒ higher probability the speculator is informed ⇒ more informative price. In our model κ instead governs how much of the blockholder's own order is hidden. A referee will ask us to distinguish these; the answer is that FGG's insider **cannot** hide (Q4, Q5), so their κ-analogue can only affect a third party's information production.
3. **Their sign on λ is the opposite of a naive reading.** Effort *falls* in λ (Prop. 1(iii)) but the *optimal float* and the value of going public *rise* in λ (Prop. 3, Lemma 4). If we make claims about how a more exit-prone blockholder behaves, we must keep these two apart: worse incentives conditional on ownership, but stronger reason to create the informative market in the first place.
4. **A precedent for an ambiguous comparative static.** Prop. 5(i) — the unconditional exit probability can move either way because of the feedback through effort — is a PROVED non-monotonicity from a two-way fixed point. This is a useful defence of our own non-monotone results in structure, but note theirs is proved and ours (the hump) is grid-certified only. Do not claim parity of label.
5. **Their assumption is our object.** FGG assume the insider's trade is always observable; we make *when* it becomes observable the choice variable (the window margin) and *whether* it does (the threshold margin). Their assumption sentences (Q4, Q5, Q6) are the cleanest possible evidence that the partition is unoccupied ground in this literature. **Caveat added by verifier:** p. 991 also says "Our model could accommodate some (limited) secret trading" (Q13). We must not write that FGG *cannot* accommodate hidden trade — only that they never do it, show nothing about it, and build their entire contrast with Kyle–Vila / Kahn–Winton / Maug / Noe on switching it off (Q14).
6. **They renounce the premium explicitly.** p. 999 sets FGG against Zingales (1995) and Burkart–Gromb–Panunzi (1999), where dispersion is a bargaining tool for extracting surplus in a future control sale: FGG assume a competitive sale market, "so there is no surplus to be extracted. The dispersion of shares yields a more accurate rather than a higher price" (Q15). Higher price versus more accurate price is exactly our axis; this sentence should be cited whenever we claim the control-premium object is free. *(added by verifier)*
7. **A control transfer is in their model — as the shock, not the outcome.** One reading of the liquidity shock is an entrepreneur "transferring control to a more effective party (e.g., a larger firm…)" (p. 991). So FGG's λ can be *read* as a takeover hazard; what is missing is any endogenous bidder, price or success probability. Say this before a referee does. *(added by verifier)*

**Sentence for the referee report:** FGG prove that liquidity, working through price informativeness, raises a blockholder's incentive to add value — in a model where the blockholder's trade is observable by assumption, with no disclosure rule and no control transfer; we make the observability itself the rule and the control outcome the object.

**Omissions added.**
- **Q15 (p. 999) — the premium renounced by assumption.** One sentence after Q2, FGG set themselves against Zingales (1995) and Burkart–Gromb–Panunzi (1999) on extracting surplus in a future control sale, and say: "we assume that sales take place in a competitive market and so there is no surplus to be extracted. The dispersion of shares yields a more accurate rather than a higher price." *Accurate versus higher price* is precisely our axis. This is the single strongest citation in the paper for the claim that the control-premium object is unoccupied, and the card did not have it.
- **Q16 (p. 991) — a control transfer is one of their readings of λ.** "an entrepreneur who has to sell out when retiring or transferring control to a more effective party (e.g., a larger firm…)". Their λ can be read as a takeover hazard; what is absent is any endogenous bidder, price, or success probability. Better we say this than a referee.
- **Q13 (p. 991) — "Our model could accommodate some (limited) secret trading."** Material because the card's whole §7 point 5 rests on observability being assumed and unrelaxed.
- **Q14 (p. 988) — the named stealth-trading lane.** FGG list Kyle–Vila (1991), Kahn–Winton (1998), Maug (1998) and Noe (2002) as the papers that model liquidity as the ability to hide one's trade, and opt out. That is the exact citation set for the literature our κ sits in.
- **§2 — no buy action.** The insider sells all or retains; Appendix I adds only a partial *sale*. Accumulation, and hence any stake threshold, is outside the model. Parallel to Admati–Pfleiderer, and it makes our four-action space a genuine extension against both.
- **§2 / Appendix F (p. 1010) — the only microfounded noise trade.** A unit mass of consumers, an equiprobable fraction 0 or d of whom must consume early and liquidate, with d(α) = d·α. Inside that specialisation the float *is* the noise-trading intensity — the tightest κ-analogue in the paper, available only in the special case.
- **§4 (p. 988) — Fishman & Hagerty (1989).** The paper's only acknowledgement that firm disclosure is an alternative route to an informative price. One sentence, no model, but it is where a disclosure-versus-liquidity trade-off would have to attach.
- Noted, not added as a section item: footnote 4 (p. 990) explains that the discrete liquidity-demand set is needed because with a Kyle continuum a separating PBE would fail — i.e. the binary structure is a technical necessity, not a simplification of convenience; and footnote 21 (p. 1002) notes the baseline never forced the insider to retain his stake absent a shock.
