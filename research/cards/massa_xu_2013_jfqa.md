**Type:** empirical (cross-sectional corporate finance / market microstructure)   **Role for us:** anchor (the canonical target-liquidity → takeover-premium result) · also measurement (liquidity index construction)

## 1. Question

Does the *target firm's stock liquidity* have value in the market for corporate control? The authors argue an acquirer inherits part of the target's liquidity, and that this inherited liquidity is worth something only to acquirers whose own shareholders want an exit option — i.e. **public** acquirers. They ask three linked questions: (i) does buying a more liquid target make the acquirer's own stock more liquid; (ii) does target liquidity shape *who* buys (public vs. private acquirer) and *whether the deal closes*; (iii) does it shape *the price* — the offer premium and the target's announcement return. The motivating puzzle is Bargeron, Schlingemann, Stulz & Zutter (2008): public acquirers pay more than private ones, and nobody knows why. Liquidity is offered as one answer.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Sample.** Thomson SDC M&A database; **4,691 completed U.S. acquisitions of publicly listed targets announced 1987–2007** (p. 1468). Adding the **2,015 incomplete** transactions gives the 6,706-deal completion sample (p. 1479, p. 1481, p. 1492). Firm characteristics from CRSP/Compustat Merged; institutional ownership from Thomson Reuters (CDA/Spectrum) 13F; IPOs from the SDC equity-issuance database. Filters follow Chen–Harford–Li (2007) and Bargeron et al. (2008): drop self-tenders, deals announced the same day as a share repurchase, and reverse mergers (acquirer assets ≤ half the transaction value). Winsorised at 1% (market/book at 5%). Unit of observation = deal (quarterly firm variables where available). Mean transaction value $303m; 36.40% of deals have a public acquirer (Table 1, p. 1469).

**Liquidity measure — this is the paper's measurement contribution.** A **first principal component** of four quarterly, daily-averaged proxies, each de-meaned and divided by its SD before factorising (fn. 4, p. 1471): bid–ask spread (loading −0.33), log volume (+0.43), log turnover (+0.42), Amihud illiquidity ratio (−0.13) (Table 2, p. 1472; definition p. 1494). The first PC captures **51% of the common variation, eigenvalue 2.05** (next 1.07) (p. 1471). The Amihud term is bounded above following Acharya–Pedersen (2005) — eq. (1), p. 1471 — and in practice **barely enters**: its correlation with the index is **−0.12%** (Table 2 Panel C, p. 1472). So "liquidity" here is effectively *spread + volume + turnover*, correlated 67%–86% with the index. Correlation of the index with market cap is 17.25% (20% within the target sample), which is why size, log size and size² are controls everywhere.

**Dependent variables / designs.**
- *Liquidity inheritance* (Table 3, p. 1475): OLS of the change in the acquirer's liquidity index (quarter −12m before announcement → +12m after effective date, ×100) on the target-minus-acquirer liquidity difference. N = 1,033.
- *Who buys* (Table 4, pp. 1476–1477): probit, dependent = acquirer public status. N = 4,691.
- *Acquirer-liquidity matching* (Table 5, pp. 1478–1479): OLS with **Heckman (1979)** correction for the public-acquirer selection (acquirer liquidity is unobservable for private acquirers). N = 1,083.
- *Completion* (Table 6, pp. 1480–1481): completion indicator, estimated separately for public and private acquirers as **seemingly unrelated equations (Zellner 1962)**, then a Wald test of the coefficient difference. N = 6,706.
- *Price* (Tables 7 & 8, pp. 1482–1484): **offer premium** and target **CAR** on liquidity, public and private acquirer subsamples, Heckman-corrected, coefficient difference tested. Windows [−1,+1], [−42,+126] ("169-day"), [−42,0], [0,+126]; CARs vs. the CRSP value-weighted index, estimation window 255 days ending 46 days before announcement (Schwert 1996).

All specifications carry **year and industry fixed effects**; standard errors are heteroskedasticity-robust and **clustered at the industry level**; tables print **t-statistics, not standard errors**.

**Identification.** Essentially **cross-sectional association with controls, fixed effects and selection corrections — no exogenous liquidity shock, no DiD, no event study of any rule, and liquidity is never instrumented.** The single IV appears in robustness and instruments a *different* endogenous variable, the **fraction acquired**, with the Herfindahl index and Harford's (2005) econshock (Table 11 Panel B, p. 1490; first-stage F = 17.56, p. 1491). The word "causal" does not appear in the paper.

**Robustness (Table 11, pp. 1490–1492).** Heckman on >50% acquired; IV on fraction acquired; Heckman on the no-toehold subsample (liquidity→toehold is significantly *negative*; the printed first-stage coefficient is +0.216*** (t = 3.84) on a column headed **"No Toehold"** — Table 11 Panel C, p. 1490 — while the text on p. 1491 describes the first stage as a probit on a *toehold* indicator; the sign reads correctly only under the table's labelling *(orientation flagged by verifier)*); all-cash (368 public acquirers / 2,944 targets) vs. all-stock (430 / 644) subsamples, with t-statistics of difference 0.63, 0.02, 0.12; including incomplete transactions; and 312 contested deals.

**Answering the notes' "+10% premium per SD of target liquidity, public bidders" query directly:** the printed figure is **10%**, it is **conditional on a public acquirer**, and the underlying coefficient is **12.151 with t = 8.832** (Table 7 Panel A, col. 1, p. 1482). **Standard errors are never printed** — JFQA tables here give t-statistics only, so the SE must be backed out (≈ 12.151 / 8.832 ≈ 1.376). ## 4. Institutional facts used

- **No disclosure rule appears anywhere in this paper.** A full-text search of the Cambridge PDF finds no "Schedule 13D", no "13(d)", no "Williams Act", no filing window, no 5% ownership trigger, and no "disclosure" used in a regulatory sense (the four "disclos*" hits are SDC deal filters and a reference title). *(Corrected by verifier: the stem "activis" **does** occur once — but only as the title of Noe, "Investor Activism and Financial Market Structure", in the reference list, p. 1497. No activist appears as an economic agent.)* "Blockholder" occurs exactly twice, both as passing motivation rather than analysis: p. 1463 ("lower exit costs also make liquid stocks more attractive to blockholders who can increase firm value by monitoring") and p. 1472 (reading Table 1 Panel D). *(Page pair confirmed by verifier; "literature-review asides" was the reader's characterisation — the first is in the introduction, the second in a table discussion.)*
- Institutional ownership = fraction held by **SEC Form 13F** filers; insider ownership = a dummy for >5% held by insiders filing **SEC Forms 3, 4, 5, 144** (p. 1494). These are the only regulatory objects in the paper, and both are used as *controls*, not as a rule being varied.
- **Toehold** = acquirer owns more than **0.5%** of the target before the transaction (p. 1495) — far below the 13D threshold; 34.85% of deals have one (Table 1, p. 1469).
- **Offer premium** — and here the paper is **internally inconsistent**: the text says "the SDC transaction value over the target's market value 63 days prior to the announcement" (p. 1481), while the Table 7 header says "the transaction value minus the product of the fraction acquired and the target firm's market value 63 days prior to the announcement, divided by the latter, in percentage points" (p. 1482). Only the second scales for partial stakes. The Table 1 "avg. premium" control averages the *first* definition per quarter and is **4.83% (median 3.83%)** — implausibly low for a takeover premium, which is what you get when partial-stake transaction values are divided by full market capitalisation.
- **(added by verifier) The accumulation channel is named in the paper, and it is our channel.** Developing Hypothesis 3, p. 1468: "Kyle and Vila (1991) and Maug (1998) argue that liquidity makes it easier to accumulate shares because of the discount associated with the presence of liquidity traders, thus circumventing the Grossman and Hart (1986) paradox." The same paragraph cites Bolton–Von Thadden (1998a,b), Kahn–Winton (1998) and Noe (2002) linking concentrated shareholdings and monitoring to stock liquidity. This is the *stake-accumulation* mechanism our κ drives, stated by the anchor paper itself — but it is invoked only to motivate why institutions with big stakes like liquidity, and **no accumulation object is ever estimated**. Cite it when we need the anchor paper to have named our mechanism without measuring it.
- Deal-flow / macro controls: Schlingemann–Stulz–Walkling (2002) industry transaction count; Harford (2005) econshock; the commercial-and-industrial loan credit spread (pp. 1473, 1494).
- Governance: Gompers–Ishii–Metrick (2003) index (p. 1474).

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths**
- Large, standard, long sample (4,691 completed / 6,706 all deals, 21 years) with the conventional M&A filters; nothing exotic to attack on data construction.
- The **public-vs-private acquirer split is a genuine internal control**: the same target liquidity, the same controls, two buyer types, and a formally tested coefficient difference. That is much harder to explain with an omitted target characteristic than a single pooled coefficient would be.
- The result **chains coherently across four different objects** — inheritance (Table 3) → matching (Tables 4, 5) → completion (Table 6) → price (Tables 7, 8) → bidding contests (Table 11 Panel G). Four independent margins pointing the same way.
- Heavy, honest control set for the obvious confounds: size, log size, size², idiosyncratic risk as dispersion of opinion (Chatterjee–John–Yan), cash/assets for asset liquidity (Gopalan–Kadan–Pevzner), market/book, governance index, toehold, termination fees, hostility, competing bidders, payment method.
- The **contested-deal sample (N = 312)** is the nicest piece of the paper for us: it holds availability of buyer type fixed and still finds liquid targets attract public bidders and close more often.

**Weaknesses / open flanks**
- **Identification is the flank.** Everything is a conditional correlation. Liquidity is never instrumented and there is no shock. The paper does not claim causality, but it also never offers an exogenous source of variation in target liquidity — an obvious referee demand today.
- **The economic magnitudes are not reproducible from the printed numbers.** Table 1 gives the target liquidity index mean (0.26, median 0.23) but **never its standard deviation**, and Table 2's constituents are standardised before factorising. So the reader cannot verify the step from coefficient 12.151 to "10%". Nor is the mean/SD of the offer premium itself ever printed. *(Partial fix, ours not theirs — **recomputed and corrected by the verifier**: the SD can be backed out and is internally consistent, but it is **≈ 0.86, not 0.8**. The three premium/return conversions only bound it loosely, because they are rounded to whole percentage points: 12.151 → "10%" gives 0.78–0.86, 2.227 → "2%" gives 0.67–1.12, 6.107 → "5%" gives 0.74–0.90. The **completion** conversions pin it, because they are printed to one decimal: 0.028 → "2.4%" (p. 1480) and 0.049 → "4.2%" (p. 1481) both give exactly **0.857**, and 0.857 reproduces all five printed magnitudes (12.151×0.857 = 10.4 → "10%"; 2.227×0.857 = 1.91 → "2%"; 6.107×0.857 = 5.23 → "5%"). **Use σ ≈ 0.86** if we rescale their coefficients, and say we inferred it. Note the premium is in **percentage points**, so "10%" means 10 pp.)*
- **The two offer-premium definitions conflict** (p. 1481 vs. p. 1482, above), and the "4.83%" average premium in Table 1 signals that partial-stake deals are contaminating at least one of them. Mean % acquired is only **36.90%** (median 10.87%) in the full sample — most "acquisitions" here are **minority stake purchases**, not changes of control. Robustness restricts to >50% acquired (Table 11 Panel A: premium coefficient 31.020***, t = 9.189) and the sign survives, but the headline "10%" is estimated on a sample that is mostly not takeovers. *(Added by verifier: the authors have one further answer we should not be caught not knowing — **fn. 10, p. 1491**: "For transactions of minority stakes, we test for whether the results depend on the trading status of the target after the transaction. The results (available from the authors) are similar in the two subsamples consisting of acquisitions after which the target firm either ceased or continued trading in the year after the transaction was completed." It is **unreported**, so it does not close the flank, but it means the criticism must be phrased as "not shown", not as "not considered".)*
- **The Amihud ratio is essentially inert** in the index (loading −0.13, correlation with the index −0.12%). Anyone matching this paper to an Amihud-based illiquidity measure — which is exactly what our κ proxy would be — is not matching the same object. This is a real translation problem, not a nitpick.
- **The mechanism is argued, not tested.** Section II offers three stories (transparency, limited attention, categorical thinking) and the paper picks none. The investor-attention evidence is relegated to "unreported tests" (fn. 7, p. 1476). The paper is a set of reduced-form facts wearing a behavioural coat.
- **Selection corrections lean on functional form.** The Heckman exclusion restriction is never named; the inverse Mills ratio ("Mills public") is itself large and significant in the premium regressions (15.738**, t = 2.576), so selection is doing real work with no stated instrument.
- **The time-series results cut against each other.** Demand for liquidity is higher in low-liquidity times (R10 matching/completion) while the *price* of liquidity is higher in high-liquidity times (premium 27.976*** vs. 8.694***). The paper reconciles this in one sentence ("in times of low overall liquidity, acquirers, even if they need it, cannot afford to pay more", p. 1484). That is a story, not a test.
- **Standard errors are never printed** — t-statistics only.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

Stated precisely in the three coordinates the positioning stage cares about:

- **Object.** They occupy: acquirer post-deal liquidity; **acquirer public status** (who buys); acquirer liquidity (matching); **deal completion**; **offer premium**; **target announcement CAR**; number and composition of bids in contests; the private acquirer's subsequent IPO. They do **not** occupy: **bidder entry as an ex-ante object** (they observe bids only within a 312-deal contested subsample and never model entry), **activism campaign success**, engagement, or any minority-shareholder welfare object. They also never report **acquirer returns** — the acquirer-side outcome studied is the acquirer's *liquidity*, not its announcement return. (Our notes asked for "acquirer returns": the paper does not have them. **Confirmed by verifier by exhaustive search:** acquirer excess returns appear only as the *definition* of the idiosyncratic-risk control — "the standard deviation of acquirer excess returns on the value-weighted CRSP index", p. 1473, repeated in the variable appendix p. 1494 — plus one reference title on p. 1496. There is no acquirer CAR anywhere.)
- **(added by verifier) One exception to "no bidder entry": the authors read the *private*-acquirer premium as competition from a *potential* public bidder.** p. 1485: "private acquirers do not pay a significant liquidity premium ... in times of high liquidity. Since these are the times of less demand for liquidity, this result indicates that the liquidity premium paid by private acquirers is driven by potential demand by other public bidders." That is a latent-entry interpretation of a premium coefficient — asserted, never estimated (no entry margin is modelled; the only realised-competition object is the 312-deal contested cut). It matters for us because our control outcome includes bidder entry: the anchor paper already reads its premium result through an entry lens, so "entry" is not virgin ground rhetorically, only empirically.
- **Margin of the disclosure rule.** **None.** Neither the threshold margin nor the window margin appears. There is no Schedule 13D, no Williams Act, no filing deadline, no 5% trigger, no 2024 acceleration (the paper predates it by eleven years). Their only stake-size object is the 0.5% **toehold** dummy, which sits below any disclosure threshold. **The entire disclosure-rule dimension is open ground relative to this paper.**
- **Identification.** Cross-sectional OLS/probit with year and industry fixed effects, Heckman selection corrections, SUR + Wald tests, and one IV *for the fraction acquired only*. **No DiD, no regression discontinuity, no rule-change event study, no structural estimation.** The word "causal" never appears; nor does any "future research" paragraph — the paper simply ends (Section IX, pp. 1492–1493) without conceding a research agenda, which means we cannot quote them declaring the disclosure margin out of scope. Its absence is established by exhaustion of the text, not by their own statement.
- Also not done: any **theory model**. Section II ("Hypotheses", pp. 1466–1468) is verbal — three hypotheses, no primitives, no equilibrium. There is nothing here to nest our model in.

## 7. Implications for our position

**What it buys us.** This is the empirical anchor for the *reduced-form* leg of the paper's premise: in the data, **target stock liquidity and takeover premium move together, and the co-movement is concentrated where the buyer's shareholders value an exit option**. That is the sign our premium wedge needs at the top level, and it is published in a good journal on a 21-year US sample, so a referee will not make us prove it again. R6 (12.151, t = 8.832, +10% per SD) is the number to cite; R8 (public−private difference 6.629, χ² = 11.05) is the number that makes it an *economic* fact rather than a liquidity-loads-on-size artefact. R13 (contested deals) is the closest thing in the literature to evidence on **bidder composition responding to target liquidity**, which is our "control outcome" broadened beyond the premium.

**(added by verifier) Their economic channel is not ours, and the difference is the whole positioning argument.** In Massa–Xu the premium is paid **for an asset the buyer keeps**: the target's liquidity is *inherited* by the merged firm (Hypothesis 1, pp. 1466–1467, R1), and the acquirer's own shareholders value that inherited liquidity because they want an exit option (Hypothesis 2, p. 1467). Liquidity enters on the **buyer's demand side**. In our model liquidity enters on the **blockholder's accumulation side** — κ determines how much can be bought behind the partition before the disclosure rule flags it. Same reduced-form sign, two unrelated mechanisms. This is a *gift*: their result cannot be re-derived from our mechanism, so we are not competing for the same explanation, and their coefficient is not a test of our model. It also means we must never write "Massa–Xu show liquidity lets blockholders accumulate cheaply" — they show nothing of the sort.

**Where it constrains us.** Three constraints, in descending order of bite.

1. **Whatever we claim about liquidity → premium, this paper already has the sign.** Our contribution cannot be "liquidity raises the premium". It has to be about the **partition** — how the disclosure rule's flagged/pooled split changes that relationship. Our T2 (disclosure attenuation: a stricter rule lowers how much control outcomes move with liquidity) is a claim *about the slope Massa–Xu estimate*, and that is a defensible way to sit on top of them rather than beside them.
2. **Their liquidity measure is not our κ.** Their index is a PCA in which Amihud contributes almost nothing (loading −0.13, correlation −0.12%); it is driven by spread, volume and turnover. Our CONTEXT.md defines liquidity empirically as "an Amihud-type illiquidity measure inverted". If we cite the +10% as calibration, we must either (a) replicate with an Amihud-only measure, or (b) state that we are borrowing the *sign and rough order of magnitude*, not the coefficient. Do not calibrate the premium wedge to 12.151 without doing (a).
3. **They never print the SD** of their liquidity index (nor of the offer premium), so "+10% per SD" cannot be inverted into a structural magnitude *from the printed table alone*. *(Softened by verifier: it can be backed out — σ ≈ 0.86, see §5 — but it is our arithmetic, not their number, and the premium's own SD is still missing, so any structural calibration inherits an unstated denominator.)*

**The whitespace this paper defines.** Massa–Xu occupies (takeover premium + completion + acquirer type) × (**no disclosure margin**) × (cross-sectional association). Our position — control outcome × **window margin** × the Feb-2024 acceleration — is disjoint from it in *both* the margin and the identification coordinate, and overlaps only on the object. That is the ideal relationship for an anchor: the same dependent variable, none of the same variation. The referee sentence writes itself: *Massa and Xu (2013) show target liquidity is priced in the market for corporate control on 4,691 US deals; they hold the legal environment fixed. We ask what happens to that pricing when the disclosure rule moves.*

**A concrete design hook.** Their contested-deal cut (Table 11 Panel G, p. 1490) is a small (N = 312) but clean template for "does target liquidity change who shows up to bid". If our window-margin design ends up needing a control outcome that is not the premium, **bidder composition in contested deals** is a pre-validated object with a published benchmark to compare our estimates against.

**One caution for the December package.** Because their sample is mostly minority stake purchases (mean 36.90% acquired), our restatement of their result should quote the **>50%-acquired** robustness figure (31.020***, t = 9.189, p. 1490) alongside the headline, or a referee who knows the paper will point at the mean and say the headline is not about takeovers.
