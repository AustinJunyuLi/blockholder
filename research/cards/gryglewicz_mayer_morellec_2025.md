## 1. Question

How does a single large shareholder's ownership stake co-evolve with the firm's policies, when the stake itself is the state variable and nobody can commit? They ask what makes a blockholder build a stake toward full ownership versus unwind it to zero, and what that endogenous trading does to effort, managerial pay, investment, leverage, default risk and the stock price. The motivating problem is econometric rather than legal: blockholder ownership is endogenous and dynamic, so cross-sectional "blockholders cause X" evidence is hard to read, and they want a structure that makes the two-way feedback explicit (p. 1). A secondary policy question: does taking control *away* from the blockholder (staggered boards, entrenchment provisions) help dispersed shareholders? Their answer is no, once the blockholder's stake is allowed to respond.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Primitives.** Continuous time, infinite horizon, three risk-neutral agents with common discount rate ρ > 0: one blockholder holding fraction θ_t ∈ [0,1] of equity, a continuum of dispersed shareholders, one manager. Capital stock K_t; scaled cash flow dX_t = α₁dt + dN_t(1 − m_t) with E[dN_t] = (α₂ + b_t)dt (p. 7, eq. 1). Blockholder effort b_t is **unobservable and non-contractible**, private cost ½κK_t b_t² (p. 7). Manager can divert the jump cash flow for private benefit λK_t; short-term contracts only; incentive constraint β_t ≥ λ binds (p. 8, eq. 3). Investment i_t with convex cost ½K_t i_t², dK_t = (µi_t − δ)K_t dt (p. 8, eq. 4). Short-term debt L_t; creditors discount at r ≤ ρ, a reduced-form tax benefit (p. 8, fn. 4). No commitment to anything except the within-period managerial contract.

**The two frictions that stand in for "liquidity" — neither is market microstructure.**
1. *Blockholder holding cost* ½πθ_t², following DeMarzo–Urošević (2006), Du–Zhu (2017), Duffie–Zhu (2017); read as "the blockholder's financial or capital constraints or higher cost of capital" (p. 10, Q-adjacent). In §6 this π is the thing that jumps — and *that* jump is what the paper calls a **liquidity shock**.
2. *Dispersed-investor holding benefit* η(θ) = π^I[θ − θ̃]⁺ (p. 10), micro-founded in App. 33–35 by index investors with quadratic tracking-error disutility who cannot short (citing Koijen–Richmond–Yogo 2024 and Haddad–Huebner–Loualiche 2025). This is **inelastic supply**, and it is the object that makes buying expensive near θ = 1.

**Information and price formation — there is no Kyle market.** Trades are public: "Trading decisions are observable to investors" (p. 6, Q5). Dispersed shareholders are competitive price takers (p. 12, Q6). The price p(θ) solves a fair-pricing ODE (eq. 16, p. 17) — the present value of dividends plus the holding benefit — with *no* market maker, *no* order flow, *no* noise trader, and *no* inference problem. The blockholder has **no private information about firm value**: `signal` = 0 hits, `asymmetric` = 0 hits, and both occurrences of "private information" (p. 6) describe *other* papers. "Price impact" in this model means only that p(θ) is increasing, so buying more costs more (p. 2, Q4; p. 11).

**Equilibrium notion.** Continuous, scaled Markov equilibrium with states (K_t, θ_t): V = Kv(θ), P = Kp(θ), L = Kℓ(θ), all controls Markov; trading solves dθ ∈ argmax_Δ K{v(θ+Δ) − Δp(θ+Δ)} (eq. 10, p. 13). Interior smooth trading forces **p(θ) = v′(θ)** (eq. 14, p. 15): the blockholder earns nothing on the marginal share and everything on infra-marginal holdings. Closed forms: v̂(θ) in (A.6), p(θ) in (A.7) (App. 5).

**The central object — the trading rate.** θ̇ = (1/p′(θ))[θ(ρ−r)ℓ′(θ) + θλ(1−λ)/κ − (πθ + η(θ))] (eq. 17, p. 17), generalised to θ̇ = (1/p′)[(ρ+δ−µi)Σ_x (∂v/∂x)(∂x/∂θ) − (πθ + η(θ))] over policies x ∈ {b, ℓ, i} (eq. 18, p. 17). Gains from trade = (A) how far a policy is distorted from the blockholder's optimum × (B) how strongly ownership passes through to that policy (via effort, ∂b/∂θ = (1−λ)/κ).

**Entry.** The blockholder buys φ ∈ [0,1] at the *uninformed* dispersed-ownership price and the rest at the informed price; enters iff E(θ₀) ≥ 0 (eq. 20, p. 20). Proposition 5: θ₀ = φ exactly. φ is motivated by pre-disclosure accumulation (fn. 13, p. 20) and is a **free scalar with no comparative static anywhere** (`φ` appears only on pp. 20, 21 and App. 13).

**Method / identification.** Theory only. Full-text greps: `data` 0, `regress` 0, `estimat` 0, `calibrat` 0, `simulat` 0, `empirical` 2 (both prose). Two numerical figures illustrate the closed forms: Fig. 1 (p. 23) and Fig. 2 (p. 27), both at α = κ = 1, ρ+δ = 0.2, r+δ = 0.1, µ = 0.066, π = 0.6, θ̃ = 0.99, θ₀ = 0.3.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- Genuinely closed-form: v̂(θ) and p(θ) are explicit (A.6)–(A.7), and the trading rate (17) decomposes into three named channels. Very few dynamic blockholder models get this far without numerics.
- Existence *and* uniqueness for the baseline among all continuous scaled Markov equilibria (Prop. 4), with the general HJB (A.16) allowing lumpy and randomised trades before smooth trading is shown to dominate — that is the right order of argument.
- The decomposition (18) is portable: any new friction enters as one more (∂v/∂x)(∂x/∂θ) term in the numerator of θ̇. This is the part of the paper most useful to us mechanically.
- The control-rights result (R14) is a clean, surprising, *proved* statement: whether exit is ever optimal depends only on who controls investment.
- The inelastic-supply parameter is micro-founded (App. 33–35) rather than assumed. **(added by verifier)** The micro-foundation also states, in prose, a genuinely liquidity-flavoured *feedback* loop that is never modelled: "When the blockholder buys the firm's stock, it would push up the price and thus increase the firm's weight in the stock market index, further stimulating demand from index investors, which in turn makes it harder to buy more of the stock" (App. 35). Neither type of investor may short (App. 33). This is the closest the paper comes to an endogenous price-impact mechanism, and it lives entirely in an appendix paragraph — a soft flank, and a place where our microstructure object is strictly stronger.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **The blockholder has no private information.** No signal, no adverse selection, no inference. Every price is set by agents who correctly anticipate effort. That is a large modelling choice for a paper whose closest cited relatives (Back et al. 2018, Collin-Dufresne–Fos) are all about informed trading, and it is nowhere defended.
- **No market for the shares.** "Price impact" is a slope of an increasing pricing function, not a microstructure object. A referee from the Kyle tradition will ask why supply inelasticity from index funds is the only source of it.
- Section 6 — the section that carries the word "liquidity" — is a **sketch**: no proposition, no proof, no figure, and its own framing concedes it is not central (p. 32, S3/S4). R20 is the paper's most quotable liquidity claim and it is the least supported.
- Prop. 8's existence/uniqueness is omitted "for brevity" (App. 21); the investor-control-with-risky-debt variant is abandoned ("We leave the further analysis of this model variant", App. 28); and no closed-form condition on R can be given because p(0) has none (App. 21).
- Uniqueness carries qualifiers in three of the four equilibrium results: Prop. 6 is unique only within smooth trading (fn. 16, p. 25, S2), Prop. 7 only within the ≤ one-discontinuity class (Q9).
- Internal slips a careful referee will find: p. 21 attributes the three entry/trading scenarios to "Proposition 2" (they follow from Prop. 3 / Cor. 2); the Fig. 2 caption (p. 27) says "solid blue line" while p. 26 calls the same object the "dotted blue line" and then the "solid black line"; **p. 23** describes Fig. 1's upper-left panel as showing "the trading rate is non-monotonic in λ" when the panel is labelled "Blockholder stake θ_t" — it plots the *stake path* *(page corrected by verifier: p. 23, not p. 22)*; App. 1 step 2 duplicates a full sentence verbatim ("The blockholder chooses its effort level, the shock dN_t realizes and, observing dN_t, the manager chooses diversion m_t." twice); **App. 25** writes "(i) ℓ < 1" for ℓ < y *(page corrected by verifier: App. 25, not App. 24)*; p. 20 has "the interest on deb r"; p. 1 has "can lead to an increase blockholder ownership". Also p. 20: the entry cost is printed R(θ₀) := η p(0) + max{0, θ₀ − φ}p(θ₀), where the leading coefficient should evidently be φ, not η *(added by verifier)*.
- The free-rider problem is raised (p. 20, Q3) and then assumed away by the scalar φ, with no Grossman–Hart citation (`Grossman` 0 hits) and no comparative static in φ.
- The Figure-1/2 calibration has 4κπ = 2.4 > 1, i.e. it sits in the region where Cor. 3(2) would force exit if the debt benefit were switched off. Nothing is wrong, but the debt channel is doing all the work in every picture in the paper.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**Object they occupy:** the blockholder's stake path θ_t and the firm's policies (effort, β, investment, book leverage, default probability, credit spread, stock price), plus one binary event — the blockholder buying the residual 1 − θ and going private.
**Object they do NOT occupy:** takeover premium, bidder entry, competing bids, announcement return, campaign success, proxy contest, settlement. `premium` 0, `tender` 0, `bidder` 0, `campaign` 0, `proxy` 0 hits in 73 pages.

**Is a bid, a bidder or a premium modelled? No.** The only "takeover" is the incumbent blockholder reaching θ = 1: "the blockholder conducts an immediate lumpy trade toward one (acquires the entire firm) and thereafter stops trading" (p. 28, Prop. 7(2)). There is no outside acquirer, no tender game, no free-rider tendering condition, no reservation price, no toehold-vs-premium tradeoff. What *does* exist is a mechanical price jump: at θ\* the price discontinuously rises from p(θ\*) = v̂′(θ\*) to p̂(1), the price that prevails under perpetual full ownership — the proof states the discontinuity is an "upward jump" (App. 29; proof of Prop. 7 runs App. 29–33) *(cite corrected by verifier from App. 30–33)*. Prop. 7(2) itself gives only p(θ) = p̂(1) with p̂(1) < v̂′(1) for θ ∈ (θ\*, 1). A "premium" is therefore *implicit in the price path* — but it is never named, never decomposed, never compared to anything, and never the subject of a comparative static. The paragraph *introducing* Prop. 7 notes the bid need not be mandatory: the discontinuity arises "when it becomes optimal for the blockholder to initiate a takeover bid for all remaining shares upon reaching an upper threshold θ\* from below, even if such a bid is not mandatory" (pp. 27–28) *(attribution corrected by verifier: this sentence is in the §4 setup text, not inside Prop. 7)*, and uniqueness is claimed only "Within the class of equilibria where p(θ) has at most one discontinuity" (p. 28, Q9). Note also that the conclusion calls the entry stake θ₀ = φ "an endogenous toehold" (p. 34) — the only use of the word, and it is a *self*-toehold with no rival bidder to hold it against *(added by verifier)*.

**Margin of the disclosure rule: NONE — background, and only just.** The rule appears once, in fn. 13 on p. 20 (Q1), as prose justifying that a fraction φ of the firm can be bought before the price adjusts. It is not a LEVEL (5% is stated, never used as a parameter and never varied), not a CHANGE (no dated reform; `window`, `days`, `13D`, `13G` all 0 hits), and not a partition (all trades are public from the first instant, p. 6, Q5 — there is no flagged/pooled distinction because there is nothing to flag). The one place the rule *could* have bitten is Prop. 5, where θ₀ = φ exactly; they do not take the derivative.

**Identification: theory only.** No sample, no estimation, no calibration. Predictions are offered as testable but never tested: "Our setting also yields testable predictions linking firm policies to the size of the blockholder's stake and highlighting how control rights affect blockholder trading and firm value" (p. 5).

**Declared out of scope / future work, quoted.**
- "In the benchmark model, we abstract from elements that would introduce exogenous trading dynamics and are not key to our main findings." (p. 32) — the framing that makes *all* stochastic ownership dynamics, including liquidity shocks, peripheral.
- "We sketch the equilibrium in this setting." (p. 32) — §6, the liquidity-shock section, in full.
- "We leave the further analysis of this model variant." (App. 28) — investor control with risky debt.
- "For brevity, we omit a formal proof of existence and uniqueness and sketch the arguments here." (App. 21) — Prop. 8.
- "Although we cannot prove it, we believe that our equilibrium is also unique among continuous, scaled Markov equilibria." (p. 25, fn. 16) — Prop. 6.

**How they position against the papers we care about (all engagement is on p. 5–6).**
- **Back, Collin-Dufresne, Fos, Li & Ljungqvist (2018)** — cited **once**, bundled with Admati–Pfleiderer–Zechner (1994) and DeMarzo–Urošević (2006) as models where a large shareholder trades and influences performance via costly effort, then dismissed on scope: "In these models, blockholders have no direct influence over firm policies (there is no investment or debt financing), limiting the scope of engagement." (p. 5, Q10). **They never engage Back et al.'s liquidity content at all** — no mention of noise-trading volatility, Kyle lambda, or the σ²T/window isomorphism.
- **Collin-Dufresne & Fos (2015, 2016)** — cited only as institutional fact on p. 20 (Q2 and the 7.51% figure), never as a modelling antecedent.
- **Maug (1998)** — named **once**, inside a "see, e.g." list on p. 5. The liquidity-versus-control tradeoff that is Maug's entire point is never discussed.
- **Governance-by-exit (Admati–Pfleiderer 2009, Edmans–Manso 2011, Dasgupta–Piacentino 2015, Levit 2019)** — dismissed as a class: "These models have a single round of trading and cannot analyze feedback from prices to blockholder actions." (p. 6, Q11).
- **DeMarzo & Urošević (2006)** — the base model; 14 mentions; the paper is explicitly an extension of it along four channels (pp. 1, 5).
- **Kyle & Vila, Burkart & Lee, Corum & Levit, Grossman & Hart, Shleifer & Vishny** — **zero hits each** in 73 pages. None is cited, discussed or refuted.

## 7. Implications for our position

**Cell they occupy:** object = *stake dynamics + firm policies + full acquisition by the incumbent*; margin = *none (background)*; identification = *theory only*. That is a different cell from ours on two of three axes.

**Do they touch the whitespace `competitor_map.md` rates as open?**

1. **"Bidder entry × window, theory" — NO, untouched, on both halves.** There is no bidder: the only acquirer is the blockholder already in the stock, and entry in this paper means the blockholder's own *initial* purchase θ₀ = φ, not a rival's arrival. There is no window: `window`, `days`, `13D` return 0 hits. The whitespace survives this paper intact.
2. **"Premium wedge from the partition" — NO, untouched, and for a structural reason worth stating.** There is no partition to work with: trades are observable to everyone from the outset (p. 6, Q5) and prices are set by competitive price takers (p. 12, Q6), so the model has no flagged/pooled distinction at all. And there is no premium object (`premium` 0, `tender` 0). The price *jump* at θ\* from p(θ\*) to p̂(1) is the closest thing, and it is a jump in the *pricing function of a monopsonist buying inelastic supply*, not a bid premium paid to induce tendering. The whitespace survives.

**What this paper does constrain for us.**
- **The object cell "endogenous stake path with a full-acquisition absorbing state" is now taken, in continuous time, with closed forms.** If draft_v3's core model presents gradual accumulation ending in a lumpy full acquisition as its contribution, this paper has it (Prop. 7). Our contribution must sit in the *partition* and the *control outcome*, not in the stake path.
- **"Blockholder ownership makes exit or accumulation self-reinforcing, so ownership is bimodal" (R8) is theirs and PROVED.** Do not claim a bifurcation/reinforcement result as novel.
- **A holding-cost comparative static on the exit outcome is theirs and PROVED** (R9: with ρ = r, 4κπ > 1 ⟹ θ_∞ = 0). If we ever state "higher holding costs ⟹ exit", cite Cor. 3(2), p. 22.
- **Naming hazard.** They use "liquidity shock" to mean a jump in the blockholder's own holding cost π (pp. 5, 32–33, Q7, Q8), which is precisely what `CONTEXT.md` forbids us from calling liquidity. A referee who has read both papers will conflate the two unless we say "noise-trading intensity κ" or "market liquidity" explicitly at every first use. Note also that their κ is the *effort cost* parameter, not liquidity — a direct symbol collision with our κ. Consider renaming ours in draft_v3, or flag the clash in a footnote.

**What this paper supports for us.**
- **Their φ is our threshold margin, unexploited.** Prop. 5 says the entry stake equals *exactly* the fraction buyable before the price adjusts, and fn. 13 (Q1) says that fraction exists because of the 5% report. They wrote down the reduced form of the threshold margin, told the reader it comes from Schedule 13D, and then never differentiated with respect to it. A model in which φ is derived from the rule (threshold level, and how long you may keep buying before filing) rather than assumed, and in which ∂θ₀/∂φ has consequences for a control outcome, is a direct and citable extension of their own Proposition 5.
- **(added by verifier) Both objects our threshold margin needs already exist in their notation, unexploited.** They call θ₀ = φ "an endogenous toehold" (p. 34, the only use of the word), and the price the toehold is bought at is p₀ from Prop. 1 (p. 12) — a closed-form *pooled/uninformed* price. So the pair (pooled price p₀, flagged price p(θ₀)) is already written down; what is missing is any map from the rule to φ and any derivative in φ. Cite Prop. 1 and Prop. 5 together when we set up the partition.
- **Their free-rider sentence is our opening** (p. 20, Q3): they state the free-rider problem, note it would kill entry entirely, and neutralise it by assumption. A partition-based mechanism — the blockholder buys in the pooled state and is flagged into the priced state — is a microfoundation for exactly the assumption they make by hand, and they give no competing microfoundation (`Grossman` 0 hits).
- **Their equation (18) is a template.** Any disclosure friction we introduce should be presentable as one more (∂v/∂x)(∂x/∂θ) term in the numerator of the trading rate, with the sign argument doing the work. This is the cleanest expositional device in the competitor set for "how a friction changes accumulation speed".
- **Their liquidity claim is weak where ours would be strong.** R20 — early holding-cost shock ⟹ exit, late shock ⟹ no exit — is exactly the kind of timing statement our window margin makes, and theirs is ASSERTED in a two-page sketch with no proposition. A PROVED timing result about the *filing* window rather than a holding-cost shock is both differentiated and stronger.
- **They leave the "who controls what" margin partly open** (App. 28, "We leave the further analysis of this model variant"), but that is governance-structure control, not disclosure — not our lane, and we should not follow them into it.

**One caution.** Because this paper carries the CEPR/AFA-2026 imprimatur and shares our exact title vocabulary ("blockholder", "ownership dynamics", "price impact", "liquidity shocks", "takeover"), a referee is likely to reach for it. The card's job in the response file is to be able to say, in one sentence, that it has no disclosure margin, no bidder, no premium and no informed trading — and to cite the greps in §4 rather than assert it.
