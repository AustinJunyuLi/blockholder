## 1. Question

Do proxy contests play an active governance role *after* the 1992 proxy reform, and — the paper's distinctive turn — does the **mere threat** of a contest discipline management, even at firms never actually targeted? Most of the prior proxy-contest literature (Dodd–Warner 1983; DeAngelo–DeAngelo 1989; Ikenberry–Lakonishok 1993; Mulherin–Poulsen 1998) uses pre-1992 data, when contests ran at ~17/year. Fos builds the first census of the post-reform era and then asks whether *predicted* contest probability moves corporate policy.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Sample.** The **universe of all proxy contests from 1994 through 2012: 1,061 unique contests, 873 firms** (about 20% of firms targeted more than once). Construction: an automatic script searches EDGAR for **PREC14A** (preliminary contested proxy statement) and **DEFC14A** (definitive contested proxy statement) filings for every company in the Compustat universe, yielding **>6,000 filings** (about six filings per contest), which are then **manually checked** to identify events. Merged with Compustat (accounting), CRSP (returns), Thomson-Reuters Institutional Holdings 13F (INST and INST-AHF), RiskMetrics (shareholder proposals, GINDEX). Frequency: **56 contests/year on average 1994–2012, 68/year 2006–2012**, versus **17/year 1979–1994** (Mulherin–Poulsen).

*Count discrepancy to flag:* the headline is 1,061 contests, but the tables total 1,027 (Table 2, types), 1,058 (Table 3, sponsors), 1,066 (Table 4, outcomes) and 1,004 (Table 5, stated goals, sample period given as 1994–**2011**). Coverage differs by classifiable field; the paper does not reconcile them.

**Three designs.**
1. *Event study* (Figures 3–5): cumulative abnormal returns from a **four-factor (market, size, book-to-market, momentum) model, from 24 months before to 24 months after** the contest announcement, split by sponsor type and by each of five stated goals. **No table, no SE, no t-statistic is printed for any CAR in this version.**
2. *Targeting probit* (Table 6): `Pr(PC_it = 1) = Φ(X_it α + ζ_t + ε_it)`, all Compustat firm-years 1994–2012, **N = 58,405**, event and non-event observations, **year fixed effects**, average partial effects reported as the change in contest likelihood per one-SD change in the covariate, **t-statistics from heteroscedasticity-robust SEs clustered within 3-digit-SIC industry**. Pseudo R² = 4.87%.
3. *The threat regression* (Table 9): `y_it = γ₁·P̂C*_it + η_t + η_i + u_it`, where `y_it` is a corporate policy (leverage, cash, investment, dividends, repurchases) and **`P̂C*_it` is the fitted contest probability from the Table 6 probit**. **Firm and year fixed effects**, so the identification is *within-firm variation in predicted contest risk*; SEs heteroscedasticity-robust, clustered by firm. When a policy is the dependent variable it is excluded from the probit generating `P̂C*`. Panel A = full sample (N = 55,195–55,799); **Panel B repeats on "non-targets" only (N = 51,192–51,766)**. [Verifier note: this is the card's reading, not the paper's. **Table 9 Panel B is never mentioned anywhere in the body text of this version** — the words "Panel B reports the results for non-targets" appear only in the table caption, "non-targets" is nowhere defined for Table 9, and the discussion on PDF pp. 25–26 talks only about the full sample. That the ~4,000 dropped firm-years correspond to the 873 targeted firms is an inference from the counts, and a reasonable one, but it is not stated.]

Plus two descriptive comparisons: unpaired targets-vs-non-targets means with two-sample t-tests, and **paired comparison against a matched non-target on year, 3-digit SIC industry, market cap and book-to-market** (Table 7); and within-firm changes over the years preceding the event (Table 8).

**Identification — stated plainly by the author: association, not causation.** The threat result is a within-firm correlation between a *fitted* probability and policy. There is no instrument, no shock, no DiD. Fos says an instrument is needed and that he does not have one (Q4, Q5 below).

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **The 1992 proxy reform** is the paper's institutional anchor: "In 1992 the regulatory burdens surrounding proxy contests were substantially liberalized, thus reducing the cost of engaging in a proxy contest. Specifically, the 1992 proxy reform reduced the costs of proxy contests by relaxing constraints on communications between shareholders of public corporations (Sharara and Hoke-Witherspoon, 1993)." (PDF p. 2). Three triggers are named for the reform: poison-pill adoption, 40 states with anti-takeover statutes by 1991, and the junk-bond collapse (PDF p. 9). **It is background narrative, not a design** — the sample begins in 1994, so there is no pre/post-1992 comparison in the data.
- **The 2010 proxy access episode** (Dodd-Frank authorisation 21 July 2010; SEC Rule 14a-11 adopted 25 August 2010; Chamber/Roundtable petition 29 September 2010; stay 4 October 2010; DC Circuit vacatur 22 July 2011) is described in footnote 1, which runs across PDF pp. 2–3 [page corrected by verifier], as motivation only — again not used as a design.
- **Rule 14a-8** eligibility (continuously held ≥ $2,000 in market value or 1% of securities for at least one year; management may exclude proposals relating to board elections or conflicting with its own) is quoted to define *issue contests* (PDF p. 11, fn. 6).
- **Filing forms as the sampling instrument:** PREC14A and DEFC14A on EDGAR (PDF p. 8) — the equivalent, for contests, of a 13D-based sampling frame.
- **ILLIQUIDITY = Amihud (2002)**, "defined as the yearly average (using daily data) of 1000·|Return|/DollarTradingVolume" (PDF p. 37, Table 1). **INST-AHF = the proportion of shares held by activist hedge funds.** GINDEX = Gompers–Ishii–Metrick (2003).
- **Schedule 13D is never used.** There is no 5% threshold, no filing window, no beneficial-ownership rule anywhere in the paper. The stake-accumulation channel enters only indirectly, via the cited Collin-Dufresne–Fos evidence that activists buy when liquidity is high.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- **A census, not a sample.** Every PREC14A/DEFC14A filing in the Compustat universe over 19 years, hand-checked. Norli–Østergaard–Schindele (2015) had 331 contests over 1994–2007; Fos has 1,061 (he says so explicitly, PDF p. 6, fn. 2). The descriptive layer (R15–R18) is the reference count for the post-reform era.
- **The threat design is genuinely clever and the non-target panel is the right robustness check.** If the policy response only appeared in eventual targets, it would be reverse causality or anticipation of a known campaign; finding the same coefficients in the non-target panel (R11) is what makes "disciplinary effect of the threat" more than a slogan. **[Verifier caveat: the numbers are real and match Table 9 Panel B exactly, but Fos never discusses this panel in the body text of this version and never defines "non-targets" for it. We would be making the argument he did not make. Check the published Management Science version before leaning on it in print.]**
- **Firm fixed effects** mean the threat result is identified off *within-firm* movement in predicted contest risk, not off cross-sectional differences between well- and badly-governed firms.
- The liquidity result is stated at three levels — probit APE, matched cross-section, within-firm time series (R1, R5, R7) — and all three point the same way, which is more than most papers offer.
- Honest and explicit about the causal limits, twice, in the body and in a footnote.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **The threat regressor is a generated regressor.** `P̂C*` is a fitted value from a first-stage probit, and the second-stage SEs in Table 9 are not corrected for first-stage estimation error. Reported significance is overstated by an unknown amount.
- **`P̂C*` is a function of the very policies being explained.** Fos handles this by excluding the dependent policy from the probit that generates `P̂C*` — but the *other* policies remain in it, and corporate policies co-move. LEVERAGE, CASH, DIVIDENDS, INVESTMENT and REPURCHASES are jointly determined; excluding one at a time does not break that.
- **`P̂C*` is largely a repackaging of poor performance and activist ownership** (R3, R4). "When the likelihood of a proxy contest increases, firms increase leverage and cut investment" may be no more than "firms that just performed badly and acquired an activist blockholder cut investment". The disciplinary reading is one interpretation of a reduced-form correlation.
- **No standard errors on any CAR in this version.** The 6.5% headline (R12) — the number most likely to be cited — is read off a figure. It cannot be labelled ESTIMATED from this document.
- **Table counts do not reconcile** (1,061 vs 1,027 / 1,058 / 1,066 / 1,004), and Table 5 silently uses 1994–**2011**.
- **(added by verifier — the sharpest open flank for us) His own year-by-year evidence says the liquidity gap is pre-2010.** Comparing targets and non-targets within each year (Figure 6), "future targets have higher stock liquidity during the pre-2010 period" (PDF p. 23) — with ROA the parallel qualifier is "pre-2000 period only", and dividend initiations, investment and market cap become insignificant altogether under within-year comparison. The pooled probit (R1) and the matched cross-section (R5) therefore average over a period in which the effect was present and a period in which it was not. Since our own margin is a **2024** rule change, this is the first thing a referee will point at.
- **The liquidity result is not causally identified either.** Liquidity, institutional ownership and firm size are tightly correlated; the probit has year FE but **no firm or industry fixed effects** (industry enters only through SE clustering). The matched design controls for size, book-to-market, industry and year but not for anything time-varying.
- The 1992 reform, the paper's motivating institutional change, is **outside the sample window** — a referee will ask why 1994 and not 1988.
- Success/goal coding is from dissidents' own proxy statements, i.e. the interested party's narrative.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **Object.** The objects are (i) the *probability of a proxy contest*, (ii) *announcement CARs*, and (iii) *corporate policy levels* (leverage, cash, investment, dividends, repurchases). **The takeover premium is never measured, and neither is bidder entry.** [Verifier: the word "premium" occurs exactly **once** in the whole 45-page file, and it is inside a reference-list title (Fos–Jiang 2014, "Inferring private control premium from…", PDF p. 29). "Schedule 13D", "13D" and "13(d)" occur **zero** times, as do "filing window", "five business days" and "beneficial owner". The card's scope claims here are confirmed exactly.] Takeover appears twice and only as background: as one of 1,061 contest *outcomes* ("Firm delisted or taken over", 60 contests, 6%, Table 4) and as the substitution narrative that hostile tender offers have been displaced by contests (Figure 2). No bid, no premium, no acquirer, no deal-level data. SDC hostile-bid counts are plotted but never analysed.
- **Margin. None.** Schedule 13D does not appear in the paper. There is no 5% stake threshold, no filing window, no disclosure rule of any kind. The only regulatory change discussed (1992 proxy reform; 2010 proxy access) is **narrative context outside the estimation window**, and is never used for identification. This is the cleanest case in the assigned set of a liquidity–activism paper with **zero disclosure-rule margin**.
- **Identification.** Descriptive census + probit + within-firm panel with a generated regressor. **No instrument, no exogenous shock, no DiD.** The author rules himself out: "one needs to find an instrument for the likelihood of a proxy contest to show that the relation is also causal" (PDF p. 6) and "finding an instrument for proxy contests at the firm level is hard, if not impossible" (PDF p. 26, fn. 11). Causal interpretation is named as future work: "Future research could provide a causal interpretation to this result." (PDF p. 27).
- **No trading model and no stake data.** He observes neither the activist's purchases nor prices during accumulation; the liquidity channel is inferred from firm-year Amihud and from citing Collin-Dufresne–Fos.
- **(added by verifier) He goes further than "no takeover premium" — he denies that the modern environment is about control at all.** On the strength of the 81% non-control share he writes that "The current corporate governance environment therefore resembles the 'market for corporate influence' and not the 'market for corporate control.'" (PDF p. 3). That is a scope statement *against* a control-outcome object, made by the closest thing we have to an ally on liquidity. It is an interpretation of counts, not a test, and his own Table 5 shows "Sale of Target Company" as a stated goal in 270 contests (27%) and Table 4 shows 60 contests (6%) ending in delisting or takeover — but the sentence exists and will be quoted at us.
- **No theory.** Kyle–Vila (1991), Bolton–von Thadden (1998), Maug (1998), Fos–Kahn (2015) and Grossman–Hart (1982) are cited as motivation for the empirical patterns; nothing is derived or tested against a model.

## 7. Implications for our position

1. **This is the strongest published within-US evidence that liquidity drives activism, and it is exactly our κ.** R1/R2/R5/R7: liquid firms get targeted more; matched targets are more liquid; targets *become* more liquid in the year before the contest. The magnitude (R2) is unusually usable — a one-SD liquidity increase moves contest probability by 24% of its base rate. Where Becht et al.'s cross-country liquidity effect dies under country fixed effects, Fos's survives industry-clustered inference within the US. **If our empirical leg needs a prior on the sign and size of the liquidity→activism channel, this is the citation — with one caveat that must travel with it (added by verifier): his own within-year evidence puts the target/non-target liquidity gap in the *pre-2010* period only (R20, PDF p. 23). Cite R1/R2/R5/R7 for the average effect, and pre-empt the period question rather than waiting for it.**
2. **He hands us the Maug lineage as an empirical fact, not just a theory.** Q2 below is Fos citing Kyle–Vila, Bolton–von Thadden, Maug, Collin-Dufresne–Fos *as the theoretical justification for his own liquidity coefficient*. That sentence lets us open the paper with an established liquidity–activism link and spend our novelty budget on the disclosure rule instead of re-litigating κ.
3. **The threat channel is the object we should adopt for "control outcome", and he shows how to measure it.** R10/R11 say firms change policy when contest *risk* rises, including firms never targeted. Translated into our vocabulary: the *option* to engage disciplines even in the pooled state. This matters for our comparative statics — if a stricter disclosure rule lowers the value of that option, the attenuation shows up in never-targeted firms, not only in flagged ones. It also names the right empirical design (fitted-probability × within-firm) and its weakness (generated regressor, jointly determined policies) so we do not repeat it naively.
4. **He vacates the disclosure margin completely — and says so by omission.** Object = contest probability and policy levels; margin = **none, Schedule 13D absent from the paper**; identification = **association, author-disclaimed**. Of the three papers on this card set, Fos is the one that has our driving variable (liquidity) and our outcome family (control), and has **no disclosure rule at all**. That intersection — liquidity, control outcome, *and* a dated margin with exogenous variation — is our position, and Fos is the paper that most sharply defines it by not occupying it.
5. **His own stated limitation is our stated contribution.** Q4 and Q5: he needs exogenous variation in intervention risk and says it is "hard, if not impossible" to find at the firm level. The **2024-02-05 window acceleration** is exactly such variation, applied at the *rule* level rather than the firm level. Our position can be written as: Fos identified the object (the threat) and confessed the missing instrument; the window margin supplies one.
6. **A caution on his CARs.** R12–R14 are figure-only in the version we hold. Do **not** cite "6.5%" as an estimate with a standard error without first checking the published Management Science article. Prefer his probit and panel numbers (R1–R11), which carry t-statistics and clustered SEs.
7. **A useful mechanism detail for the premium wedge.** R13: contests whose stated goal is *selling the company* jump ~10% at announcement (vs 6.5%) but then **drift down**, ending level with the rest at +24 months; contests about *business strategy* start far lower and gain the most over two years. If our premium wedge is the engaged-vs-unengaged gap in expected takeover value, this says the announcement-date gap and the long-run gap are different objects — and the sale-motivated announcement effect is partly transitory.
