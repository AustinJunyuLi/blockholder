## 1. Question

Does shareholder activism generate positive *net* returns for the activist once the private costs of monitoring are subtracted? The prior literature (Brav et al. 2008; Klein–Zur 2009; Clifford 2008; Becht et al. 2008) measured gross activist returns and ignored campaign costs, so it "may have overestimated the returns generated by activism" (PDF p. 3). Gantchev's answer requires a *cost function* for activism, which is unobservable: only proxy-fight costs have anecdotal estimates, and negotiation and board-seat costs have none. He recovers the whole cost schedule from activists' revealed continue/exit decisions.

## 2. Model / data and method

**The decision model.** Activism is modelled as an escalating *sequence* of tactics, `n ∈ {0,1,2,3}` = (0) 13D intent with no specific demands, (1) demand negotiations, (2) board representation, (3) (threatened) proxy contest. An activist escalates only after the cheaper tactic fails. At each node he compares continuation utility to exit-at-market utility:

- `U^cont_in = −c_{n+1} + p_{i,n+1}E(α_{i,n+1}V_{i,n+1}) + (1−p_{i,n+1})E[max(α_{i,n+1}M_{i,n+1}, U^cont_{i,n+1})]`, `U^exit_in = α_in M_in` (PDF p. 12, Eq. 1).
- Rearranged, the break-even constraint collapses to **two** explanatory variables: the expected gross return from a successful campaign `V_i/M_i`, and the (inverse) marked-to-market value of the stake `1/(α_i M_i)` (PDF p. 12, Eq. 4). Costs are assumed fixed per stage across activists (relaxed later with activist fixed effects).

**Estimation.** Random-utility / logit assumptions (i.i.d. Type-I extreme value errors) turn each break-even constraint into a conditional binary logit, `log(continue/exit) = β̂_{1n}·1/(α_in M_in) + β̂_{2n}·V_in/M_in` (PDF p. 14, Eq. 12), estimated by **statistical backward induction** (Bas–Signorino–Walker 2008): start at the last node (proxy), then substitute the estimated proxy cost into the board-representation constraint, then into the negotiations constraint. Standard errors from the recursive substitution are biased, so bias-corrected **nonparametric bootstrap** CIs are reported.

**Identification (the point the positioning stage should note).** The first logit coefficient gives *relative* stage cost, `β̂_{1n} = λ̂_n = (stage cost function)/(stage continuation probability)` (PDF p. 15, Eq. 13). Logit is under-identified in scale (`σ_ε`), so *absolute* magnitudes are not recovered by the regression alone. Gantchev **fixes the scale by imposing the theoretical restriction that the coefficient on `V_i/M_i` equals one** — the break-even condition says it must — which pins down `σ_ε` per stage and hence the dollar level of costs (PDF p. 16). Identification therefore rests on (a) the break-even condition being the true decision rule and (b) the measurement of `V_i`.

**Target fundamental value `V_i`.** Estimated as the valuation of a matched peer with similar fundamentals, following Edmans–Goldstein–Jiang (2012): a **censored quantile regression** (Powell 1984 CLAD) of the target's Q ratio on tercile ranks of five characteristics assumed unaffected by the activist — size (log sales), asset turnover, market share, sales growth, R&D/sales. The improvement parameter is **calibrated to the median realised value improvement of successful activists, 35.38%** (PDF p. 17). Alternative specification: best-performing 3-digit-SIC industry peer in the same value tercile (Table 9 Panel B).

**Data.** SEC Schedule 13D + amendments, preliminary and definitive contested proxy statements (PREC 14A, PREN 14A, DFAN 14A, DEFN 14A), and SharkRepellent.net. Period **2000–2007**. Filer list built from ~5,000 Dow Jones-reported 13D filings, verified against FT/Alpha/Infovest21/Greenwood–Schor lists and Factiva → ~200 funds grouped into **129 hedge fund families**. Final sample: **1,164 distinct campaigns, 5,645 individual filings, 171 hedge funds**; unique targets reported as **1,023** on PDF pp. 6 and 21 but as **1,164** on PDF pp. 21–22 and in Table 1 Panel B (whose column header is "Targets") — an internal inconsistency, and the two conflicting figures sit on the *same page* (PDF p. 21) [page cites corrected by verifier]. CRSP-Compustat merged subsample: **4,610 filings, 953 unique targets**. 21 sub-5% ("nonpublic") campaigns from Brav et al. (2008) are added. 85 of 129 fund families matched to Thomson Reuters 13F for return calculations; returns follow Griffin–Xu (2009) monthly-from-quarterly-holdings.

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **Schedule 13D threshold margin: 5%.** "Schedule 13D must be filed by any person or group that acquires more than 5% of the voting stock of a public company with the intention of influencing its operations or management" (PDF p. 19; also pp. 7, 9, 37).
- **Schedule 13D filing window: ten days** — the pre-2024 window. "must be submitted to the US Securities and Exchange Commission within ten days by any investor who acquires ownership of 5% of the voting stock of a public company" (PDF p. 37, Appendix A).
- **Amendment rule.** Body text: "the SEC requires an amended filing within ten days of any material change in the amount or intent of ownership, which allows me to track the evolution of each campaign through its stages" (PDF p. 19). Appendix A instead says a material change (>1% ownership change, change of intent, change of preferred communication method) "requires a **prompt** amendment" (PDF p. 37). These two statements of the amendment rule are inconsistent; the amendment timing is load-bearing for his stage-tracking, so the Appendix wording is the safer one to cite.
- **Item 4 of Schedule 13D** ("Purpose of Transaction") is the demand-classification source; its ten enumerated actions are reproduced in full (PDF p. 38). **Item 7** (exhibits — letters to management) is called the second most important section (PDF p. 37).
- **Schedule 13G** described as the passive alternative: "Activist practices are not permitted by 13G filers unless they re-file as 13D investors" (PDF p. 38).
- **The below-threshold blind spot is acknowledged and patched by hand.** 21 sub-5% campaigns are imported from Brav et al. (2008); footnote 21 concedes "these events represent a nonrandom sample of campaigns below the 5% filing threshold because they involve large and newsworthy targets with above-average press coverage" (PDF p. 20).
- **Poison-pill / special-meeting thresholds:** "85% of Standard and Poor's (S&P) 500 companies impose ownership thresholds above 25% to call a special meeting or do not give their shareholders that right" (PDF p. 34).
- **The only price-reaction-to-exit fact in the paper (added by verifier).** Footnote 12 (PDF p. 11) justifies the exit branch of the decision tree by borrowing an *exit event study* from elsewhere: "Note also that an unsuccessful exit by an activist is typically penalized by the market. Brav et al. (2008) show that the market reacts very negatively to hedge fund exits in failed campaigns resulting in negative abnormal returns of -4% in the (-20, +20) window around exit." He runs no exit event study of his own; the exit payoff in Eq. (1) is simply `α_in M_in`, the market price, with no price impact from the activist's own sale.
- **Ownership ceiling used in robustness (added by verifier).** The 95th percentile of activist ownership in the sample is **16%**, "below the level required to influence company procedures relying on shareholder voting"; excluding those campaigns leaves the cost estimates unchanged (PDF p. 34).
- **Institutional ownership is the one investor-base variable he uses (added by verifier).** In an unreported success regression he states "Institutional ownership is an important determinant of success in the more confrontational stages of activism" (PDF p. 34), and Table 6 shows targets have *higher* institutional ownership than matched non-targets (+0.126***, SE 0.007; PDF p. 50).
- Data sources: SEC EDGAR (13D, PREC/PREN/DFAN/DEFN 14A), SharkRepellent.net, CRSP/Compustat, Thomson Reuters 13F, I/B/E/S, SDC Platinum (104 acquisitions of activist targets identified).

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- The first — and still the standard — dollar benchmark for the *private* cost of each rung of the voice ladder. Every later paper that needs a cost of voice cites these three numbers.
- The identification trick is clean and cheap: the theory says the coefficient on `V/M` is one, so imposing it buys the scale parameter that logit cannot deliver. No distributional assumptions on costs are needed — he says so explicitly (PDF p. 15).
- Hand-collected campaign-stage data at a granularity nobody had before (5,645 filings coded into a four-node sequence, with target reactions and per-demand outcomes).
- Honest about the bootstrap: recursive substitution biases the analytic SEs, so bias-corrected bootstrap CIs are used and compared to normal CIs.
- The takeover robustness (Table 11) is a genuine test of Greenwood–Schor, not a throwaway, and it reverses their headline once costs and demands are controlled for.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **Costs are identified off a maintained decision rule, not off observed expenditure.** Nothing in the data is a cost. If activists exit for reasons outside the break-even condition (fund-flow shocks, mandate limits, redemption pressure), those exits are read as high costs. The estimates are a lower bound on cost thresholds under his model, not a measurement.
- **`V_i` is a calibrated construct.** The 35.38% calibration target is itself computed from the *successful* campaigns in the same sample, so the reward measure and the outcome measure share data. He argues this makes the estimation "consistent by construction" (PDF p. 18); a referee can equally call it circular.
- **Model fit is weak in exactly the stage where the costs are largest in aggregate.** McFadden R² is 17.90% and only 58.09% of exits are correctly classified in the demand-negotiation stage, on N=241.
- **Tiny estimation samples at the deep nodes:** N=63 for the proxy-stage logit, which is where the $5.94M (and hence the whole backward induction) originates.
- **No standard errors on the cost point estimates**, only bootstrap intervals; and the intervals are wide (proxy: 3.04–10.86, a 3.6× range).
- **Selection into the sample is the 5% threshold.** Below-threshold activism enters only through 21 hand-picked, admittedly non-random press events. His "stage 0" (13D filed, no demands) is therefore the *legal* first move, not the economic one.
- **No liquidity variable anywhere.** Maug (1998) appears only in a literature footnote (PDF p. 4). Trading, price impact, and stake accumulation are entirely absent — ownership is treated as static ("activists do not significantly increase ownership during a campaign", PDF p. 25).
- Sample stops in **2007**; nothing on the post-crisis activism wave, on wolf packs, or on any disclosure-rule change.
- Success is coded from the activist's own 13D narrative and SharkRepellent, i.e. self-reported by the party with the incentive to claim success.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **Object.** The object is the *activist's private net return* and the *private cost of each tactic*. It is **not** the takeover premium, not bidder entry, and not the target shareholders' gain from control. Takeover enters only as a binary M&A/no-M&A partition of campaigns (104 acquisitions from SDC) used to test Greenwood–Schor — and the effect dies once costs and stated demands are netted out (PDF p. 35, Table 11).
- **Margin.** The **threshold margin (5%) is used purely as a sampling frame**, never as a variable. The **window margin (ten days) is stated as background in Appendix A and never used**. There is no variation in either margin — the whole sample sits under one unchanging US rule. No disclosure-rule change is studied, no cross-country threshold comparison, no event around any rule.
- **Identification.** Structural in the narrow sense (a decision model whose parameters are backed out of choices), but with **no exogenous variation and no control group**. There is no DiD, no instrument, no natural experiment. He explicitly warns: "These results need to be interpreted with caution as the decision to continue an activist engagement is endogenous to each campaign" (PDF p. 24).
- **Liquidity is out of scope entirely** — no Amihud measure, no turnover, no bid–ask spread, no price impact. Costs are assumed "independent of campaign characteristics" (PDF p. 11), i.e. deliberately not a function of any market variable.
- **Trading and stake dynamics are assumed away**, and he defends the assumption: "The empirical evidence in Section 5 supports the modeling assumption that the activist would not use ownership strategically to exert more pressure on the target. Hence, I drop the stage subscript for α" (PDF p. 12, fn. 14).
- **Firm-side value and welfare are out.** He measures what the activist earns, not what minority shareholders gain.

## 7. Implications for our position

1. **It hands us a calibrated cost of voice, and nothing else.** Our core model needs a cost of engaging (and a *higher* cost of the flagged, public rung). Gantchev gives exactly that shape: quiet negotiation $2.94M < board seat $1.83M increment < public proxy fight $5.94M, i.e. **the public rung is the expensive one**, and the total ladder is $10.71M. This is the empirical anchor for a model where *public voice* costs strictly more than *quiet voice*. Cite R1–R4.
2. **It vacates the whole liquidity × disclosure-rule × control-outcome cell.** Object = activist net return (not control outcome). Margin = none (5% is a sampling frame, ten days is background prose). Identification = structural decision model with no exogenous variation. Liquidity: absent. He occupies the "private cost of voice, US, 2000–2007, structural" cell and leaves the partition, the window margin, and the premium wedge untouched.
3. **His takeover result is a constraint we must respect, and it is friendlier to us than it looks.** R13–R14: the raw M&A-vs-no-M&A return gap (23.77%) is real but disappears once you (a) subtract costs and (b) condition on the activist's stated demands. Read our way: *what the activist announced he was doing* explains the takeover-linked return, not the takeover per se. That is an announcement/partition story, and it is consistent with a model in which the flagged state carries the information. If we claim liquidity moves takeover premia through disclosure, Gantchev is evidence that the *stated purpose channel* (13D Item 4) dominates the mechanical takeover channel — but he also warns us that gross-return results on takeover subsamples are fragile to cost netting.
4. **It supplies the "activism is deterred at the margin" fact our comparative statics need.** Only 6.36% of campaigns reach a proxy fight while proxy fights succeed 57.38% of the time (R11, R12). That gap — most successful tactic, least used — is exactly the deterrence-by-cost wedge a model of voice choice wants, and he draws the policy conclusion himself (proxy access, PDF p. 7).
5. **His identification strategy is a template we could reuse and should be careful with.** If v4 ever needs to price the cost of crossing the disclosure threshold, the Gantchev move — impose a theoretical coefficient restriction to fix the logit scale — is available. But the referee objection (costs are read off a maintained decision rule) transfers with it. Our advantage over Gantchev is that we *have* a dated rule change (2024-02-05) and he has none; we should not give that advantage away by adopting a design with no exogenous variation.
6. **(added by verifier) The control-outcome margin is not incidental to his data — it is the modal demand.** Table 3 Panel A: *sale of the company to a third party* is the single largest stated demand (159 campaigns, 31.55%, success 32.08%), and activists bid for the target themselves in a further 10.52% (52 campaigns, success 40.38%) — "Hedge fund activists not only demand a sale of their targets to third parties but also bid for the targets themselves in more than 10% of the campaigns" (PDF p. 23). So the paper's own data say roughly **two in five flagged campaigns are about who owns the firm**, even though the paper's object is the activist's private return. That is the strongest in-sample evidence that a control-outcome model of 13D activism is the right object — and it is a fact Gantchev reports but does not exploit. It also sharpens his Table 11 result: what predicts the return is the *announced* objective, not the realised M&A, which is exactly a partition/announcement story.
7. **Vocabulary mapping.** His four-stage ladder (no-demands / negotiations / board / proxy) is *not* our Exit–Hold–Quiet voice–Public voice partition: his stage 0 is already a 13D filer, so his entire ladder lives inside our *flagged* state. Our quiet voice (engagement below the threshold) is precisely the region his data cannot see — the 21 hand-picked press events (PDF p. 20). That blind spot is our whitespace, stated in his own words.

**Omissions added by the verifier (each material to a liquidity × disclosure × control-outcome position):**
1. **R19 / §7 pt. 6 — the demand distribution (Table 3 Panel A, PDF p. 47; text p. 23).** A sale of the company to a third party is the *modal* demand (159 campaigns, 31.55%, success 32.08%) and activists bid for the target themselves in a further 10.52% (52 campaigns, success 40.38%); opposition to a proposed merger is another 12.50%. The card treated takeover as a side robustness test; in Gantchev's own data the control outcome is the biggest single thing 13D activists ask for. This is the strongest in-sample support for making the control outcome the object.
2. **R20 — target characteristics (Table 6, PDF p. 50).** Targets are small (mean/median market cap $868.54M / $155.22M; matched difference −$1,313.50\*\*\*), cheap (Q matched diff −0.374\*\*\*), thinly covered (analysts −0.355\*) but **more institutionally held** (+0.126\*\*\*). The card said "no liquidity variable" — true — but the paper does carry an investor-base measure, and it points the opposite way from a naive "activists target illiquid neglected firms" prior. Any calibration of our κ against Gantchev's sample has to reckon with this.
3. **§4 — footnote 12 (PDF p. 11), the −4% exit reaction.** The paper's exit branch is justified by importing Brav et al.'s −4% CAR in the (−20,+20) window around exits from failed campaigns. Gantchev himself models exit as sale *at* the market price `α_in M_in` with no own-trade price impact — i.e. the exit channel is present in his decision tree but assumed frictionless, which is precisely the assumption our κ relaxes. The card omitted both the fact and the assumption.
4. **§4 — the 16% ownership ceiling and the voting-power gap (PDF p. 34).** The 95th percentile of activist ownership is 16%, explicitly "below the level required to influence company procedures relying on shareholder voting"; dropping those campaigns changes nothing. Combined with the S&P-500 25% special-meeting fact the card already has, this says the whole sample lives strictly between the 5% disclosure threshold and any control threshold — the pure "voice without control" region.
5. **§4 — institutional ownership as a success determinant (PDF p. 34, unreported).** "Institutional ownership is an important determinant of success in the more confrontational stages of activism." Unreported, so ASSERTED, but it is the one place the paper concedes that the *composition of the float* moves campaign outcomes.
