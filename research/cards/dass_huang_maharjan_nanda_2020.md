## 1. Question

Does the *liquidity of the acquirer's stock* change the terms on which corporate control changes hands? The authors propose an "acquisition currency" hypothesis: target shareholders who expect to trade after the deal prefer to be paid in liquid stock, so they will accept a lower price for it. That makes acquirer liquidity — relative to the target's — a determinant of whether a firm acquires at all, whether it pays in stock, how large a premium it pays, and how the market reacts. The paper then argues that firms know this and deliberately manufacture liquidity (splits, earnings guidance) before stock deals.

## 2. Model / data and method

**The model (Appendix B, printed pp. 37–41).** Three dates. Acquirer A and target T are both public. Target shareholders are of two types: atomistic holders who hold to the terminal date, and large investors owning fraction α who face a liquidity shock with probability π and must liquidate at date 1 at a price discount λ (λ is written as a "liquidity parameter" but is in fact an *il*liquidity parameter — p. 38 says so explicitly). Post-merger illiquidity is λ* = λ_AT − f(c), where λ_AT = ωλ_A + (1−ω)λ_T is the value-weighted average of the two pre-merger illiquidities (ω = V_A/(V_A+V_T)), and f(c) is the improvement bought by spending c on disclosure/splits, with f(0)=0, f′>0, f′′<0. Merger surplus in a stock deal is ΔV_S = V₀ + απ(λ_T − λ*) (E-1). Boards Nash-bargain with **equal bargaining power**, giving

> P{Stock} = V_T + ½ (V₀ + απ(λ_T − λ*))   (E-3, printed p. 39)

so the **premium is ½(V₀ + απ(λ_T − λ*))**, decreasing in acquirer liquidity and increasing in target liquidity. Optimal liquidity investment satisfies L·f′(c*) = 1 (E-5). In a cash deal P{Cash} = V_T + ½(V₀′ − κ_T), which does **not** depend on acquirer liquidity — this asymmetry is the paper's identifying prediction, the one that separates it from the governance and valuation channels.

**Liquidity measures (Appendix A, printed p. 34).** Two, both daily-data-based and both multiplied by **−1** so that higher = more liquid:
1. **Amihud (2002) illiquidity ratio** (`AMH`): ln(AvgILLIQ × 10⁹), AvgILLIQ = yearly mean of |return| / dollar volume.
2. **Relative bid-ask spread** (`Spread`): ln of the yearly mean of (Ask − Bid)/((Ask+Bid)/2) from daily closing quotes.
**`Relative Liquidity` = acquirer liquidity − target liquidity**, both measured as of year t−1. Sample SDs: 2.123 (AMH) and 0.934 (Spread) over the 3,032 public-target deals (Table 1 Panel D, printed p. 44).

**Samples.** SDC US M&A, announced 1 Jan 1985 – 31 Dec 2018, completed by Oct 2019; public listed acquirer with Compustat/CRSP data; >50% of target acquired; public or private target (subsidiaries excluded); deal value ≥ $50m. Final sample **10,627 deals**, of which **3,032 involve public targets** (printed p. 11). The firm-year panel for the acquisitiveness tests is **15,670 firms / 142,053 firm-years, 1984–2018**. Premium regressions run on **1,536 stock deals and 940 cash deals** (Table 8 Panel A); the blockholder-interaction versions on 1,205 and 822.

**Deal definitions (printed p. 34).** A "stock" deal = stock is ≥60% of consideration; "cash" deal = cash ≥60%; "hybrid" = both between 40% and 60%. **Deal Premium** = effective offer price as a percentage premium over the target's market price **two days before** the announcement. Mean premium 0.268, SD 0.383, median 0.253 (N = 2,837).

**Identification — five strategies, none of them decimalization alone:**
1. **OLS / linear probability**, industry × year FE (Fama-French 48), SEs clustered by firm (eq. 1).
2. **DiD around decimalization (2001)** — NYSE and Amex moved to decimal pricing in January 2001, NASDAQ in April 2001. Firms are ranked on the change in liquidity from year −1 to year +1 and split into terciles; top tercile = treated, bottom = control (1,708/1,729 firms for AMH; 1,698/1,729 for Spread), then **nearest-neighbour propensity-score matched**, caliper 0.01, no replacement → **671 pairs (AMH), 746 pairs (Spread)**. `Decimal` = 1 for 2002 onward, 0 for 2000 and earlier, **2001 dropped**. Window [−3,+3]. Balance checks: post-match probit pseudo-R² <1%, χ² p-value 1.00, no significant pre-period differences. Parallel-trend test with leads and lags in Panel E.
3. **DiD around the 1997 minimum tick-size shift** from $1/8 to $1/16 (phased 7 May – 24 June 1997 on the three major exchanges); `Shift` = 1 from 1998, 0 for 1996 and earlier. 1,024 pairs (AMH), 998 (Spread).
4. **Actual change in liquidity** around each event (eq. 3, Fang-Noe-Tice / Edmans-Fang-Zur style long-difference).
5. **Low-price stratification** of decimalization (eq. 4, following Edmans, Fang & Zur 2013): the tick-size change bites harder on low-priced stocks, so `Low Price × Decimal` is the coefficient of interest. **This is the only identification strategy that reaches the premium regressions** — the DiD cannot, because too few acquirers of public targets appear both before and after the shock (body text and footnote 27, **printed p. 23** — page corrected by verifier). *(verifier: the **19** matched treated acquirers in fn. 27 is the count for the **stock-payment-fraction** DiD; the footnote then says "The count is even smaller for the premium and CAR tests later since we need to further split this subsample based on the payment method, stock or cash." So the premium DiD is infeasible on **fewer than 19** matched acquirers, not 19.)*
Plus an **IV** for the liquidity-enhancement test: `Stockacq(t+1)` instrumented by the number of M&As that year in the same FF-48 industry (printed pp. 27–28).

**Sign conventions worth keeping straight.** Both liquidity measures are multiplied by −1, so a *positive* coefficient means *more liquidity*. In the model, λ is illiquidity. `Relative Liquidity` is acquirer minus target, so a negative premium coefficient means **more acquirer liquidity relative to the target ⇒ lower premium**, which is the paper's claim.

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **Decimalization**: NYSE and Amex switched from fractional to decimal pricing in **January 2001**; NASDAQ in **April 2001** (printed p. 15). Cited as narrowing spreads and improving market quality (Bessembinder 2003; Furfine 2003).
  ⚠️ **Internal inconsistency to flag**: the introduction (printed p. 2) says "the introduction of decimalization in **2002**". The body, the `Decimal` dummy definition (=1 from 2002, =0 through 2000, 2001 dropped) and the variable appendix are all consistent with the true 2001 event. Treat the p. 2 sentence as a typo, and do not quote it.
- **Minimum tick-size shift**: $1/8 → $1/16 on the three major US exchanges, **7 May – 24 June 1997** (printed p. 19), citing Chordia, Roll & Subrahmanyam (2008).
- **Blockholder definition = 5% or more ownership**, counted in the quarter before the deal announcement (printed p. 35). This is the US Schedule 13D/13G threshold used purely as an ownership screen — **the paper never mentions the filing rule, the filing window, or Schedule 13D by name.** Data are quarterly institutional holdings; there is no filing-date variable anywhere.
- **Premium reference price** = target market price **two trading days before** the announcement (printed p. 34).
- Data sources: Thomson One SDC (deals, payment, tender-offer flag), Compustat/CRSP (accounting, returns, liquidity inputs), I/B/E/S (analyst coverage), **First Call** for earnings guidance (guidance sample limited to **1994–2018** by coverage, printed p. 27).
- Financial-constraint indices: Kaplan-Zingales (1997), Whited-Wu (2006), Hadlock-Pierce (2010), all defined in Appendix A.
- **(added by verifier) Clustering in the premium regressions.** Table 8 (both panels) reports "Robust standard errors … clustered by Year x Industry" (printed pp. 56, 58) — the **same** dimension as the fixed effects, and different from the firm-level clustering used for the firm-year panel (footnote 19, printed p. 13). The headline SEs 0.011 and 0.014 are therefore clustered at the FE cell, which is a live referee objection on precisely the coefficient we would cite.
- **(added by verifier) Which standard deviation the headline uses.** The −4.46 / −4.67 pp conversion multiplies the Table 8 coefficients by the SDs of `Relative Liquidity` in the **full 3,032-deal public-target sample** (Table 1 Panel D, printed p. 44), not by the SD in the **1,536-deal stock subsample** the regression actually runs on. The CAR conversion on printed p. 28 does not use those SDs (see §5), which implies the stock-subsample AMH SD is nearer 1.6 than 2.123 — and if so the honest AMH premium magnitude is nearer **3.4 pp** than 4.46 pp. Another reason to cite the effect qualitatively.
- **(added by verifier) Voluntary disclosure, stated as such.** Prediction 3's mechanism is that acquirers "improve their transparency in the stock market by **disclosing more information than what regulations mandate** (e.g., providing more informative earnings guidance)" (printed p. 8). That phrase is the cleanest available statement of the chosen-versus-forced disclosure line our §6 draws.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- A single mechanism generates four predictions with an unusually sharp discriminating test: **the effect must appear in stock deals and vanish in cash deals**. Both the governance channel and the valuation channel predict target liquidity affects the premium regardless of payment mode; only the currency channel predicts the mode-dependence. The paper argues this explicitly (printed pp. 5, 10–11) and the cash-deal nulls are clean. **(added by verifier)** The argument is sharper than "discriminating test": at printed p. 10 they state the exclusion restriction outright — "under the governance channel, the relation between target liquidity and premium should not depend on whether the acquisition is paid in stock or in cash" — which makes payment-mode dependence the *definition* of their channel and payment-mode independence the signature of ours. See §7.1a.
- The economic magnitude is internally consistent and checkable: −0.021 × 2.123 = −4.46 pp; −0.050 × 0.934 = −4.67 pp. The paper's headline number reproduces from its own table.
- Two independent liquidity shocks (1997 and 2001) plus four estimation strategies on each, with propensity-score matching, balance diagnostics and an explicit parallel-trends test.
- Prediction 3 (firms manufacture liquidity before stock deals) is a genuinely non-obvious implication, and it is where the paper earns its keep relative to the "liquidity is good" literature.
- The blockholder interaction is a real cross-sectional prediction, not a fishing expedition — the horizon story is in the model.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **The premium result is not identified by the quasi-natural experiments.** The DiD is confessedly infeasible for the premium sample (footnote 27, printed p. 23: **19** matched treated acquirers for the stock-payment test and "even smaller" for the premium and CAR tests once the sample is split by payment method). The premium therefore rests on cross-sectional OLS (Table 8) plus one low-price stratification (Table 6 Panel B). A referee can say the headline number is an OLS correlation with year × industry fixed effects, and would be right.
- **Adjusted R² of 0.050 and 0.048** in the premium regressions. Almost nothing about the premium is explained; the liquidity coefficient is a small piece of a mostly unexplained object.
- **Selection into stock versus cash is the whole design and is not modelled empirically.** Payment mode is an outcome of the same liquidity variable (Prediction 1) and is then used to split the premium sample (Prediction 2). Conditioning on a treated outcome is exactly the selection problem the paper's own theory predicts should exist. There is no Heckman correction, no bounds, no discussion.
- **The Online Appendix is missing from our copy**, which matters most for the acquirer/target separate-liquidity specification — the result that most directly answers "does target liquidity alone predict premia".
- Equal Nash bargaining power is assumed, not derived or estimated. The premium is mechanically half the surplus, so every comparative static is a statement about the surplus, not about bargaining.
- λ_AT as a value-weighted average of pre-merger illiquidities is admitted to be a convenience assumption (footnote 3, printed p. 38: obtainable "under restrictive assumptions" in a Glosten-Milgrom setting; the model is "available from the authors", i.e. not in the paper).
- Winsorisation at 1%/99% is aggressive for CARs: footnote 18 (printed p. 12) reports the raw CAR range is −63% to +59% and the post-winsorisation range is −21% to +15%.
- Two small arithmetic checks that do not tie cleanly: the CAR text says 0.47%–0.64% per SD, but 0.004 × 2.123 (AMH) = 0.85% and 0.005 × 0.934 (Spread) = 0.47%. Footnote 36 reveals the printed 0.005 is really 0.00527, so the Spread number ties; the AMH number appears to use a different (stock-deal subsample) SD than the one in Table 1 Panel D. Flagged for the verifier.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**Object.** Acquisition **premium** (offer price over the price two days before announcement), payment mode, acquisitiveness, and acquirer announcement CAR — all from the **acquirer's** side of the currency decision. The paper does **not** study bidder entry as a contest, campaign success, activism, or any post-deal governance outcome. It also does not study the *level* of takeover activity as a control-market outcome, only firm-level acquisition propensity.

**Margin.** **None.** There is no disclosure rule in this paper at any margin. "Blockholder" appears only as a *count* of 5%-or-more holders taken from quarterly holdings data (printed p. 35), used as a proxy for shareholder horizon. Schedule 13D, the 5% filing trigger *as a rule*, the filing window, and any dated change to either are entirely absent. The only "disclosure" in the paper is **voluntary** — earnings guidance and stock splits as liquidity-manufacturing actions (Prediction 3), and the paper says so in the strongest available words: acquirers improve transparency "by disclosing more information than what regulations mandate" (printed p. 8). That distinction matters for us: they study *chosen* disclosure as a liquidity input; we study *forced* disclosure as a partition.

**Identification.** DiD with propensity-score matching around two tick-size events, long-difference on realised liquidity change, and low-price stratification — **but for the premium object specifically, only OLS and the low-price stratification.** No structural estimation. No theory beyond the reduced-form Appendix B.

**What they say they leave out.** The body text of §4.2.2 and footnote 27 (**printed p. 23**) are the explicit scope confession on identification for the premium: the DiD "would require a sample of acquirers that acquire public targets both before and after decimalization (or shift in 1997), which results in too small a sample to yield any meaningful inferences." Footnote 26 (printed p. 22) notes they do not examine private acquirers "due to the lack of data on their characteristics". Footnote 34 (printed p. 29) leaves anti-takeover provisions out of the main test for data-coverage reasons. Several results are "available upon request" rather than shown (hybrid-payment deals, alternative stock/cash definitions, non-winsorised results, the Yan-Zhang turnover measure of investor horizon).

## 7. Implications for our position

**What this paper occupies:** object = **takeover premium** (plus payment mode, acquisitiveness, acquirer CAR); margin = **none**; identification = tick-size DiD for the propensity results, **OLS only for the premium**. Mechanism = *target shareholders' expected future trading costs*.

**7.1 It is the closest thing to a competitor on our headline object, and it does not occupy our whitespace.**
This is the one paper in the set that puts liquidity and the takeover premium in the same regression and gets a signed, sized answer. Any referee reading our claim "liquidity moves the takeover premium" will ask about it. But the position it occupies is orthogonal to ours in the way that matters: **their liquidity is the acquirer's own stock liquidity, functioning as a currency; ours is κ, the noise-trading intensity in the target's market that governs whether a blockholder can build a position without being detected.** Their channel runs through what the *seller* will accept for a piece of paper. Ours runs through whether a blockholder can accumulate, engage, and be flagged. Different liquidity, different agent, different mechanism. That is a clean, defensible separation and it should be stated in one sentence in draft_v3's related-literature paragraph.

**7.1a (added by verifier) They name our channel as a rival and build a test designed to reject it. This is the single most important thing in the paper for us and the card missed it.**
§2.2 (printed pp. 9–10, previewed at p. 5) sets out two alternative channels the acquisition-currency hypothesis has to beat, and the first is **governance** — stated in exactly our literature's terms: "Greater stock liquidity could also ease the formation of blockholdings and strengthen monitoring (e.g., **Maug, 1998; Edmans, et al., 2013**). On the other hand, greater liquidity could have adverse effects on governance if lower costs of exiting stock positions reduce incentives of blockholders/institutions to monitor (e.g., **Bhide, 1993**)" (printed p. 10). They then concede the governance channel *could* deliver a liquidity–premium relation — "it could be argued that an illiquid target, if it was also poorly governed, would command a lower acquisition premium" — and dispose of it with one discriminating restriction: **"under the governance channel, the relation between target liquidity and premium should not depend on whether the acquisition is paid in stock or in cash"** (printed p. 10).
**What this means for us.** Their cash-deal null is not just a robustness check, it is a *sorting device* that assigns any payment-mode-**in**dependent liquidity–premium relation to the governance family. Our κ story is in that family. So:
- The cash-deal null (R4) is **not evidence against us** — by their own logic it is what separates their channel from ours, and a liquidity–premium effect of ours that shows up in *both* cash and stock deals is the signature of the channel they set aside, not a failed replication of theirs.
- Conversely, a referee holding this paper can say the governance channel is "the one they already considered and found no independent evidence for". We should pre-empt that by pointing at the split they did not run: theirs is *acquirer* liquidity as currency; ours is *target-market* liquidity as accumulation cover, and the two have different predictions for the **flagged** versus **pooled** state, which no channel in their §2.2 has.
- **Write the payment-mode split into our own design from the start** and state in advance which sign we expect in cash deals. That converts their discriminating test from a weapon into a diagnostic we own.

**7.1b (added by verifier) Three antecedents the card should have named, all cited by this paper on printed pp. 5–6.**
- **Roosenboom, Schlingemann & Vasconcelos (2014)** — the existing liquidity × governance × M&A paper. This paper cites it (footnote 5, printed p. 5; again p. 10) as finding acquirer liquidity works through **governance**, with the *sign flipping between public and private targets* because "the monitoring role of voice and exit differs in these cases". That is an exit-versus-voice reading of an M&A return, i.e. our frame applied to our object, and it is already in print. It belongs in the competitor set and probably deserves a card.
- **Massa & Xu (2013)**, *The Value of (Stock) Liquidity in the M&A Market*, **JFQA 48(5), 1463–1497** — cited by this paper at printed p. 6. **It is already in the repo as `research/txt_extracts/massa_xu_2013_jfqa.txt`, and it matters more to us than the paper it is cited inside.** Its object is the **target's** liquidity, not the acquirer's, and it reports that with a public acquirer **a one-standard-deviation increase in target stock liquidity corresponds to a 10% higher offer premium** (plus a 2% higher 3-day and a 5% higher 169-day target return), on US M&A 1987–2007 (printed pp. 1464, 1483). Two consequences:
  1. **The number §7.3 below says is locked inside the missing Table O-5 is published, in hand, and sized.** Read Massa & Xu before spending any effort chasing Dass et al.'s Online Appendix.
  2. **It breaks the discriminating test.** Massa & Xu state that "there is **no significant difference between cash- and stock-financed transactions**" (printed p. 1465). By Dass et al.'s own exclusion restriction at their printed p. 10 — payment-mode dependence is the signature of the currency channel, payment-mode independence the signature of the governance family — Massa & Xu's target-liquidity effect sits in **our** family, not theirs. That is a published, payment-mode-independent, target-side liquidity→premium estimate. **It deserves a card of its own and probably a place in the competitor set.**
- **Officer (2007)** and **Koeplin, Sarin & Shapiro (2000)** — the price **discount** for unlisted targets, "because sellers cannot trade their equity easily" (printed p. 6). This is the incumbent target-side liquidity-and-premium result and it runs the *opposite* way to Massa & Xu; the literature our headline sits in is already internally contested on the target side.

**7.2 The number to quote, exactly as this version prints it.**
Our notes said "−4.5pp per SD in stock deals". **This version prints −4.46 to −4.67 percentage points per one-standard-deviation increase in `Relative Liquidity` (acquirer minus target), in stock deals only, against a mean premium of 26.8% — about 17% of the mean premium (printed p. 25; also p. 3).** The underlying coefficients are −0.021 (SE 0.011) for Amihud and −0.050 (SE 0.014) for the bid-ask spread, N = 1,536 (Table 8 Panel A, printed p. 58). Two cautions before this goes in a draft: (i) the sign is *acquirer* liquidity relative to target, not target liquidity, and (ii) it is not causally identified — the DiD does not reach this regression.

**7.3 Does target liquidity alone predict premia? — Yes, positively, but only as an ASSERTED result in our copy.**
The model says the premium is *increasing* in target illiquidity's mirror, i.e. **a more liquid target commands a higher premium** (E-3, printed pp. 39–40). The text reports exactly that from a specification entering the two liquidities separately: acquirer liquidity negative, target liquidity positive, both significant, in **stock deals only** (printed p. 25 and footnote 3, p. 3). **The table (O-5) is not in this PDF**, so we cannot report a coefficient or a standard error, and we must not imply we can. If we need this number — and we may, because "target liquidity → premium" is much closer to our object than "acquirer liquidity → premium" — the JCF 2024 version or the authors' online appendix has to be obtained. **That is a concrete follow-up — but do §7.1b first: Massa & Xu (2013) is already on disk, it estimates the target-liquidity→premium relation directly (10% higher premium per SD of target liquidity), and it is published.**
Note the sign conflict worth thinking about: their mechanism says an illiquid target is *cheap* (its holders are trapped and value the acquirer's liquid paper). Our model's κ is the target's own noise-trading intensity, and a natural reading of our premium wedge is that liquidity in the target's market changes the blockholder's ability to build a position and hence the probability of engagement. If our comparative static on target liquidity comes out with the same sign as theirs, we should say so and treat it as convergent; if the opposite, we need a sentence explaining why (theirs is a currency-pricing effect at the deal table, ours a pre-deal accumulation effect).

**7.4 Their blockholder interaction is the most useful thing in the paper for us, and it is under-exploited.**
`Relative Liquidity × Blockholder` is **positive and significant** in the premium regression (+0.037***, +0.101***) and **negative and significant** in the payment-mix and CAR regressions. Their reading is horizon: blockholders are long-term and do not value liquidity. But the same interaction is exactly what our model would predict for a different reason — **a target with a 5%-plus holder is a target where a blockholder has already accumulated and (in the US) has already been forced to file.** The presence of a blockholder is our *flagged* state. That this interaction turns off the liquidity-premium relation is at minimum suggestive that the partition matters, and it is a fact our paper can cite and re-interpret rather than contest. Their 5% counting threshold is the Schedule 13D/13G threshold; they use it as a convenience, we use it as the rule.
**Correction by verifier — `Blockholder` in the regression is not a count.** The count of 5%-plus holders (Appendix A, printed p. 35) is only the input: the sample is sorted into **terciles** on that count and `Blockholder` is a **dummy equal to 1 for the top tercile and 0 for the bottom tercile, with the middle tercile dropped** (Table 8 Panel B header, printed p. 58; footnote 29, printed p. 23 — which is why N falls from 1,536 to 1,205). So the paper's own sentence in Q9, "each additional blockholder reduces the sensitivity … by 3.7 to 10.1 percentage points", **cannot be read off that coefficient**: +0.037 and +0.101 are top-tercile-versus-bottom-tercile contrasts in the *slope* on Relative Liquidity, not per-blockholder marginal effects, and unlike the main effect they are not converted through a standard deviation. Quote Q9 only as the authors' own gloss, never as our arithmetic.
Deliverability note: their `Blockholder` input is a count from quarterly institutional holdings, not from filings. Our empirical leg would build the same variable from EDGAR 13D/13G filings, which gives us both the count *and* the filing date — the window margin — which they have no access to at all.

**7.5 Design lessons and warnings for our own empirical leg.**
- **Premium is a legitimate, defensible object.** They use offer price over the price two days pre-announcement. That is the standard and we should match it, not invent one.
- **Payment mode is a first-order confound for any liquidity-premium claim.** Their whole result lives in the stock-deal subsample and vanishes in cash. Any specification of ours that regresses premium on target-market liquidity **must** split by payment mode or control for it, or a referee will say we are picking up the currency channel. Add this to the referee checklist.
- **The tick-size shocks (1997, 2001) are used up.** Fang-Noe-Tice, Edmans-Fang-Zur, Fang-Tian-Tice, Brogaard-Li-Xia and now this paper all run on them. They are also twenty-plus years stale relative to the Feb-2024 acceleration. This strengthens the case for the disclosure-rule anchor rather than a liquidity-shock anchor: the shock we have is *new* and no one has occupied it.
- **Their own power failure is a warning about ours.** The premium DiD died on 19 usable treated acquirers. A liquidity-shock DiD on takeover premia is underpowered because premia only exist for completed public-target deals. Our Feb-2024 design has the same structural risk — 13D filings are frequent, but 13D filings *followed by a takeover with an observable premium* are not. **Compute the MDE before committing** (it is already on the referee checklist; this paper is the evidence for why it matters).
- **Adjusted R² of 0.05 on premium regressions is apparently publishable in this literature.** Useful calibration: we do not need to explain the premium, we need one clean coefficient.
