## 1. Question

What does hedge fund activism actually do? The paper builds the first large-scale, hand-collected census of US hedge fund activism (2001–2006) and asks five questions in order: which firms get targeted; how the market reacts to the Schedule 13D; whether activists achieve their stated objectives; whether they are short-term; and whether target firm performance, CEO pay and turnover, payout and leverage change afterwards. A secondary aim is explicitly a policy one — to answer critics and regulators who claim activists are short-termist value-destroyers.

## 2. Model / data and method

- **Source / sample:** three-step hand construction. (1) Bought the full list of Schedule 13D filers 2001–2006 from LiveEdgar — **11,602 filers** — and manually filtered Item 2 ("Identity and Background") to strip banks, brokerages, corporations, foreign institutions, individuals, insurers, pension funds and trusts, leaving hedge funds, PE/VC and non-fund advisors; classified the residual by web search and, where ambiguous, **by telephoning the filer** (p. 9). **311 hedge funds.** (2) Pulled all 13D and 13D/A filings for those 311 from EDGAR; excluded bankruptcy/distress financing, merger risk arbitrage, and closed-end-fund targets → **236 funds, 1,032 events**. One deliberate exception: Franklin Mutual Advisers, a registered mutual fund, is kept "because it behaves like the other activist funds in our sample" (p. 10). (3) Factiva news searches for motive, target response and resolution; plus a 13F sweep to catch sub-5% activism at firms with market value > $1bn where the fund held > 2%, which added **27 non-13D events** (pp. 10–11).
- **Final N: 236 activist hedge funds, 1,059 hedge fund–target pairs, 882 unique target companies, 2001–2006.** 97% match a CRSP permno, 82% of those have complete return data. Targets span 183 three-digit SIC industries. Funds (events) rise from 39 (97) in 2001 to 126 (252) in 2006 (p. 11).
- **Unit:** hedge fund–target event, coded into five non-mutually-exclusive stated-objective categories and seven tactic categories (Table 1, pp. 42–43).
- **Design / identification: event study + industry × size × book-to-market matched sample + probit targeting model + calendar-time factor-portfolio regressions.** No DiD, no IV, no structural estimation, no theory section. Causal language is hedged throughout.
  - **Announcement window: (−20, +20) trading days** around the 13D filing date; abnormal return = buy-and-hold return in excess of the CRSP value-weighted NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ index (p. 23, Figure 1).
  - Matched sample: same year, same three-digit SIC, same Fama–French 10×10 size × book-to-market portfolio; relaxed to two-digit SIC and 5×5 if no match (p. 19).
  - Long-run: **calendar-time portfolio regressions** on RMRF, SMB, HML and MOM, equal- and value-weighted, for windows from (−12,−10) to (+10,+12) months (Table 6 Panels B–C, p. 50).
  - Operating performance: matched two ways — year-by-year industry/size/BM peer match, and Barber–Lyon (1996) **beginning-of-period performance match** (matched firms within 90–110% of the target's t−2 performance) (pp. 32–33).
- **Outcome coding:** success = the fund achieves its **main stated goal**; partial success = a negotiated settlement partially meeting the goal. On-going events (7.4%) are counted in the denominator, which the authors flag as conservative (footnote 7, p. 40).
- **Exit coding:** first quarter-end at which 13F holdings fall below 1% or $1m; failing that, the last 13D/A date showing the stake below the 5% threshold; supplemented by news search (p. 18).
- **Liquidity measure: the Amihud (2002) illiquidity ratio**, yearly average of 1000·|Return|/(Dollar Trading Volume) from daily data, taken from Hasbrouck's (2006) posted estimates (his "I2" series) (p. 21 and footnote 11, p. 41). **This is the only one of the three activism classics that puts a liquidity measure on the right-hand side.**

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **Threshold + window margin, and Congressional intent:** investors "must file with the SEC within ten days of acquiring more than 5% of any class of securities of a publicly traded company if they have an interest in influencing the management of the company. Congress intended that the filing of a Schedule 13D would notify the market that the filer might seek to force changes or seek control at target companies." Item 4 requires the filer to declare its reasons (p. 9).
- **The most complete statement of the 13D/13G partition in any of the three classics** — footnote 2 (p. 40) lays out four distinct timing rules: passive holders of 5–10% file a 13G *within 45 days of calendar year end*; passive holders crossing 10% file *within ten days after the end of the first month* in which they exceed 10%; a would-be 13D filer with no control intent and ≤20% may elect 13G but then must file *within ten days of crossing 5%*; and "if an institutional investor changes its initial passive purpose and decides to become active, it would need to file a Schedule 13D to announce this shift to the market."
- **What the 13D discloses:** shares, percentage ownership, and all purchases and sales within the previous 60 days. "Exchange traded derivatives must be disclosed, but equity swaps and other OTC derivatives generally do not have to be fully disclosed (Hu and Black, 2006a)." (p. 10).
- **Amendment duty:** the fund must "promptly" file a 13D/A on any "material" change; footnote 8 (p. 40) records that changes of more than 1% are deemed material, and the body notes "some law firms recommend filing within one business day" (p. 30). Footnote 9 (p. 40): the last 13D/A reveals the transactions "that free the hedge fund from future reporting obligations associated with the 5% or more investment."
- **The window is where the pre-filing trading happens.** The abnormal-turnover spike occurs in the ten days *before* the filing, which the authors attribute to the filing fund's own additional buying inside the window (pp. 23–24) — see Q3.
- **The window margin is symmetric: it binds on the way *out* too (added by verifier).** Figure 2 tracks the last 13D/A (the filing that takes the fund below 5%), and "Trading volume tends to spike during the ten-day window leading up to the filing" (p. 29). Because the fund must file "promptly" — "some law firms recommend filing within one business day" — "they would have very little time to sell their block before making a public statement" (pp. 29–30). Entry and exit are both pre-disclosure trading windows; the card's Q4/Q6 cover only the entry side.
- **Post-flag accumulation is documented in their own case narrative (added by verifier).** MLF Investments filed at 5.8% of Alloy, Inc. and "had continuously increased its stake in Alloy from time to time since its initial Schedule 13D filing. By September 7, 2005, MLF Investments owned 16.1% of the company" (pp. 11–12) — accumulation continues *after* the flag, not only inside the ten-day window. (The Klein & Zur card records the same pattern for Pirate Capital.)
- **Sub-5% activism concentrates in large firms (added by verifier).** The 27 non-13D events recovered by the 13F sweep are all at firms with market value > $1bn, and "target companies involved in these events tend to be larger and more mature (lower growth and higher cash flows)" (p. 11). The 5% threshold therefore binds differently by firm size — activism that never trips the flag is a large-cap phenomenon. This is a threshold-margin data fact the paper reports and does not use.
- **The "group" question, unresolved in law.** Footnote 12 (p. 41) is a long discussion of wolf-pack investing: funds "frequently acquire significant stakes in targets within hours of learning that the initial fund has taken a position", relying on advice that follow-on investors need not co-file; "As of this time, the legal issue is unresolved."
- **Why activism is a hedge-fund phenomenon:** mutual funds face adverse tax consequences above 5% of assets in one company or a 10% stake; Investment Company Act limits on performance fees; restrictions on shorting, borrowing, and **investing in illiquid securities**; exemptions require staying private (≤100 investors) or having only "qualified" investors ($5m of investments) (p. 7). Typical hedge fund fee 2% + 20%; lock-ups of two years or more (pp. 7–8).
- **Cost of a stake scales with target size:** the median sample fund is $793m (25th/75th $278m/$4,446m); a 5% stake in the average (median) top-size-quintile firm would cost $760m ($285m) (p. 20). This is the paper's explicit explanation for why targets are small — a **capital-constraint** account of target selection that our liquidity account competes with.
- **13F timing:** the Pre-13F variable requires the 13F portfolio date to be at least 60 days before the 13D, "taking into account the allowed 45-day delay" (p. 28).
- Data sources: LiveEdgar, EDGAR, CRSP, Compustat, ExecuComp, I/B/E/S, IRRC (GINDEX), Thomson Financial 13F, WRDS CISDM, Factiva, Hasbrouck's posted Amihud/Gibbs liquidity estimates, Ken French's factor library, NYSE/NASDAQ monthly short interest.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths:**
- The dataset is the contribution and it is built to a standard no competitor matched: a purchased universe of 11,602 filers, manual Item 2 classification, phone calls to ambiguous filers, a 13F sweep to recover sub-5% activism, and a stated deliberate avoidance of survivorship, reporting-selection and backfill bias (they note that TASS and CISDM together contain only about a quarter of their activist funds, footnote 5, p. 40).
- Every headline claim is attacked from several directions. The stock-picking alternative gets four independent tests (hostility premium, Pre-13F subsample, exit-return asymmetry, holding-horizon argument). The over-reaction alternative gets a calendar-time factor model. Creditor expropriation gets a zero-long-term-debt subsample.
- Operating performance is matched two ways, including Barber–Lyon performance matching, and the attrition rate is reported (18.2%) rather than hidden.
- Honest about what the market reaction is *not*: R20's 0.04 correlation between announcement return and ex post success is reported and interpreted as an equilibrium condition, not buried.
- **It measures liquidity directly (Amihud) and reports the quintile distribution**, which is what makes it usable for us at all.

**Weaknesses / open flanks:**
- **The (−20,+20) window is very wide** and, as the paper itself shows, contains the fund's own accumulation trading inside the ten-day filing window. The "announcement return" is therefore partly a pre-disclosure price-pressure return, and the paper cannot separate the components (Q4).
- **The R² of the announcement-return regression is 0.032.** Stated objectives explain almost nothing of the cross-section.
- **No causal identification.** The probit explains selection into targeting; nothing corrects the return or performance estimates for it. The authors' strongest causal statement is that CEO turnover changes are "highly improbable" absent intervention (p. 32) — an argument, not an estimate.
- **Operating improvements are marginal and slow.** The significant EBITDA/Sales gain appears only at t+2, only on one of two matches at conventional levels, and the (t+1)−(t−1) change is negative. Klein & Zur, on an overlapping period, find profitability *falling*. The literature does not agree here.
- **The 18.2% attrition rate cuts against the paper's own performance analysis** and is acknowledged (p. 33) but not corrected — no bounds, no selection model, no Heckman.
- Success and partial success are hand-coded from news and filings by the authors; there is no inter-coder reliability check, and "partial success" is a soft category carrying 25.8% of the weight.
- Returns decline from 15.9% to 3.4% across six years (R22) with no formal trend test, and the sample is a single takeover-and-credit boom.
- **The liquidity result is reported and then dropped.** Amihud appears in Table 3, is discussed for four sentences on p. 21, and is **absent from the Table 4 probit and from the Table 5 return regression**. It never becomes a right-hand-side variable in any estimated equation.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **Object: announcement return, long-run alpha, deal return, campaign success, CEO pay/turnover, payout, leverage, operating margins. Not the takeover premium, not bidder entry, not deal terms.** The sale of the target enters as an *objective* (R7) and as an *exit route* (R8), never as a priced transaction. The authors even note that counting sales as payout would raise the payout measure — and then don't: "if we count activism that results in a target's liquidation, sale, or being taken private, as a complete payout to existing shareholders, then the post-activism payout ratio is much higher than the conventional payout measures indicate." (p. 33).
- **Margin: neither margin is a treatment.** The 5% threshold and the ten-day window are recited (p. 9), elaborated in footnote 2 (p. 40), and used to *interpret* the pre-filing turnover spike (pp. 23–24). Nothing is varied. There is no policy change, no cross-country threshold, no deadline experiment.
- **Identification: event study + matching + probit + calendar-time factor regressions.** No DiD, no IV, no structural model, no theory. Maug (1998) and Cornelli & Li (2002) are invoked to interpret R20, not to derive or estimate anything.
- **They explicitly cannot identify what drives the pre-filing run-up.** Three candidate explanations — the filing fund's own additional buying inside the ten-day window, wolf-pack investing by unaffiliated funds, and tipping — and: "Given the informal and secretive nature of such communication, our data does not allow for a formal testing of these two explanations." (p. 24). **This is the paper declaring the window margin out of scope.**
- **They cannot rule out the stock-picking story outright:** "it is possible that activist hedge funds are merely stock pickers, but use long holding periods to sustain an (unwarranted) reputation for activism. … We cannot rule out these possibilities, although we believe that a more plausible interpretation is that only a small portion of the positive abnormal return might be due to stock picking." (p. 30).
- **Liquidity is measured once and never used as an explanatory variable.** Amihud is in Table 3 only; it does not appear in the Table 4 probit, the Table 5 return regression, or any interaction.
- Hostility is described as endogenous and left there: "tactics represent endogenous decisions by the hedge funds, so that this evidence is best interpreted as an equilibrium outcome" (pp. 14–15).

## 7. Implications for our position

**What this paper occupies:** object = announcement return, long-run alpha, success rate, and firm-level operating/governance outcomes; margin = **none** (the disclosure rule is a data source and an interpretive aid); identification = event study + matching + probit. Competitor on the object; **measurement source on liquidity and on the window**.

**How it constrains us — and this is the paper that constrains us most:**
1. **They already measured our driving variable, found the sign we need, and dropped it.** Targets are *more* liquid than matched peers (Amihud −0.075, t = −3.99, R12) and the authors' stated reason is exactly the κ mechanism: "Higher liquidity makes it easier for the activists to accumulate a stake within a short period of time" (Q2). Then the crucial qualification: relative to the whole CRSP universe targets are **not** liquid — 43.4% of them sit in the third liquidity quintile (R12). Activism concentrates in the *middle* of the liquidity distribution. That is a non-monotonicity in the raw data, unremarked by the authors, and it is the empirical shape our **hump** (draft_v2's R1) would predict. This is the single most valuable fact on this card. It is also a warning: the fact is *already in print*, so our contribution has to be the mechanism and the margin, not the pattern.
2. **Liquidity and the capital constraint are competing explanations for the same fact, and they name the rival.** Their account of why targets are small is the cost of a stake: a 5% stake in the average top-quintile firm costs $760m against a median fund size of $793m (p. 20). A referee will offer this as the alternative to any liquidity story about target selection. Our κ has to do work the capital constraint cannot — and the third-quintile concentration is precisely where the two accounts diverge, because a pure capital constraint predicts monotonicity in size, not a hump in liquidity.
2b. **Two more rivals for the same cross-section, and they are theory-driven (added by verifier).** On p. 22 the paper tests Kahn and Winton (1998) — "They predict that investors are more likely to intervene in well-understood firms or industries so that the market can rapidly appreciate the effects of an intervention. Hedge funds should avoid 'opaque' and complicated businesses, such as those with high levels of R&D" — and reads Table 3's negative RND difference as "some support" (hedged, because RND is insignificant in Table 4). Black (1990) supplies a second: focusing on generalisable issues "helps hedge funds lower the marginal cost of launching activism at a new company" (p. 22). Together with the capital constraint, that is **three** live explanations for who gets targeted, two of them already tied to named theory. Our κ story must beat all three, and the third-quintile hump (R12) is the only fact none of them predicts.

3. **The window margin is visible in their data as an unexplained spike.** Abnormal turnover peaks in the ten days *before* the filing, not on it (Q3), and they list three candidate mechanisms and say their data cannot separate them (Q5). Our **partition** — trading in the pooled state before the flag — is the formal object behind that spike. Between R1's 3.2% pre-filing run-up and Q5's declared inability to test, this paper hands us both the motivating fact and the licence to model it. The Feb-2024 acceleration halves exactly this window.
4. **They give the exit-side liquidity constraint too.** "the typical target firm's stock has a turnover rate of about 0.4% a day and targeted firms tend to be mid- and small-cap stocks, making a quick private exit very difficult to execute" (Q6) — the blockholder cannot take the Wall Street Walk cheaply, which is the friction that makes voice worth choosing. And they name the theory: hedge funds "in the absence of reputation concerns and trading liquidity constraints, would have no incentive to continue with costly intervention" (p. 24), citing Maug (1998) — the liquidity-versus-control trade-off, invoked in one sentence and never estimated.
5. **The premium wedge is again absent where it is needed.** Sale of the target earns the highest announcement return of any objective (8.54%, t = 4.10; 10.70% when hostile), i.e. the market prices the control outcome above every other channel. But no premium is measured. The three classics between them establish that the control outcome is the object that pays and that nobody has priced the wedge.

**The whitespace this leaves:**
- **Margin: entirely open across all three classics.** Brav et al. state the rule most completely (footnote 2's four timing regimes) and use it least. The 13G→13D switching rule — a passive investor who "changes its initial passive purpose … would need to file a Schedule 13D to announce this shift to the market" (footnote 2, p. 40) — is the legal instantiation of our flagged/pooled partition and is quotable as an anchor.
- **Liquidity as an estimated channel, not a descriptive statistic.** Amihud is in Table 3 and in no regression. Putting it on the right-hand side of a control-outcome equation, interacted with the window margin, is untouched ground.
- **The non-monotonicity.** Nobody has asked why targets cluster in liquidity quintile 3.

**Danger:** this is a strong paper and it pre-empts the easy version of our claim. It has already said (i) targets are more liquid than peers, (ii) because liquidity eases accumulation, (iii) but the pre-filing run-up cannot be decomposed. Any positioning that presents (i) or (ii) as new will be refuted from Table 3. Our position must live in (iii) — the margin, the decomposition, and the control-outcome consequence — not in the correlation.
