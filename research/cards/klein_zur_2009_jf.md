## 1. Question

What do *confrontational* entrepreneurial shareholder activists — investors free of Investment Company Act regulation who buy ≥5% and say in the 13D purpose statement that they intend to redirect management — actually achieve? The paper runs two parallel samples, hedge funds and "other entrepreneurial activists" (individuals, PE, VC, wealthy-investor asset managers), and asks: what firms do they target, how does the market react at the 13D, how often do they get what they demanded, and what changes at the firm in the following year?

## 2. Model / data and method

- **Source / sample:** Schedule 13D and 13D/A filings, **1 January 2003 – 31 December 2005**, from SEC EDGAR. 13D/A amendments are traced backwards to the first filing so that only *initial* filings are events. Only filings whose purpose statement "clearly professes … its goal to redirect managements' efforts" are kept; filings where the investor merely reserves the option to influence, or states an intent to communicate with management, are excluded (stated in the introduction, p. 1; the sample dates are given in the data section, p. 13). Note that Table I Panel B shows first filings dating back to 1995, 1998, 1999, 2000, 2001, 2002 — the campaigns are the ones *active* in 2003–2005 (p. 51).
- **Final N: 151 hedge-fund campaigns by 101 hedge funds; 154 other-entrepreneurial campaigns by 134 investors** (Table I Panel A, p. 51). The "other" group: 58 individuals, 8 ex-officers of the target, 38 investment advisors to wealthy investors, 16 private asset management firms, 9 private equity funds, 5 venture capital firms (p. 15).
- **134 hedge-fund and 139 other-activist targets is the working sample for *returns as well as accounting* (corrected by verifier).** Footnote 14 (p. 21) explains the cut: nine hedge-fund and twelve other-activist targets trade on the pink sheets and are dropped because they are "exempt from filing financial statements with the SEC", and "[m]issing data further reduces our sample to 134 hedge fund and 139 other activist targets." The notes to **Tables IV, V and VII** — every announcement-return table on this card — all read "for 134 firms targeted by hedge fund activists and 139 firms targeted by other entrepreneurial activists". So R1–R3 and R7–R11 rest on 134/139, not on the headline 151/154; the 151/154 denominators apply only to the success and takeover *counts* (Tables VI and the p. 37 text). One-year *change* tables use 132 and 129 firms (Table VIII, pp. 63–64).
- **Unit:** activist–target campaign, classified into 13 purpose categories read from Item 4 "Purpose of Transaction" (Table I Panel E, p. 52).
- **Design / identification: event study with characteristic-adjusted buy-and-hold returns, plus an industry × size × book-to-market matched control sample.** No DiD, no IV, no structural estimation, no theory.
  - **Event window: [−30, +5] and [−30, +30] trading days around the 13D filing date (day 0).** The −30 start is chosen *explicitly* to span the ten-day 13D filing window plus leakage and pre-filing price pressure (p. 26) — see Q3.
  - Three abnormal-return measures reported side by side: **size-adjusted** (target BHR minus Fama-French 5 size-matched portfolio, computed in Eventus), **market-adjusted** (minus CRSP value-weighted NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ), **industry-adjusted** (minus FF48 industry, target excluded) (p. 24).
  - **Matching algorithm:** for each target, the ten closest firms in revenues (five above, five below) within the same Fama–French (1997) 48-industry class; of those ten, the one with the closest book-to-market ratio (p. 20).
  - Long-run return: size-adjusted BHR from **+30 trading days to one year** after the filing date (p. 39).
  - All variables winsorized at 1% and 99% (footnote 13, p. 21).
- **Outcome coding:** "success" = the activist achieved the goal stated in the initial 13D **within one year**, coded from subsequent 13D/A filings and Factiva news (p. 30). Proxy fights from the Georgeson Shareholder "contested solicitations" list; *threatened* proxy fights from Factiva news reports that never reached an SEC solicitation (p. 33).
- **Takeover coding:** merged or acquired within one year of the initial 13D filing (Table VII Panel C, p. 62).
- Multivariate: pooled logistic models of being targeted (Table III, p. 56), with a probit robustness check reported in footnote 17 (p. 26).

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **Threshold margin + window margin, quoted from the rule itself.** Footnote 12 (p. 13) reproduces Rule 13d-1(a) verbatim: any beneficial owner of more than five percent "shall, within 10 days after the acquisition, file with the Commission a statement containing the information required by Schedule 13D." The same footnote quotes Rule 13d-2(b) on when a 13G may be filed instead — "in the ordinary course of his business and not with the purpose nor with the effect of changing or influencing the control of the issuer…" This is the cleanest statement of the **partition** in any of the three activism classics: the legal test is *purpose*, not stake size alone.
- **The window is used to set the event window.** "Our event window begins on day –30 to allow for the ten-day 13D filing window, possible prior leakage of information, and pre-filing price pressure that may occur due to the activist accruing a large stake in a relatively short period of time." (p. 26).
- **Cross-country threshold margin, in passing:** footnote 9 (p. 12) — "Under UK company law disclosure rules, all investments exceeding a 3% threshold must be disclosed."
- **13G and 13F obligations.** Footnote 2 (p. 6): they are not exempt "from filing SEC Form 13D or 13G when crossing the 5% threshold of ownership or from filing an SEC Form 13F. The 13G filings are required for passive investors who acquire at least a 5% interest in a publicly traded equity security." The 13F must be filed within 45 days of each calendar quarter end by managers with ≥$100m; critically, **"It does not contain any indication of investment purpose."**
- **What a 13D reveals:** "the investor identifies name, background (including any criminal convictions within the last five years), number and type of shares purchased, the percentage of the class of equity owned, and the purpose of the transaction." (p. 13).
- **Falling below 5% ends the story** — used in the Columbia Ventures narrative: a 13D/A on 5 July 2004 "reveals that Columbia has reduced its position in International Aluminum to under 5%." (p. 19).
- **The regulatory exemptions that define the activist class:** Securities Act 1933, Securities Exchange Act 1934, and most notably the Investment Company Act 1940; 3(c)(1) funds limited to 99 investors, 3(c)(7) to 499 investors (footnote 3, p. 6). Fee structure: "a percentage of invested funds and a percentage of the funds' profits, usually 5–40% over zero percent or the risk-free rate" (p. 6).
- **Empty voting is named as a disclosure loophole:** activists "are not required to disclose their investment strategies, short-selling positions, or leverage ratios", so they can use stock lending (Christoffersen et al. 2007) or derivatives (Hu and Black 2007) "to acquire voting rights without owning a long position in the company's underlying stock. Thus, an activist can build up voting rights in a target company to buttress a threat of an impending proxy fight." (p. 8).
- **Liquidity does appear in this paper — as a *regulatory* constraint on who can be a blockholder, never as a measured variable (added by verifier).** Two places, both institutional. (i) p. 7: individuals, hedge funds, asset-management groups, PE and VC funds "are wholly unrestricted in how they can invest in terms of diversification, illiquid assets, short-selling, and leverage. The funds need not have sufficient capital to cover redemptions and can restrict investors from exiting their funds." (ii) p. 11: "Black (1990, 1998) describes regulatory constraints that inhibit mutual funds from engaging in confrontational activism, including rules on maintaining liquidity, holding large blocks of any one company, and insider trading." So the activist's *own* liquidity constraint is slack by construction of the sample, and the comparison group's is binding by regulation. That is the selection story behind "why hedge funds", and it is a liquidity story — just not a measured one. §6's "liquidity absent" claim is about measurement and should be read that way.
- **Sample-construction exception mirroring the other two classics (added by verifier):** all activists required to file under the Investment Company Act or the 1934 Act are eliminated, "most notably mutual funds and corporations (both U.S. and foreign). The one exception is GAMCO, formerly known as Gabelli Asset Management, a publicly traded fund." (p. 14). Compare Brav et al.'s Franklin Mutual carve-in and Greenwood & Schor's Gabelli carve-out — all three classics make a bespoke exception for the same handful of borderline filers.
- Data sources: EDGAR, CRSP, Compustat, Eventus (event-study engine), Factiva, Georgeson Shareholder contested-solicitations list, Fama–French 48 industries, fund websites and investor journals for the HF/other classification (pp. 14, 19, 24, 30, 33).
- The Pirate Capital / Cornell Companies case study (pp. 17–18) is a documented trace of a stake escalating **5.2% → 11.8% → 14.8%** across 13 filings over 14 months, with the demands escalating in each amendment — the cleanest narrative in the literature of a blockholder accumulating *after* the flag.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths:**
- The two-sample design is the contribution. Holding the filing event, the window and the matching fixed and varying only the *type* of blockholder produces the paper's sharpest result: R7 vs. R8, where the takeover conditioning works for one group and not the other.
- Three abnormal-return measures reported side by side (size-, market-, industry-adjusted), so the announcement result is not a benchmark artifact.
- Outcome coding is done by hand from the 13D/A trail plus Factiva, and success is defined against the activist's *own* stated objective — not an outside notion of success. Table VI Panel A prints demand-by-demand success counts, so the reader can see which cells are thin.
- The proxy-threat finding (R16) is genuinely novel and correctly framed: threat, not fight, and only for board seats.
- Honest about the profitability result running against Brav et al.: R14 reports deteriorating EBITDA and says so.

**Weaknesses / open flanks:**
- **The event window is [−30, +5] / [−30, +30] — an enormous window for an announcement study**, and the paper itself concedes it is measuring leakage and pre-filing price pressure as well as the announcement (p. 26). This inflates the headline 10.2% relative to the tight-window numbers in the neighbouring literature and makes the "announcement return" partly a *pre*-disclosure accumulation return.
- **Sample period is three years (2003–2005) in a takeover boom**, and the samples are 151 and 154 campaigns — of which only **134 and 139** carry the return data behind every CAR table — small next to Brav et al. (1,059 hedge fund–target pairs, 2001–06) and Greenwood & Schor (980 events, 1993Q3–2006Q3). *(Corrected by verifier: the card previously gave Brav et al.'s N as 888; their final sample is 1,059 events across 882 unique target companies.)*
- **No standard errors are printed anywhere.** Tables IV–VIII give means, medians, quartiles and significance stars, and t-statistics only for *between-group* differences. A referee cannot compute a confidence interval from this paper.
- **Cell sizes in Tables V and VI are frequently in single digits** (buy back own stock N = 4, replace CEO N = 3, cut CEO's salary N = 1, pay dividend N = 2). Several 100% success rates rest on 2–4 observations, and the paper's own rule of concentrating on cells with ≥11 occurrences (p. 29) is not applied to Table VI.
- **No causal identification.** The matched sample supports "who gets targeted", not "what activism causes". Selection into activism is modelled (Table III) but never used to correct the return or outcome estimates.
- **The takeover result is unstable across the two samples and never explained.** R7 (hedge funds: no takeover premium in the CAR) directly contradicts Greenwood & Schor's headline on an overlapping population and period; the paper notes the contradiction with Barclay & Holderness but does not test it. *(Corrected by verifier: they do offer two verbal explanations — see §6 — and they do engage Greenwood & Schor, though only on the purpose-statement CARs, where they call their results "consistent with those reported by Greenwood and Schor (2007) for a different sample of hedge fund targets" (p. 29). Greenwood & Schor appear in the reference list as a 2007 Harvard working paper, p. 49.)*
- **Version hazard:** this WP draft contains at least one table typo — Table VII Panel C labels the hedge-fund short window "[–30 to +30]" twice where the first row must be "[–30 to +5]" (p. 62), and one median entry (6.74%**) is missing its brackets. *(Verifier: both confirmed in the print.)* Any number taken from this card should be re-checked against JF 64(1), 187–229 before it enters a draft.
- No liquidity variable of any kind. The only trading-friction fact is the exchange-venue tabulation (9 HF and 12 other targets on pink sheets, 6 and 11 on the OTC bulletin board, Table I Panel C, p. 52), used to explain missing Compustat data rather than as economics — and those 21 pink-sheet targets are exactly the ones dropped from every return and accounting table (footnote 14, p. 21), so the *least* liquid targets are the ones this paper cannot measure at all.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **Object: announcement and one-year abnormal returns, campaign success, and one-year accounting changes. Not the takeover premium, not bidder entry.** Takeover enters only as a one-year binary in one panel of one table (Table VII Panel C, p. 62). No deal price, no premium, no competing-bid count, no auction outcome.
- **Margin: neither margin is a treatment.** The 5% threshold and the 10-day window are recited as the rule (footnote 12, p. 13) and used to justify a −30-day event-window start (p. 26). No filing deadline is varied; the UK 3% threshold is mentioned in a footnote (p. 12) only to contextualise Becht et al. The 13D/13G distinction is quoted (footnote 2, p. 6; footnote 12, p. 13) and never exploited.
- **Identification: event study with a matched control sample only.** No DiD, no IV, no structural model, no theory section. The single robustness exercise on functional form is logit-vs-probit (footnote 17, p. 26).
- **They stop the outcome horizon at one year** and treat the second-year results as preliminary: "Because of the smaller sample sizes, we view these findings as preliminary and briefly discuss them. … We cautiously conclude that changes to the target occur mainly in the first year subsequent to the activism." (p. 45).
- **They flag endogeneity of tactics and leave it there:** "We interpret this finding as consistent with the activist's choice of tactics and probability of success being endogenous to the characteristics of the target firm." (p. 35) — an acknowledgement, not a correction.
- **They do not *test* the contradiction with the takeover literature — but they do explain it, twice (corrected by verifier; the card previously said "no explanation").** The paper notes its result "is contrary to that of Barclay and Holderness (1991)" (p. 40) and offers two accounts. (i) **Blockholder type:** "One possible reason for the different acquisition rates is that the majority of blockholders studied by Barclay and Holderness are other companies, whereas our blockholders are predominantly hedge funds, private investors, venture capitalists, or private equity managers." (p. 37). (ii) **Stated intent:** "These differences also reflect the disparity in incidences in which the activist professes an intent in having the target firm acquired… other entrepreneurial activists declare an intention of buying more stock in the target firm 24 times vs. 12 times for the hedge fund activist." (p. 38). Both are verbal arguments, not tests — no interaction term, no formal comparison, no reconciliation with Greenwood & Schor's takeover result. **The accurate scope claim is "explained but not tested", not "unexplained".**
- Liquidity, price impact, trading volume, turnover, depth: absent. "Pre-filing price pressure" appears once, as a reason to widen the event window (p. 26), and never as a measured object. (Verifier: liquidity *does* appear twice as a regulatory constraint on who can be an activist, pp. 7 and 11 — see §4. What is absent is any liquidity *measure*.)

## 7. Implications for our position

**What this paper occupies:** object = announcement CAR + one-year abnormal return + campaign success + one-year accounting change, conditioned on realised outcomes; margin = **none** (the disclosure rule is a sample-construction fact and a window-choice justification); identification = event study + matched control. It is a *competitor* on the object and a *measurement* source on institutional detail — not on either margin.

**How it constrains us:**
1. **It is the counterexample to Greenwood & Schor, on the same object.** For **hedge-fund** targets Klein & Zur find the takeover conditioning does *nothing*: [−30,+5] CAR is 2.66% for merged targets and 8.63% for those that stay independent, t = −1.51 (R7). For **other** entrepreneurial activists the same split gives 17.39% vs. 3.25%, t = 2.59*** (R8). Two classics, overlapping years, opposite signs on the headline. Any positioning memo that leans on "activism returns are takeover returns" as settled fact is overclaiming — and a referee who knows this literature will say so. **This is the single most useful fact on this card.**
2. **Their takeover frequency is much lower than Greenwood & Schor's.** 14.6% (HF) and 12.3% (other) within one year here (R6), against Greenwood & Schor's 26.1% overall and 18.1% within twelve months. Different windows and coding, but the gap is large enough that "the share of activist targets acquired" is a contested number, not a stylised fact. Report a range, cite both.
3. **They confirm the object is a *control* outcome broadly defined, not operating improvement.** R14 has profitability *falling* after activism. Whatever activism does for shareholders here, it is not running the business better. That supports our broadened **control outcome** and against any model whose engagement payoff is a productivity gain.
4. **Their event window is our mechanism, treated as noise.** Q3 (p. 26) says the window starts at −30 to absorb the ten-day filing window, leakage, and "pre-filing price pressure that may occur due to the activist accruing a large stake in a relatively short period of time." That sentence contains the whole **window margin** and the whole **liquidity** channel — accumulation speed against price impact before the flag — stated as a measurement nuisance to be windowed away. Our **partition** is exactly this object taken seriously. Cite Q3 whenever we need to show the literature has *seen* the window and declined to model it.

**The whitespace this leaves:**
- **The margin is untouched by all three activism classics**, and Klein & Zur states the rule most precisely (footnote 12's verbatim Rule 13d-1(a) and 13d-2(b)) while using it least. The 13d-2(b) language — 13G is for holdings acquired "not with the purpose nor with the effect of changing or influencing the control of the issuer" — is a *purpose* test, which is precisely the flagged/pooled split our model formalises. That is a quotable anchor no competitor has used as economics.
- **Liquidity as the driving variable is open.** Their targets sit on the pink sheets and the OTC bulletin board (21 of 305 campaigns) with median market cap $70–133m. The illiquid tail is where their sample lives and where nobody has looked.
- **The HF-vs-other contradiction wants a mechanism — but the 24-vs-12 observation is *theirs*, not ours (corrected by verifier).** The card previously presented the stated-intent asymmetry as our own insight. It is stated in the paper: "These differences also reflect the disparity in incidences in which the activist professes an intent in having the target firm acquired… other entrepreneurial activists declare an intention of buying more stock in the target firm 24 times vs. 12 times for the hedge fund activist." (p. 38; Table I Panel E, p. 52, gives 24 and 12, and R11 shows that category earning the highest CAR in both samples, 15.93% and 13.06%). Presenting "the group that can plausibly buy the firm is the group whose CAR responds to takeover" as new will be refuted from p. 38. **The whitespace is one step further in:** they assert the correlation between *declared* intent and the takeover-conditioned CAR; nobody derives *why* type should shift the premium wedge and the takeover probability by different amounts, and nobody tests it (no interaction, no formal comparison). A model that predicts the sign and the cross-sectional shape of that difference — in κ, and in how fast a type can accumulate before the flag — is still open ground. Position on the derivation and the test, never on the observation.

**Danger:** Klein & Zur is also the cleanest evidence that *voice* pays without exit — 60–65% success rates, board seats won by threat rather than fight (R16), and long-run returns unrelated to whether the campaign succeeded (R9's +30-to-1-year columns). A referee can use this paper to argue that control outcomes are only one of several channels, and that our narrowing to takeover is a modelling convenience.
