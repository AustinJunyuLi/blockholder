## 1. Question

Does a liquid stock market make corporate governance worse? The received view — Bhide (1993), Coffee (1991) "Liquidity versus control" — says yes: liquidity lets a large shareholder sell out on bad news instead of staying and fixing the firm, so liquidity and control trade off. Maug asks whether that trade-off survives once the *size of the block is endogenous*. His answer is that it does not: liquidity also lets the large shareholder buy more shares cheaply and thereby recover the cost of monitoring from uninformed traders, and once the block size is chosen rather than handed down, the second effect always wins.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Primitives (Section II, pp. 70–71).** One firm, one unit of shares (normalized to 1). Assets are worth `L` in current use and `H > L` if restructured, with `L < H` common knowledge. Incumbent management will not restructure. A single large investor `F` (i) is not subject to liquidity shocks and (ii) can monitor at cost `c_M < H − L`, which raises firm value to `H`. "Monitoring" is a comprehensive label covering intervention *and* information acquisition, used synonymously with "intervention" and "shareholder activism" (p. 66). A continuum of identical households of total measure 1 holds the rest. All agents risk-neutral; risk-free rate zero.

**Liquidity.** Households suffer *correlated* liquidity shocks: with probability 1/2 nobody sells; with probability 1/2 a fraction φ ∈ (0,1) of households sell their entire holdings. So each household's ex-ante shock probability is φ/2, and **φ is the liquidity (noise-trading) parameter**. Aggregate uninformed supply is φ(1 − α) — note it shrinks in α, which is the *liquidity effect*: a bigger block means a thinner market (p. 67, p. 72).

**Market microstructure.** Kyle (1985) with a discrete (binary) state; the paper notes a binary Kyle model and a Glosten–Milgrom (1985) type model "are similar (see Krishnan 1992), [so] this modeling choice has no substantial consequences for the analysis" (fn. 9, p. 71). Households and `F` submit orders to a risk-neutral market maker who sees only the aggregate order flow `y` and prices at `P₁ = E(ṽ | y)`.

**Timing (Figure 1, p. 71).** (1) Shares placed at initial price `P₀`; `F` buys α, households buy 1 − α. (2) `F` decides about monitoring and trading. (3) Households experience the liquidity shock with probability 1/2. (4) Market maker receives orders, sets `P₁`. (5) Firm profits realized, all parties paid.

**Strategies.** `F` plays a mixed strategy: with probability `q`, buy `x_B > 0`, intervene and improve; with probability 1 − q, sell `x_S < 0` and do not intervene. `F` can only monitor if α + x_B ≥ μ, where μ ∈ [0,1] is the voting power needed to force restructuring (need not be 0.5 — larger if the charter requires a supermajority, smaller if `F` can rely on other blockholders). **Camouflage condition**: the market maker must not be able to tell "F buys and households sell" from "F sells and households do not sell", i.e. `x_S + 0 = x_B − φ(1 − α)` ⟺ `x_B − x_S = φ(1 − α)` (eq. 1, p. 72). Baseline imposes symmetry `x_B = −x_S = φ(1 − α)/2 ≡ u`; Appendix A (pp. 90–92) shows the asymmetric case is qualitatively equivalent.

**Solution concept.** Backward induction / subgame perfection, solved first for exogenous α, then with α chosen endogenously by `F` at stage 1 (Section III.B). Order flow takes three values −3u, −u, u; only −u is uninformative (Table I, p. 74). Equating the payoffs from "buy and monitor" (eq. 3) and "sell and don't intervene" (eq. 4) gives the unique mixing probability

  `q = 1/2 − 2(c_M − α(H − L)) / (φ(1 − α)(H − L))`  (eq. 5, p. 74).

Trading profits are `(1 − α)G` with `G ≡ (φ/2)q(1 − q)(H − L)` (eq. 6, p. 75); Kyle's lambda here is exactly 1/2 (eq. 8, p. 75). The initial price carries an adverse-selection discount exactly equal to `F`'s per-share trading gain: `P₀ = qH + (1 − q)L − G` (eq. 11, p. 79), so `F` earns the *same* profit per share from informed trading and from the initial-stake discount, and total profits collapse to `G − qc_M` (eq. 13, p. 79).

**Takeover section primitives (Section IV, pp. 83–87) *(added by verifier)*.** Restructuring by method j ∈ {M, T} succeeds with probability s_j, 0 ≤ s_M < s_T ≤ 1 (Assumption 1), at cost c_j incurred "even if restructuring is unsuccessful or the bid fails", with 0 < c_j < s_j(H − L) (Assumption 2, eq. 16, p. 84). **Condition 1 (Cost Advantage)**: takeovers enjoy a cost advantage iff s_T/c_T ≥ s_M/c_M (eq. 17, p. 84). `F` then chooses among **three** stage-2 actions (p. 84): "(A) Sell and don't intervene; (B) Buy and monitor; or (C) Buy and attempt a takeover." Maug explicitly declines to *assume* s_T > s_M or c_T > c_M, though he says both are plausible.

**The two forces, named by the paper.** *Lock-in effect*: a larger α makes the capital gain on the portfolio holding matter more, biasing `F` toward intervention. *Liquidity effect*: a larger α leaves fewer shares in household hands, thinning the market and cutting `F`'s trading profits.

**No data.** Pure theory. Section V ("Empirical Issues", pp. 87–88) reviews others' results (Nesbitt 1994, Smith 1996, Gillan and Starks 1995, Wahal 1996, Gordon and Pound 1993, Leleux–Vermaelen–Banerjee 1995, Brickley–Lease–Smith 1988) without estimating anything.

**What buys tractability:** binary firm value {L, H}; binary/discrete order flow with exactly three realizations; correlated all-or-nothing liquidity shocks with the coin-flip structure (so λ = 1/2 exactly); symmetric trade sizes `x_B = −x_S` in the baseline; monitoring succeeds with an exogenous probability rather than one increasing in votes controlled (the paper concedes this simplification explicitly, p. 82); one large shareholder only; risk neutrality throughout; no short-sale constraints.

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **Motivating anecdote**: British Airways, 1993 — Fidelity, "the second largest shareholder with a holding of 4.5 percent, 'has disposed of almost its entire position,' and the Prudential and Standard Life stakes have fallen below 3 percent" (*Financial Times*, January 21, 1993; pp. 65–66 and fn. 1).
- **The only "5 percent" in the paper is a mutual-fund holding cap, not a disclosure trigger**: fn. 6, p. 68 — "some shareholders are not allowed to hold a stake above a certain limit in any individual company (for mutual funds in the United States, this limit is 5 percent)." Reiterated in the body at p. 83: "In the United States, the high majority requirement would probably exclude mutual funds from governance activities."
- **The corporate charter's majority requirement μ** is the paper's only *institutional dial*: founders choose μ (and, through the listing exchange, market liquidity) before date 0 (pp. 82–83). This is a *charter* variable, not a securities-law variable.
- Institutional framework references only, no data: Black (1992a) for the US, Black and Coffee (1994) for the UK, Coffee (1991), Gordon (1991), Grundfest (1993) (p. 70); Nesbitt (1994) on CalPERS campaigns (p. 70, fn. 7); the 1980s shift of institutional targeting from takeover defenses to performance and governance issues, e.g. confidential voting (p. 71, fn. 8).
- **The charter beats liquidity as the lock-in instrument (p. 89) *(added by verifier)*.** "Shareholders can reduce or even eliminate this rent if they require higher majorities in voting contests, forcing the large shareholder to invest in larger stakes, which are then less liquid. **This is a more efficient way of locking the large shareholder into her stake than reducing the liquidity of the market.** Reduced liquidity aggravates the free-rider problem, but it does not eliminate the rents of the large shareholder." Maug's *ranking of instruments* is explicit: charter μ dominates liquidity φ. Material for us — our instrument is neither, it is the disclosure rule.
- **Insider-trading law** appears only in the earlier working-paper title ("On the Impact of Insider Trading Legislation on Large Shareholder Activism", fn. * p. 65) — the published paper does not model it.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths:**
- One clean, decisive move: endogenize the block size. The paper's whole force comes from noting that the trade-off literature holds α fixed, and that `F` will never voluntarily accept the lock-in that the trade-off argument requires (p. 81).
- Everything is closed-form. `q̂`, `α̂`, `μ̄`, `φ*` all have explicit expressions; the comparative statics are unambiguous, not numerical.
- Beautiful internal accounting: `F` earns exactly the same per-share profit from informed trading and from the IPO adverse-selection discount, so total payoff collapses to `G − qc_M` (eq. 13, p. 79) and the whole problem becomes "choose the randomization that maximizes trading gains net of expected monitoring cost".
- *(added by verifier)* **He names the retrading property as the contribution** (p. 69): "An important contribution of this paper is its emphasis on an ownership structure that is robust to retrading in secondary markets; other papers assume that the relevant ownership structure is the initial one determined in the IPO." Directly relevant to us — a filing window is precisely a *bound* on how much retrading stays pooled.
- The result is *signed*, not ambiguous, and the paper says so up front (p. 66): a rare thing in this literature at the time, and the reason it became the classic.
- The takeover section (IV) is a genuine extension, not decoration: it produces a distinct prediction (liquidity shifts `F` from the cheap method to the *effective* method) rather than restating the monitoring result.

**Weaknesses / open flanks:**
- **Binary everything.** Two firm values, three order-flow realizations, λ pinned at exactly 1/2 by construction (eq. 8, p. 75). Liquidity φ enters only through the *size* of the tradeable block, never through price informativeness. A modern referee would ask whether R7 survives a continuous-state Kyle model where λ itself moves with φ.
- **Monitoring succeeds with an exogenous probability**, not one increasing in the votes `F` controls. The paper concedes this: "although this way of modeling may be more realistic, it is unlikely to change the results of this section" (p. 82) — an ASSERTED robustness claim with no proof.
- **One large shareholder only.** Named as the paper's own limitation (p. 89): it cannot address several blockholders free-riding on each other, or coordination among them.
- **No agency inside the institution.** Also flagged by the author as set aside (p. 89).
- The camouflage condition (eq. 1) *forces* `x_B − x_S = φ(1 − α)` — trading intensity is not chosen freely but pinned by the anonymity constraint, and the split between `x_B` and the randomization `q` is a normalization (fn. 12–13, p. 73).
- Proposition 6 shows the headline result **is not universal**: with a binding supermajority the sign flips back and liquidity *reduces* monitoring. The paper's own charter analysis (Cor. 2) says value-maximizing founders will set exactly the μ that makes `F` indifferent — i.e. drive the economy toward the boundary where Proposition 6's region begins.
- Zero empirical content of its own; Section V is a literature discussion that concludes "no clear conclusion to this question has been reached" (p. 87).

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **Object.** The object is the **probability that a large shareholder monitors** (`q`), plus welfare and the initial share price `P₀`. Takeovers enter as an *alternative technology* with its own success probability `s_T` and cost `c_T` (Section IV, pp. 83–87), but there is **no takeover premium, no bidder competition, no tender-offer game, and no target-shareholder tendering decision** — *verifier's executed check: the strings "premium", "tender" and "bidder" return **zero** hits in the full text, body and references alike*. `H − L` is the whole surplus and it accrues to price, not to a negotiated premium. Bidder entry is never modelled.
- **Margin.** **There is no disclosure rule with a threshold or a window.** The paper touches stake disclosure exactly once, and *assumes it away by timing* (p. 73): pre-trade publicity would limit liquidity and the quantities `F` can trade, while post-trade regulatory filing "does not affect F's trading strategy" (Q5 below). This single sentence is the seam our paper works in — a 13D-type rule is exactly the intermediate case (a *bounded window* between the trade and the filing) that Maug's binary framing skips. He also does not model insider-trading law despite the earlier title.
- **Nearest antecedent he names and departs from *(added by verifier)*.** **Kyle and Vila (1991)** are, in Maug's own words (p. 69), "closer to the approach taken in the current paper, as they also observe that the investor who acquires a toehold before intervening in the management of the company can gain if the market cannot detect this purchase. They perform comparative static analysis in terms of the size of the initial stake held by the large investor, but they do not endogenize the decision to hold such a stake, so their analysis of the impact of market liquidity remains ambiguous." Also flagged at p. 66 ("The last point is extensively analyzed in relation to takeovers by Kyle and Vila (1991)"). This is the *closest* thing in the paper to our object — undetected accumulation ahead of a control event — and the card originally missed it.
- **Kahn and Winton (1995) *(added by verifier)*.** p. 69: in their model monitoring and informed trading are "mutually exclusive alternatives", so "their analysis does not address the crucial conflict of interest experienced by a large shareholder who has identified an underperforming company and has to decide between selling on this information or intervening." Maug's stated point of departure — and the exact exit/voice choice our partition sits on.
- **Identification.** Theory only. No sample, no period, no estimation, no numerical exercise.
- **Explicitly declared out of scope (p. 89):** several blockholders free-riding on each other's monitoring and coordinating; the internal organization and incentives of the large shareholder itself. "These issues are beyond the scope of this paper and left for future research."
- **Not addressed:** which specific governance action is taken; heterogeneity across households; risk aversion; multiple firms/portfolio choice (mentioned only as intuition for why blockholders hold small stakes, p. 81); dynamic accumulation over time.

## 7. Implications for our position

**What it occupies:** object = *probability of monitoring / activism* (with takeover-vs-monitoring *method choice* as an extension); margin = **none of the disclosure rule** — it is assumed away in one sentence on p. 73; identification = *theory only*.

1. **This is our direct antecedent and it hands us the margin.** Maug's p. 73 sentence is the cleanest possible statement of the whitespace: he splits disclosure into "before trading" (kills liquidity, kills the mechanism) and "after completing a transaction" (irrelevant). A Schedule 13D rule is *neither*: it is a trigger at a stake threshold plus a **finite filing window**, so some of the accumulation happens flagged and some pooled. The *partition* is exactly what he does not have. Our position — how the **threshold margin** and the **window margin** move control outcomes as κ varies — sits precisely in the gap his dichotomy leaves open. This is the single most quotable sentence in the whole competitor set for our purposes.
2. **It fixes what "liquidity helps" means, and we must not overclaim past it.** Maug's liquidity result is *conditional*: Proposition 2 says the sign depends on whether α covers `c_M/(H − L)`; only Proposition 4's endogenous α settles it. If our v4 core model takes α (or the initial stake) as exogenous, a referee will say we are re-running the pre-1998 argument. Conversely, Proposition 6 is a live counterexample: with a binding supermajority, more liquidity means *less* monitoring. Any "liquidity helps control outcomes" claim in draft_v3 needs its α-endogeneity and its μ region stated.
3. **His φ is our κ, and the mapping is exact enough to cite.** φ = the fraction of households hit by a correlated liquidity shock = uninformed supply φ(1 − α) = the size of the transaction `F` can hide behind. That is noise-trading intensity, and it is the same object as our κ — unlike Levit–Malenko–Maug (2026), where γ is a quadratic trading cost. When we say "liquidity", we should say "in the sense of Maug (1998)'s φ" and cite p. 71–72.
4. **The free-riding logic constrains our premium wedge.** R16 (p. 79, p. 88) is the sharpest version of the free-rider result: improvements are *fully* impounded in price, so `F` profits only from the volatility she creates, never from the mean. If our model gives the blockholder a share of the control gain through the price, Maug's accounting says that channel is exactly zero and the only rent is informational. Our premium wedge must therefore come from somewhere his model does not have — an outside bidder and a negotiated premium — which is a defensible reason for our object to be the takeover premium rather than `q`.
5. **Non-monotonicity in liquidity is not our discovery.** Proposition 7 (p. 83) already delivers a **hump in liquidity**: initial shareholders' wealth is maximized at an interior φ*. Draft_v2's R1 "hump" claim (minority gains non-monotone in liquidity, certified only on a grid) must be positioned against this — it is either the same hump in a different object, or it needs a different mechanism named. Given R1 is already flagged as disposable in `CONTEXT.md`, the safe move is to drop it rather than defend a NUMERICAL claim against Maug's PROVED one.
6. **Method choice is a control-outcome result we can build on.** Proposition 10(ii) (p. 87) says higher liquidity makes `F` *less* likely to choose the takeover route, because in a liquid market the cost advantage of the cheap method matters less than its effectiveness. That is a liquidity → control-outcome comparative static already on the books, in the direction "more liquidity, more monitoring, fewer takeovers". If our result is "more liquidity, more takeover premium", we have to be explicit about whether we contradict him or work on a margin (bidder entry / premium size) he leaves untouched. He does not model the premium at all, so the honest line is "complementary object, same driving variable".
7. **Deliverability signal.** The whole paper is closed-form with a binary state, ten propositions and two appendices, and it is 34 printed pages. That is a realistic template for the December package's core model — and a reminder that the v4 instinct to collapse the four actions (exit/hold/quiet/public) into fewer is well precedented: Maug gets a canonical result out of a two-action randomization.
