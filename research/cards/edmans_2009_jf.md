## 1. Question

Can a blockholder govern a firm *without any ability to intervene* — purely by trading? Edmans asks whether informed blockholder trading ("the Wall Street Rule") disciplines managerial **myopia**: managers who weight the interim stock price will underinvest in long-horizon projects that depress current earnings. He also asks whether market **liquidity** helps or hurts, against the then-dominant view (Bhide 1993, Porter 1992, Thurow 1993) that liquid US markets cause short-termism. The stated broader motive is to explain why the US is full of small, non-controlling blockholders whom intervention theories cannot rationalise (pp. 2481–2484).

## 2. Model / data and method

Pure theory; no data, no simulation, no calibration. Three dates (Figure 1, p. 2487).

- **Primitives.** One share outstanding. Blockholder B holds α; atomistic holders hold 1 − α. All risk neutral, risk-free rate zero. Fundamental value V revealed at t = 3.
- **t = 1.** A public signal s ∈ {s_g, s_b} (an earnings announcement) is released. If s = s_g then V = X > 0 with certainty; if s = s_b then V ∈ {0, X} with equal probability. All the action is conditional on s_b (p. 2488, p. 2489).
- **t = 2 (monitoring).** B chooses effort µ ∈ [0,1] at cost ½cµ², getting a binary signal i ∈ {i_g, i_b} with Pr(i_g|X) = Pr(i_b|0) = ½ + ½µ. Posteriors: Pr(X|i_g) = (1+µ)/2, Pr(X|i_b) = (1−µ)/2 ≡ π_b (eq. 1, p. 2488).
- **t = 2 (trading).** B either does nothing or sells β units; she sells iff i = i_b. **Short-sales constraint** β ≤ α — this is the whole engine linking stake size to information incentives (p. 2488).
- **Liquidity.** Noise demand u ~ exponential with rate λ = 1/(ν(1−α)), so mean liquidity trade E(u) = ν(1−α). ν ≤ 1 is the exogenous **liquidity parameter**; free float (1 − α) is the endogenous part. The exponential (rather than normal) noise distribution is the stated methodological contribution: it lets the informed trade be solved in closed form with *binary* firm value (pp. 2488–2489).
- **Pricing.** Competitive Kyle (1985) market maker sees d = b + u and sets P = E[V | d, s]. Because noise is one-sided, d ≤ 0 fully reveals a sale (P = π_b X); d > 0 pools (P = π_m X), π_m = [1 + e^(−λβ) + µ(1 − e^(−λβ))] / [2(1 + e^(−λβ))] (Lemma 1, eqs. 2–3, pp. 2489–2490).
- **Equilibrium notion.** Nash: B's trading and monitoring optimal given the pricing rule; the pricing rule zero-profit given B's strategy (p. 2489). Parameter restriction X ≤ 8c so µ ≤ 1.
- **Closed forms.** µ = βX/4c; β = min(1/λ, α) = min(ν(1−α), α) (eqs. 4–5, p. 2490; Proof of Lemma 2, p. 2506).
- **Market-efficiency metric.** π_X = ½[µ²(1 − e^(−λβ))/(1 + e^(−λβ)) + 1], the ratio of the expected price of a high-quality firm to X; π_X = 1 is full efficiency (eq. 7, p. 2492).
- **Real layer (Section II).** A manager weights ω on the t = 2 price and 1 − ω on t = 3 value. At t = 0 he picks investment θ ∈ [0,1]: value becomes X + gθ but s_b is emitted with probability θ². The project is *unknown to B and to the market maker*, so B cannot possibly be intervening (p. 2494). Solution θ = min{ [(1−ω)/(2ωX)]·[g/(1−π_X)], 1 } (Lemma 3, eq. 11, p. 2494).

## 4. Institutional facts used

The model is institution-free; institutional facts appear only as motivation and as three asides.

- **Schedule 13D / Section 13 appears three times, all outside the model.** (i) p. 2493: other participants "may be able to observe blockholders' sales with a lag by studying Section 13 filings" — an unmodelled robustness remark. (ii) p. 2497 n.18: "(After the purchase, α becomes publicly known through Section 13 filings.)" — used only to justify calling α public. (iii) p. 2499: "certain shareholders choose to hold fewer than 5% of a firm's shares to avoid triggering a Section 13(d) filing, or hold fewer than 10% to avoid being classified as an insider" — cited as a reason α may fail to respond to liquidity, i.e. a reason the exogenous-α case (Prop. 3) matters.
- **The 5% threshold level is named once** (p. 2499) and once in n.4 (p. 2483, Holderness's 5% definition). **The filing window is never mentioned** — no 10-day rule, no 5-day rule, no timing of any filing anywhere in the paper.
- **Disclosure enters the model only as an unmodelled shifter of ν**: "The parameter ν captures factors other than free float (1 − α) that affect liquidity, such as transaction costs, taxes, disclosure requirements, and other regulations." (p. 2488).
- **Ownership facts used for motivation:** 96% of US firms have a 5% blockholder (Holderness 2009); only 20% (10%) of large (medium) US firms have a 20% blockholder (La Porta–Lopez-de-Silanes–Shleifer 1999), n.4 p. 2483. Graham–Harvey–Rajgopal (2005): 78% of executives would sacrifice long-term value to meet earnings targets (p. 2482).
- **The control threshold, named once (added by verifier).** n.4, p. 2483 carries the paper's only statement of what confers control: La Porta et al. "estimate that a 20% stake gives effective control if the shareholder is an insider; the threshold is likely to be higher for outside shareholders." Edmans's blockholder is, by construction, below any such threshold.
- **Accumulation under camouflage is named once and outsourced (added by verifier).** n.18, p. 2497: "In Kyle and Vila (1991), the blockholder camouflages her purchase by trading with liquidity investors. In their model, this leads to an additional benefit of liquidity—it facilitates initial block acquisition." This is the only place the *purchase* side — the accumulation path that a 13(d) window regulates — appears at all, and it is assigned to another paper.
- **No data set. No sample, period, or estimate is produced anywhere in the paper.**

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths:** The canonical exit-governance model; the short-sales constraint gives a genuinely new and micro-founded link from stake size to information acquisition, which prior Kyle-in-corporate-finance papers assumed. Closed form throughout — every claim is an analytical proposition with a proof. The liquidity result is stated in the sharpest possible form (exact interior optimum ν* = α/(1−α)), and the exogenous-vs-endogenous-α contrast with Maug (1998) is made explicit and defended (pp. 2498–2499). The real-effects layer is clean because the investment project is unknown to the blockholder, which forecloses any "she is secretly intervening" reading. **He also maps the prior art on liquidity himself (added by verifier):** n.16, p. 2496 records that in Kahn–Winton (1998) "liquidity has no effect, rather than a nonmonotonic effect", and that Bolton–von Thadden (1998) do have an interior optimal liquidity but only because their liquidity *is* (1 − α), so more liquidity requires a smaller stake; n.17, p. 2496 records that in Faure-Grimaud–Gromb (2004) liquidity *encourages* intervention and "exit is not intentional"; n.20, p. 2498 gives the exact Maug (1998) condition — liquidity is undesirable there if exogenous α exceeds c_M/(H − L). Four footnotes, ready-made, for placing any new liquidity result.

**Weaknesses / open flanks (all of them our whitespace):**
- **Noise trading is one-sided and exponential**, chosen for tractability; a sale is fully revealed whenever u ≤ β. Liquidity is a scalar ν with no institutional content.
- **The blockholder never buys in the core model** (n.8, p. 2488) — only sells or holds. So there is no accumulation problem, no threshold to cross, and hence no room for a disclosure rule.
- **α is either exogenous or chosen once at t = 0 in an unobserved trade.** There is no *path* of accumulation and therefore no window during which a stake is partly built.
- **No control outcome of any kind.** No bidder, no tender offer, no premium, no campaign. *(Corrected by verifier — the original count was too low.)* "Takeover" appears **four** times in the body, never as a modelled object: p. 2485, where "regulations against takeover defenses" is listed as a standard remedy for shirking that would make myopia worse; p. 2494, as one of several exogenous motivations for ω > 0 (Stein 1988); p. 2495, "equity compensation and a more active takeover market are potential solutions. However, if myopia is the principal issue, such measures make it worse."; and p. 2502, citing Atanassov (2008) on antitakeover legislation and patent citations. "Premium" appears exactly once in the whole paper — in the Porter (1992) epigraph on p. 2481, in the sense of "placing a premium on investment". There is no takeover premium anywhere.
- **He makes a positive claim *against* control rights that a control-outcome paper must answer (added by verifier).** p. 2497: a short-term blockholder's threat to punish investment by selling "is not credible as it is dynamically inconsistent", because low earnings are already in the price; and "not only is it unnecessary for blockholders to have control rights in order to exert governance, but it may also be undesirable". p. 2495 gives the policy version quoted above. Our control-outcome claim has to meet this head-on, not ignore it.
- **One-sided noise is defended, not merely assumed (added by verifier).** p. 2489: the results "continue to hold if the distribution of liquidity trades is transposed downwards, so that the bulk of such trades are sales", and the free-float–liquidity link is argued from Merton (1987)-style investor recognition and from Bolton–von Thadden, Kahn–Winton, Holmstrom–Tirole. Attacking the noise distribution alone will not land; the exploitable feature is the *action space* (sell-or-hold, no purchase), not the density.
- **The hump is fragile to the very margin we study.** Prop. 4 kills it by endogenising α; Edmans himself notes (p. 2499) that a 5% disclosure trigger is one reason α may not adjust — i.e. the disclosure rule is what preserves his hump, but he never studies it.
- **Zero empirical work.** Section III is a wish list; Edmans is candid that block size is endogenous and that "cross-sectional correlations can only be interpreted as tentative support" (p. 2502).

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

- **OBJECT.** Price efficiency π_X (an expected-price-to-fundamental ratio) and managerial real investment θ. Secondarily firm value and the manager's earnings-manipulation choice (§II.B, p. 2499–2500). The **model** never produces an announcement return, a takeover premium, bidder entry, or campaign success; there is no acquirer and no control transaction in it. *(Corrected by verifier: Section III does state one announcement-return prediction — the event-study sign of an unanticipated block-size change flips with initial block size (p. 2503), read against Wruck (1989) private equity placements (pp. 2503–2504). It is a prediction about block trades, not about control, and it is a prediction, not a result.)*
- **MARGIN of a disclosure rule: NONE.** No disclosure margin — neither the threshold level nor the filing window — is a choice variable, a parameter, or a comparative static. The 5% threshold is named once as an anecdotal explanation of why stakes cluster low (p. 2499); the window is never named at all. Disclosure requirements are folded, unnamed and unmodelled, into the scalar ν (p. 2488).
- **IDENTIFICATION: theory only.** No estimation, no simulation, no numerical grid. Section III lists what an empiricist would have to do and explicitly defers it: block size is endogenous, so "it is insufficient simply to document significant correlations" and one must "identify sources of exogenous variation in block size" (p. 2500). The instruments he suggests are others' to implement. *(added by verifier — the specifics matter for us.)* **Instruments named:** "a negative liquidity shock, a sudden increase in surplus cash (from the sale of other holdings or investor inflows), temporary stock underpricing, a change in management at the blockholder, or **a change in regulation**" (p. 2502); Becker–Cronqvist–Fahlenbrach's (2008) density of wealthy individuals near headquarters (p. 2503); Wruck's (1989) private equity placements, for block changes not motivated by information (pp. 2503–2504). **Proxies named:** Tobin's q as the indirect measure of unobservable intangible investment; R&D as an input measure and patents as an output measure (p. 2502); ownership *concentration* in preference to total institutional ownership as the measure of investor informedness (pp. 2500–2501, 2486). **Sample restrictions he imposes on any test:** exclude inside blockholders and blockholders who rarely trade on information (families, index funds), and focus on settings where short-sale costs are nontrivial (pp. 2500–2501). Note that "a change in regulation" is Edmans's own suggested instrument for block size — the closest the paper comes to our identification strategy, named and never used.
- **Intervention/voice is assumed away by construction** — the whole point. "This paper shows that blockholders can improve firm value even if they are unable to intervene (engage in 'voice') and can only trade (engage in 'exit')." (p. 2483).
- **On disclosure rules and takeovers as future work: no such sentence exists.** The paper's forward-looking passages (pp. 2504–2505) point future theory at "more complex effects typically featured in asset pricing models of informed trading" and at multiple blockholders (Edmans–Manso), and future empirics at treating blockholders as informed traders. Neither disclosure rules nor takeovers are ever named as a research agenda. The closest the paper comes to acknowledging a disclosure margin is the aside on p. 2499 quoted at Q7 — an unmodelled explanation of why α may be sticky, not a call for future work. **Recorded as: silent, not deferred.**

## 7. Implications for our position

**What Edmans occupies:** OBJECT = price efficiency and real investment; MARGIN = none (disclosure appears only as an unnamed component of the liquidity scalar ν); IDENTIFICATION = theory only. He is therefore an **antecedent**, not a competitor: he owns the liquidity axis and the exit mechanism, and leaves both the disclosure margin and every control outcome open.

**How he constrains us:**

1. **The liquidity hump is his, and it is a hump in a different object.** Prop. 3 says *price efficiency and investment* are inverse-U in liquidity ν with peak ν* = α/(1−α), **holding block size exogenous**. Our draft_v2 R1 (minority gains from control non-monotone in κ) is a hump in a *control outcome*, which is new — but we must never state "liquidity has a hump-shaped effect" as if it were ours. We cite Prop. 3 as the antecedent and say precisely what is different: our object is a control outcome, not price efficiency; our non-monotonicity is certified on a grid (NUMERICAL), his is PROVED.
2. **The exogenous/endogenous-α distinction is the load-bearing caveat.** Prop. 4 destroys the hump the moment the blockholder can freely size her stake to liquidity. Any hump we claim in κ must survive an endogenous-stake objection, or must be explicitly conditioned on something that pins the stake. This is a gift: **the disclosure rule is exactly such a pin, and Edmans says so himself** — p. 2499 names the 5% Section 13(d) trigger as a reason stakes do not rise with liquidity. Our threshold margin therefore has a ready-made antecedent justification for studying exogenous-stake comparative statics, in Edmans's own words.
3. **He supplies the mechanism decomposition we should re-use verbatim.** Trading effect / camouflage effect / effort effect (p. 2492). If our GE decomposition maps onto these three names, the referee reads it instantly.
4. **He forecloses one cheap claim.** "Blockholders matter without control rights" is 2009 news, not 2026 news. Our contribution cannot be the exit channel; it must be the **partition** (flagged vs pooled) that a disclosure rule imposes on his market maker's inference — something his one-sided exponential noise and sell-or-hold action space cannot express.
5. **Whitespace confirmed on all three coordinates.** Object: no control outcome anywhere. Margin: neither threshold nor window is a parameter; the window is never even mentioned. Identification: no data. Nothing in this paper competes with a liquidity × disclosure-rule × control-outcome position.
