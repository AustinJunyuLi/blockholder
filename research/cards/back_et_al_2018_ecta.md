## 1. Question

Does market liquidity help or hurt shareholder activism, and hence economic efficiency? The prior literature splits: Coffee (1991)/Bhide (1993) say liquid markets let a blockholder "take the Wall Street walk" instead of engaging; Maug (1998) says liquid markets let a would-be activist accumulate the stake that makes engagement worth it. The paper asks whether that disagreement is really about *the activism technology* — the shape of the map from block size to value created — and it asks the reverse question the literature had largely ignored: how does the *possibility* of activism feed back into market liquidity? The vehicle is a continuous-time Kyle model in which the strategic trader's private information is her own stake, and firm value is endogenous to that stake.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Primitives.** Continuous-time Kyle (1985)/Back (1992) market on [0, T]. One strategic trader (the potential activist), noise traders, competitive risk-neutral market makers.

- The trader has **no** private information about exogenous firm value; her only private information is her own initial stake X₀, which the market prices as X₀ ~ N(μ_x, σ_x) (p.1436). Noise order flow Z is a zero-drift Brownian motion with instantaneous standard deviation σ; total order flow Y_t = X_t − X₀ + Z_t.
- **Activism technology.** At the fixed date T the trader chooses a share value v at cost C(v), C lower semicontinuous into [0, ∞], with lim_{v→±∞} |C(v)/v| = ∞ (eq. 1, p.1435). Given terminal block X_T = x she solves G(x) = sup_v {vx − C(v)} (eq. 2), and V(x) = argmax is both the common share value after effort and the trader's marginal value of a share (p.1436). G convex ⇒ V = G′ increasing; the **curvature of V (i.e. the third derivative of G)** is the object that signs almost every comparative static.
- **Liquidity** in this model is *two different things, deliberately*: the exogenous noise-trading volatility σ, and the endogenous market illiquidity λ̄ = λ(0,0) = E[average Kyle lambda] (p.1441). **Economic efficiency is measured by the initial price P̄ = P(0,0)**, i.e. value per share, *not* net of the activist's costs — because cost is total and value is per share, so the two are in different units (p.1441).
- **Equilibrium notion.** Kyle equilibrium: a pair (P, θ) with θ optimal given P subject to a no-doubling admissibility condition (eq. 9), and P(t, Y_t) = E[V(X_T) | ℱ_t^Y] (eq. 6, p.1437). Λ ≡ (σ√T + √(σ²T + σ_x²))/(σ√T) is the amplification factor; V[X_T] = (σ√T + √(σ²T + σ_x²))² (pp. 1438, 1457).

**Method.** Closed-form solution via a generalised Brownian-bridge construction (Lemma 1) plus verification of the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation (Appendix B). Comparative statics proved from the integral representations P̄ = E[V(μ_x + ΛY_T)], λ̄ = ΛE[V′(μ_x + ΛY_T)] (eqs. 21–22, p.1442). Five worked examples with closed-form C, G, V, h, P, λ (Table I, p.1444).

**Data / empirics: none.** The published Econometrica paper has sections 1 Introduction, 2 Related Literature, 3 Model, 4 Equilibrium, 5 Liquidity and Activism, 6 Examples, 7 Discussion, 8 Conclusion, and Appendices A–D. There is **no empirical section, no sample, no regression, no estimate, and no standard error anywhere in the paper.** The header states it "consolidates and substantially extends the *theory parts* of two prior working papers: Back, Li, and Ljungqvist (2015) and Collin-Dufresne and Fos (2015b)" (p.1431, emphasis ours) — the empirical work of those predecessors did not travel with it.

**Takeover / bidder / premium object: absent.** A full-text search returns no bidder, no tender offer, no takeover premium, and no control transfer as modelled objects. So Examples 4–5 are offered as reduced forms *for* control-type campaigns. A referee who says "Example 5 is your takeover" must be answered on the counterparty, not on the topic.

## 4. Institutional facts used

- **Schedule 13D, 5% threshold** (p.1436, fn. 13): "Investors with activist intentions are required to submit a Schedule 13D filing to the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) only once their ownership reaches 5% of a targeted firm's outstanding shares." Used solely to motivate the assumption that X₀ is private.
- **Section 13(f)** of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (pp. 1436 fn. 13, 1453): quarterly disclosure of long (not short) positions in U.S. stocks and options by institutional investment managers with more than $100 million AUM. Between filings only the manager knows her position; smaller managers and short positions escape entirely. Used to motivate σ_x > 0 and to interpret comparative statics in σ_x as disclosure-precision changes.
- **The pre-disclosure period / filing window** (p.1434): "the period between the date at which the investor exceeds the 5% ownership threshold and the date at which the 13D must be filed," citing Bebchuk, Brav, Jackson, and Jiang (2013). Discussed again p.1452. The window is *not* modelled as a window; it is mapped into cumulative noise trading σ²T (p.1453). **(added by verifier)** The same paragraph continues: "The relation is also central to the debate about insider trading rules and, more generally, about required disclosure rules for the trading positions of significant blockholders (e.g., Fishman and Hagerty (1992, 1995))" (p.1434) — i.e. they claim the *whole* blockholder-disclosure-rule debate as within their relation's reach, which raises rather than lowers the bar on how we distinguish our margin.
- **Two named policy levers on the 13(f) precision channel (added by verifier)** (p.1453): "Changes to Section 13(f) — say, to the frequency of 13(f) filings or the inclusion of short positions — could, therefore, affect investors' uncertainty about an activist investor's initial toehold." *Filing frequency* is the closest thing in the paper to a window margin, and it is routed through σ_x (precision), not through a partition. Same page: a **Tobin tax** is offered as a second real-world shifter of noise trading.
- **Descriptive activism facts** (p.1431): *The Economist* (7 Feb 2015) — between 2010 and 2014, half the S&P 500 had an activist shareholder and one in seven was the target of a campaign.
- **Calibration anchors quoted from the empirical literature** (p.1441, fn. 17): Collin-Dufresne and Fos (2015a) report the average activist holds **7.51%** of the target's shares at first Schedule 13D; Collin-Dufresne, Fos, and Muravyev (2017, printed as "2107") report exposure rises to **8.70%** once derivatives are counted. Used to argue the activist recoups costs from a 7–8% toehold, so costs per share are small relative to value.

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths**
- Closed-form continuous-time equilibrium for a *general* cost/activism technology — not a functional-form special case. That is the paper's Econometrica-level contribution.
- It converts a long-running verbal dispute (Coffee/Bhide vs Maug) into a single testable primitive: the convexity or concavity of V. This is a genuinely clarifying reduction.
- It separates exogenous noise trading σ from endogenous market liquidity 1/λ̄ and shows they can move in opposite directions — a distinction most of the prior literature elides (their explicit contrast with Treynor 1971 / Kyle's lambda, p.1433).
- The reverse channel (activism → liquidity) is new and is what generates the negative cross-sectional efficiency–liquidity relation.
- Full proofs in appendices; the one-period appendix (D) honestly shows the tractability is a continuous-time artefact.

**Weaknesses / open flanks**
- **No empirics.** Nothing is estimated. The paper's own "large-sample evidence" paragraph (p.1452) points at Brav et al. (2008)'s campaign-goal taxonomy as a *way someone else could* identify activism technologies; it does not do so.
- **The comparative static is not signable without knowing V's curvature**, and the paper says it cannot in general sign G‴ (p.1441). So "does liquidity raise activism?" has the answer "it depends on an unobservable" — which is exactly the flank an empirical or institutional refinement can occupy.
- **Efficiency is measured gross of the activist's cost** (p.1441), by the authors' own admission, because cost and value are in incompatible units. Welfare claims are therefore weaker than "economic efficiency" suggests.
- **V unbounded.** A convex V is unbounded above and a concave V unbounded below (violating limited liability, fn. 18 p.1442); the two bounded examples (4 and 5) are neither convex nor concave, so Theorem 2's clean dichotomy does not cover them — and those are the binary campaigns the empirical literature actually studies.
- **T is exogenous and effort happens once, at T.** No pre- vs post-disclosure regime distinction, no trading after the flag, no partition of market beliefs into flagged/pooled states.
- **One activist, no rival, no bidder, no target management.** *(Corrected by verifier: the p.1454 future-work list flags only the single-activist assumption. Its six items are the fixed disclosure horizon, common-knowledge activist technology, all uncertainty resolved at T, risk neutrality, shares-only trading, and one activist. Rival bidders and target management are simply absent — not flagged as extensions anywhere.)*
- **Their own empirical caution cuts at us too (added by verifier).** p.1452: pooling different activism technologies "could fail to find significant effects even if uncertainty matters." Any event-study or DiD leg of ours that pools campaign types is pre-criticised by the competitor.
- Minor: Collin-Dufresne, Fos, and Muravyev is misprinted as "(2107)" **both** in fn. 17 (p.1441) and in the reference list (p.1462).
- Minor: the Table II caption (p.1445) attributes ∂P̄/∂μ_x ≥ 0 to "Theorem 1"; the result is Theorem 2(ii)(a). Second typo in the same neighbourhood as the Figure 3 caption error (R14).

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**Object.** They study *economic efficiency* = the initial share price P̄(0,0), and *market illiquidity* = Kyle's lambda λ̄. They do **not** study any control outcome: no bidder entry, no takeover premium, no campaign success probability as an equilibrium object (Example 5's "probability of success" is a choice variable priced into v, not a control outcome with a counterparty). No announcement return.

**Margin of the disclosure rule: neither, in the model.** The **threshold margin (5%)** appears only as motivation for private X₀ (p.1436 fn. 13) and as a future-work item (p.1454) — no agent in the model ever crosses a threshold and no state is "flagged". The **window margin** is discussed twice (pp. 1434, 1452) but is *collapsed into* σ²T rather than modelled: on p.1453 they say reducing T is "isomorphic to reducing noise trading volatility." A disclosure rule in this paper is thus a scalar rescaling of noise trading, not a partition of the market's information.

**Identification.** Theory only. No structural estimation, no reduced-form design, no calibration exercise beyond the illustrative grids (σ_x = 0.1, T = 1 in Fig. 3).

**Declared out of scope, in their words:**
- Endogenous disclosure horizon — the *first* item on the future-work list: **"First, we assume that the horizon at which the activist's stake is disclosed is fixed and common knowledge. Depending on the application, it might be interesting to endogenize the horizon. In the United States, for example, activists have to disclose their stake to regulators when it reaches a regulatory threshold of 5%."** (p.1454)
- Endogenous T as a choice variable: "Allowing T to be a choice variable would be an interesting extension, but it is beyond the scope of this paper." (p.1435, fn. 10)
- Private information about the activist's own technology/productivity; new information acquired on becoming active; risk aversion; derivatives positions; **multiple competing activists ("wolf-pack" campaigns)** — all p.1454.
- Discrete-time convergence: "We have not checked this convergence result for our model, but we conjecture that it would be true." (p.1435, fn. 9)
- Minimum block size for activism to succeed: shown to play "only a minor role" in a predecessor paper and dropped (p.1435, fn. 11).

## 7. Implications for our position

**What of ours this paper already owns.** The liquidity × activism cell, with object = *price-based efficiency*, margin = *none (σ²T only)*, identification = *theory, closed form, general technology*. Any claim of the form "more liquidity makes engagement more likely" is theirs (via Theorem 2(i) plus the convex examples), and any claim of the form "the answer depends on the shape of the value-creation technology" is theirs too. We must not restate either as our contribution.

**Where the whitespace is, precisely.**
1. **Object.** They stop at P̄ and λ̄. They have no bidder, no premium, no control transfer — no *control outcome* in our glossary's sense. Our premium wedge (m₁ − m₀) has no counterpart here. This is the cleanest separation and should carry the position.
2. **Margin.** Their treatment of the disclosure rule is a *scalar*: shortening the window = shrinking σ²T (p.1453, verbatim in Q7 below). Our **partition** — flagged vs pooled market states — is a different object entirely, and the **threshold margin** (who gets flagged) has no representation at all in their model, only in a footnote and a future-work sentence. Occupying the threshold margin, or the window margin *as a partition rather than as a rescaling of σ*, is open ground they explicitly hand over (p.1454, Q8).
3. **Identification.** They have none. A theory + one clean empirical estimate around the Feb-2024 acceleration sits in territory this paper does not touch.

**Where they constrain us.**
- **Do not claim a sign for liquidity → engagement without a curvature assumption.** Theorem 2 is the standing result: our comparative statics in κ must either (a) state the analogue of the convexity condition, or (b) show why our control-outcome object signs where their price object does not. Sloppiness here is the most likely referee kill.
- **Do not conflate noise trading with market liquidity.** They proved (R7, R13) that more noise trading can *reduce* market liquidity. Our κ is noise-trading intensity; if we ever call κ "liquidity" and then compare to an Amihud measure in the empirics, we owe the reader this distinction and a citation to p.1443.
- **Do not claim the "liquidity raises activism" result is unconditional or new.** Maug (1998) owns the binary version; Back et al. own the general version and the refutation of the stake-threshold condition (R12).
- **The disclosure-attenuation claim (draft_v2's T2) needs a margin.** In their framework a shorter window is just less cumulative noise trading, so "stricter disclosure attenuates the liquidity–outcome link" would, in their world, be a re-parameterisation, not a mechanism. Our T2 is only interesting if the flagged/pooled partition — not σ²T — does the work. **This is the single most important discipline this paper imposes on our position.**
- **Their `efficiency gross of cost` caveat** is a template for how to state our own measurement limitation honestly.
- **(added by verifier) Our empirical leg must split campaign types, not pool them.** p.1452 states that pooling across activism technologies can null out a real effect. If our Feb-2024 estimate pools all 13D campaigns and finds nothing, this paper supplies the referee's explanation for the null; if it finds something, the referee will ask which technology drove it. Pre-register the split (control-seeking vs governance-only campaigns) rather than defending a pooled coefficient.
- **(added by verifier) Do not lean on Theorem 2 as an "if and only if".** See R4. It is two one-way implications and it is silent exactly where the empirically relevant technologies live (bounded, neither convex nor concave — their own Examples 4 and 5). This *helps* us: the case the data is about is the case their theorem does not sign.

**Referee risk to pre-empt.** A referee who knows this paper will ask: "Isn't your κ result Back et al. Theorem 2 with a different label?" The answer must be a control outcome they do not have plus a partition they do not have — stated in one sentence, with the p.1454 quote as the receipt.
