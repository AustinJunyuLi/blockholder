## 1. Question
What do we know about large shareholders in corporate governance — who they are, how many there are, through which channels they govern (voice = direct intervention; exit = selling), and what the evidence shows? The chapter has four jobs: (i) ground blockholders in property-rights logic (collocation of decision rights and wealth effects, plus alienability); (ii) interrogate what a "blockholder" even is, and argue the 5% convention has no theoretical basis; (iii) present *new* hand-collected descriptive evidence on US block ownership; (iv) build one unifying model that nests the main voice and exit theories in common notation, then survey the empirics and the identification problem. It closes with an explicit methodological argument that clean identification should not be a prerequisite for blockholder research, and a long list of open questions.

## 2. Model / data and method

**Unifying model (§5, pp. 19–44; proofs Appendix A pp. 79–85; techniques Appendix B pp. 86–93).** A modified version of Edmans, Levit & Reilly (2016). Risk-neutral players, shares normalised to 1, four periods, no discounting. Blockholder owns α ∈ (0,1] at t=0. Action a ∈ {0,1}; a=1 raises value from V̲ to V̄ ≡ V̲ + ΔV̲^γ and costs the actor c, drawn from cdf F and privately observed. γ indexes whether monitoring is additive (γ=0, % stake is the relevant measure) or multiplicative (γ=1, $ stake is the relevant measure). In *voice* models the blockholder takes the action; in *exit* models a manager takes it and maximises ωP + (1−ω)V. Liquidity traders sell φ w.p. β; φ = ν(1−α) with ν the liquidity parameter. A competitive market maker prices from order flow. Five thresholds are derived in sequence: c*₀ (no trade, eq. 2, p. 23), c*₁ (camouflaged cut-and-run, eq. 4, p. 25), c*₂ (blockholder liquidity shock, eq. 5, p. 26), c*₄ (buy *and* sell allowed, eq. 6, p. 29), c*₅ (exit / manager effort, eq. 11, p. 35), plus endogenous investigation γ* (eq. 12, p. 38).

**New descriptive data (§4, pp. 13–19; Tables 1–7, pp. 94–101).** Hand-collected from **proxy statements of 375 randomly selected CRSP- and Compustat-listed US corporations**, mostly early 1995 (Feb–Mar), the sample of Holderness (2009). Collected: every ≥5% holder, board representation, blockholder type, CEO and board ownership. 950 blockholders in 360 firms. Dollar figures inflated by the CRSP equal-weighted price index from 1995-01-01 to 2015-12-31 (factor 6.75). Methods: descriptive tables, marginal probits (Table 5, n=940), probit + OLS on ownership determinants (Table 7, n=367), robust p-values.

**Modelling-technique taxonomy (Appendix B, pp. 86–93; Table 9, pp. 103–106).** Classifies **16** trading-blockholder models (corrected by verifier — the card said 14; counting Table 9's rows gives Shleifer–Vishny 1986, Admati–Pfleiderer–Zechner 1994, Maug 1998, Kahn–Winton 1998, Bolton–von Thadden 1998, Faure-Grimaud–Gromb 2004, Admati–Pfleiderer 2009, Edmans 2009, Edmans–Manso 2011, Khanna–Mathews 2012, Levit 2013, Goldman–Strobl 2013, Song 2015, Dasgupta–Piacentino 2015, Fos–Kahn 2016, Edmans–Levit–Reilly 2016) on four axes: players, blockholder's trading motive, price formation (Kyle 1985 total order flow / Glosten–Milgrom direction-only / individual trades with unknown identity / directly observable trade), and how initial block size is pinned down (exogenous, firm-value optimum, social optimum, private optimum). **No axis and no cell in Table 9 refers to a disclosure rule, a threshold, or a filing deadline** (verified column by column: the six columns are Model / Governance / Players / Blockholder's Trading Motive / Price Formation / Initial Block Size).

## 4. Institutional facts used
- **US Schedule 13 at the 5% threshold** (pp. 60–61): on acquiring 5% a shareholder must file a Schedule 13 — 13D if she intends to intervene, stating the intended form of intervention in **Item 4**; 13G if she does not, shorter with fewer disclosure requirements. Passive holders may still file 13D but rarely do (p. 61, fn. 49). **No filing deadline, window, or amendment rule is stated anywhere in the chapter.**
- **13F filings**: institutional investors with ≥$100M of assets must disclose sub-5% positions; named as the data source for studying blocks below 5% (p. 12) and used to describe Apple/GE ownership (p. 14).
- **5% is a reporting mandate, twice stated**: proxies list every ≥5% holder, "which is the level at which they are legally mandated to report publicly" (p. 13) — the same framing as p. 11 (added by verifier).
- **50.1% has no clustering**: a few theory papers (e.g. Shleifer & Vishny 1986) attach significance to 50.1% majority ownership, but the survey notes we do not observe a clustering of ownership slightly above 50% (Holderness 2009), and that even majority holders face fiduciary and pro-rata constraints (p. 11) (added by verifier).
- **10% insider threshold**: at 10% a shareholder is an insider under securities law and must comply with insider-trading rules even without a board seat (p. 16).
- **Investment Company Act of 1940** diversification limits: a "diversified" mutual fund may, on 75% of its portfolio, hold no more than 5% in one security and no more than 10% of one company's voting rights (p. 60, fn. 48); "prudent man" rules constrain pension funds (p. 60).
- **State corporation law**: distributions must be strictly pro rata; managers generally may not favour some shareholders over others — which bounds where blockholder expropriation is plausible (p. 52).
- **Decimalization of US exchanges, 2001** — the liquidity shock used by Norli et al. (2015), Fang et al. (2009), and Edmans, Fang & Zur (2013) (pp. 67, 69).
- **Russell 1000/2000 index assignment** as the workhorse instrument for institutional/index ownership, with Appel, Gormley & Keim (2016a)'s methodological caveats (pp. 57, 64).
- **1992 proxy reform** reducing shareholder communication costs (pp. 58, 61); **2003 mutual-fund late-trading scandal** (p. 57); EU financial transaction tax agreed by ten member states for September 2016 (p. 25); Florange law (France, 2014) double voting rights after two years, plus the reversed Italian equivalent, Toyota's five-year share class (June 2015), and Clinton's July 2015 short-horizon capital-gains proposal (p. 40).
- **Data**: 375 randomly selected CRSP/Compustat firms, 1995 proxies; CRSP equal-weighted price index adjustment factor 6.75 for 1995→2015 dollars (pp. 13–14, Table 1 p. 94).

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses
**Strengths.** (i) One notation covers voice and exit, so a reader can see exactly which force flips which comparative static — the c*₀…c*₅ ladder is the cleanest available statement of "why liquidity cuts both ways". (ii) Genuinely new descriptive evidence from hand-collected proxies, not a recycled database; the authors are explicit that electronic block data are unreliable (p. 14). (iii) Appendix B is a rare, honest catalogue of *which modelling device buys which tractability and at what cost* — including the admission that Admati–Pfleiderer-type setups "do not allow an analysis of the effect of liquidity on governance, since there are no liquidity traders or liquidity parameter" (p. 86). (iv) An unusually forthright methodological stance on identification, backed by the Atanasov–Black count. (v) **(added by verifier)** It names a rival simple unifying model of liquidity × governance — Foucault, Pagano & Röell (2013) — as "quite different" (p. 20); we should know that map exists before claiming ours is the only one. (vi) Its status as *the* handbook chapter means its framing is the default prior a referee brings.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.** (i) The descriptive sample is **1995 proxies** — twenty-two years stale at writing, and pre-dating almost every development the chapter itself calls important (index-fund growth, modern hedge-fund activism). (ii) **The disclosure rule is treated purely as a data convention.** The 5% threshold is dismissed as having "no theoretical basis"; the chapter never asks what the rule *does* to equilibrium behaviour. (iii) **The filing window does not exist in this chapter.** No 10-day rule, no amendment deadline, no pre-filing accumulation period as an economic object — only Collin-Dufresne & Fos's 60-day disclosed-trades measurement (pp. 68–69). (iv) Control outcomes are surveyed as *effects of activism* (takeover likelihood, premia) but never as an endogenous outcome of the trading game; the unifying model's firm value is binary {V̲, V̄} with no bidder, no tender price, and no premium — the sole exception being the restatement of Shleifer–Vishny's π*(α) (pp. 31–33). (v) The chapter's own liquidity conclusion — "generally improves governance" (p. 44) — is a sign statement, never a magnitude, and never conditioned on the disclosure regime. (vi) Written before the 2024 SEC acceleration, so its silence on the window is chronological as much as conceptual.

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**(a) How the survey frames disclosure rules: as a measurement convention, explicitly NOT as an economic force.**
The 5% threshold is introduced only to be dismissed as arbitrary, and its origin is named as a disclosure mandate rather than economics:

> "There is no unambiguous definition of a blockholder. There is no theoretical basis for the commonly used 5% threshold or indeed any threshold. Future research should study blocks below 5% when possible." (p. 4)

> "Some papers use a threshold of 10% or 20% to classify blockholders, but we are unaware of any theoretical basis for these thresholds. Others use 5%, but this is because most countries (including the United States) mandate public disclosure at this level, rather than a theoretical justification." (p. 11)

The prescription that follows is a *sampling* prescription, not a modelling one: "our suggestion is that researchers err on the side of expanding the fractional range of ownership in their investigations rather than narrowing it. In particular, it would be interesting to study blocks of less than 5%." (p. 12). When 13D/13G appears in §6 it is always an empiricist's *label for intent* (active vs. passive), never a constraint in an agent's problem. **No model in the chapter — neither the unifying model of §5 nor any of the **16** models classified in Table 9 (pp. 103–106) — contains a disclosure rule, a threshold, or a mandated revelation.** **One qualification (added by verifier):** in the Shleifer–Vishny restatement the blockholder "attempts to buy α_min − α shares through a **publicly-observed tender offer**" (p. 31), and La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer (1999) are cited for the conjecture that α_min = 0.2 may suffice for effective control. So one model does contain a *publicly observed* purchase — but it is a bid, priced by the free-rider condition, not a mandated revelation of an accumulated stake, and no threshold in it is set by a rule. The scope claim stands; the sentence should be written as "no model contains a **mandated disclosure** rule" rather than "no model contains any observability primitive". Table 9's own scope note confirms what its axes are: "This table only considers models where the blockholder trades, since the goal is to demonstrate the different ways in which trading can be modelled." (p. 103). The single place disclosure is treated as an institutional *variable* is a cross-country future-research bullet, and even there it is one item in a list:

> "Consider settings beyond the United States. As alluded to above, the institutional framework (including disclosure rules, the ability of blockholders to act in concert or have privileged access to management, stock liquidity, and voting arrangement) likely differs across countries." (p. 75)

**(b) The filing window: never mentioned.** There is no reference in 108 printed pages to a 13D filing deadline, a ten-day rule, a 13D/A amendment period, or a pre-filing accumulation window as an economic margin. The closest the chapter comes is a *measurement* use of the disclosure requirement: "Collin-Dufresne and Fos (2015) show that the trades made by 13D filers over the 60 days before the filing date (which must be disclosed in the filing) are highly profitable." (p. 68) and, on the buy side, "Collin-Dufresne and Fos (2015) find that purchasers by eventual 13D filers, over the 60 days before the filing date, increase prices." (p. 69). The 60 days is a *sample window in the data*, not the legal window, and the survey draws no rule-design implication from it.

> "Take blockholder heterogeneity seriously… and different situations (e.g. a takeover situation versus a disagreement on dividend policy). Considering heterogeneity will help us understand which types of activism are successful, under which circumstances, and by which blockholders." (p. 75)

> "Consider interactions. At least four types of interactions may be of interest." **(d) What the survey says about trading/liquidity interacting with control outcomes.** Very little, and it names the gap. Three strands only:
1. **Theory, indirect:** Kyle & Vila (1991) is the one liquidity model placed "in the specific context of takeovers" (p. 29), and Shleifer & Vishny (1986) is the one place a *premium* is endogenous — but there liquidity is absent by construction ("their are no liquidity trades (by either the blockholder or households), and so she is unable to profit from trading", p. 31).
2. **Empirics, one paper:** Roosenboom, Schlingemann & Vasconcelos (2014) — the only surveyed study whose object is liquidity × blockholder structure × an M&A outcome (p. 70).
3. **Adjacent empirics:** activism raises takeover likelihood and premia (Greenwood & Schor 2009; Boyson et al. 2016; Jiang, Li & Mei 2016; Fich, Harford & Tran 2015, pp. 58, 62) — but liquidity never enters these regressions in the survey's telling.
4. **(added by verifier) Accumulation, not control:** the survey does report one empirical result on liquidity and *pre-filing accumulation* — Gantchev & Jotikasthira (2016), that hedge-fund activists time purchases to coincide with liquidity-driven institutional selling (p. 28) — but it appears purely as an illustration of the camouflage assumption in §5.1.4, and is never connected to the 13D filing that follows, nor to any control outcome.
The chapter's own summary of takeovers is confined to one clause: hedge-fund activism affects "the likelihood and terms of being acquired" (p. 73). And the interaction with takeovers is listed as *future work* (p. 76), not as settled ground.

## 7. Implications for our position

**What this paper occupies.** *Object:* governance broadly — firm value, firm outcomes, price informativeness, block formation; takeover premia only as a surveyed downstream consequence of activism. *Margin of the disclosure rule:* **none as an economic force** — the 5% threshold is a measurement convention (p. 11), the window is absent entirely. *Identification:* survey; its own new evidence is descriptive/correlational and says so ("We do not claim causality", p. 17); its methodological verdict is that no credible blockholder instrument exists (p. 47).

**How it constrains us.**
1. **Do not claim novelty on "liquidity affects governance."** That is the chapter's second theory-summary bullet (p. 44) and its **eighth** of nine empirical-summary bullets (pp. 73–74; corrected by verifier), and both directions of the voice channel plus the exit channel are already settled here. Our contribution cannot be the sign of ∂(governance)/∂κ.
2. **Do not claim novelty on "activism changes control outcomes."** R54–R57 already give takeover likelihood 6–8× higher after a 13D, blocked low-premium deals, and higher premia with concentrated monitors.
3. **The whitespace this survey leaves is exactly our partition.** In this chapter the disclosure rule is never a *state variable* — it never partitions what the market observes, and it never enters a blockholder's optimisation. The chapter says the 5% threshold has "no theoretical basis" (p. 4); that is an invitation to supply one, not a verdict that none can exist. A model in which the rule creates a flagged/pooled partition, and in which κ shifts mass across that partition, occupies ground the field's own map marks empty.
4. **The window margin is entirely unoccupied.** In 108 pages there is not one sentence about a filing deadline. Even Collin-Dufresne & Fos's 60 days enters as a *data window* (pp. 68–69), never as a rule to be moved. Our window-margin anchor (10 → 5 business days, 2024-02-05) has no competitor in the field's canonical survey.
5. **The survey pre-authorises our modelling agenda in its own words.** The future-work bullet on financial markets asks for theories with "more complex microstructure features… that have so far been analyzed in pure-trading models in which firm value is exogenous and thus trading has no effect on governance" (pp. 76–77), and the interactions bullet lists "takeovers (Corum and Levit, 2016)" as an open interaction (p. 76). A liquidity × disclosure-partition × control-outcome model is a direct answer to both. Quote them as the field's own statement of the gap.
6. **It also pre-authorises our identification stance.** If the December package cannot deliver a clean instrument, this chapter is the citation that says clean identification is not the bar — "clean identification should not be a requirement for future empirical research because it will be too narrowing" (p. 75), and "studies should be led by economics, not econometrics" (p. 4). But note the flip side: the same authors demand direct testing of alternative explanations (Brav et al. 2008 as the template, p. 49). Our referee checklist should be framed as *that* — alternative explanations tested directly — not as an identification claim we cannot support.
7. **A caution on our own machinery.** The survey's summary of the exit/voice literature says small blockholders choose stakes where F(αΔ) < ½, so the *buy* channel dominates and liquidity helps voice (p. 30). Our four-action structure (exit / hold / quiet voice / public voice) must not accidentally assume the cut-and-run channel dominates; if it does, we are arguing against Maug's private-optimum logic as restated here, and must say so explicitly.
8. **Anchor value.** For the December package, this is the paper to cite once in the introduction to establish (a) that blockholders are ubiquitous and worth studying (96% of firms, p. 14), (b) that the 5% threshold is legally, not economically, motivated — which makes the rule an object of study rather than a nuisance parameter, and (c) that the field itself lists the missing interaction we supply.

### Omissions found and added

1. **p. 11, R41b** — "there is evidence that initial accumulations of blocks between 5% and 10% are associated with significant announcement effects" (fn. 7: Holderness & Sheehan 1985; Mikkelson & Ruback 1985). This is the *only* place the survey gives the 5%–10% band an empirical content, and the card had dropped it while quoting the surrounding paragraph. It cuts both ways for us: it is evidence the disclosure region matters empirically, and it is the survey's own reason for saying thresholds should be picked by question, not by rule.
2. **p. 28 (and p. 76), R33b** — Gantchev & Jotikasthira (2016): "hedge fund activists camouflage their purchases by timing them to coincide with liquidity-driven selling by institutions." This is the survey's one empirical result on **liquidity × pre-filing accumulation** and the card had no line for it anywhere. It is the closest existing empirical neighbour to our accumulation margin, and the survey never links it to the 13D that follows.
3. **p. 31, §6(a) qualification** — the Shleifer–Vishny blockholder buys α_min − α "through a publicly-observed tender offer", and La Porta et al. (1999) are cited for α_min = 0.2. Material because it bounds how strongly we may say "no model in the chapter has any public-revelation primitive".
4. **p. 43 fn. 41, R59b** — Barclay & Holderness (1992): block trades at a premium to the post-announcement exchange price *also* raise the stock price, so private benefits are either not at minority expense or are outweighed. Directly relevant to any premium-splitting claim in our model.
5. **p. 56, R57b** — Chen, Harford & Li (2007): independent long-term institutions are associated with superior M&A performance **and the withdrawal of bad bids**, particularly with a large stake. A control-outcome result the card's §3(e) omitted.
6. **p. 65, R58b** — Schmidt & Fahlenbrach (2016): index ownership is associated with **lower** M&A announcement returns. The card carried only the Appel et al. (2016c) side of the index-fund evidence, which is the opposite sign on control outcomes; a referee who knows this literature will expect both.
7. **p. 55, R59c** — Cremers & Nair (2005): a takeover-vulnerability portfolio earns 10–15% annualised only where public-pension ownership is high; outside blockholders and takeover exposure are complements.
8. **p. 13, §4** — the second statement of the rule-as-measurement: proxies list every ≥5% holder, "which is the level at which they are legally mandated to report publicly". Strengthens the card's own §6(a) argument.
9. **p. 11, §4** — no clustering of ownership just above 50% (Holderness 2009), and even majority holders face pro-rata and fiduciary constraints. Relevant if we ever put a control threshold in the model.
10. **p. 20, §5** — Foucault, Pagano & Röell (2013) is named as a "quite different" simple unifying model of liquidity × governance. We should not claim ours is the only unifying map without knowing that one.

### Overall verdict

**Sound.** 27/27 quotes verbatim and correctly paged, including every decision-critical one (pp. 4, 11, 44, 47, 48, 62, 70, 76–77 and the Table 9 scope note). The two load-bearing scope claims — no disclosure rule as a primitive anywhere, and no filing window in 108 printed pages — both survive an executed grep of the full text, the second without a single near-miss. Three errors found and corrected (Table 9 has 16 models not 14; the liquidity bullet is the eighth not the ninth; R35 spans pp. 69–70), none of which touches the positioning argument. Ten omissions added, of which two are decision-critical for us: Gantchev & Jotikasthira on liquidity-timed activist accumulation (p. 28), and the 5%–10% announcement-effect evidence (p. 11) — the field does have an empirical toehold in the disclosure band, and our claim to whitespace must be stated as *no theory of the rule*, not *no evidence near the rule*. One claim left UNCHECKED: the header's "vol. 1, ch. 8", which this version of the document does not state.
