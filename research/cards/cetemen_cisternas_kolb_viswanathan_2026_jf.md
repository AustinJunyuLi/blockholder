## 1. Question

How do multiple activist blockholders coordinate block accumulation *non-cooperatively*, when each one's terminal stake determines how much value-creating effort they will exert? Specifically: if a "leader" activist trades before a "follower", the leader's order flow is a public signal that the follower reads. The leader therefore has a motive beyond price impact — she can move the follower's beliefs about her own future effort, and hence the follower's willingness to build a stake and bear intervention costs. The paper asks what this does to (a) the predictability of insider trades, (b) free-riding and firm value, and (c) average prices around activism episodes. The authors position it explicitly against the Edmans–Holderness (2017) "program": p. 6, "only models with either one activist building stakes in isolation, or with multiple activists where blocks are fixed, have been studied."

## 2. Model / data and method

**Type.** Pure theory. **No data, no estimation, no sample, no calibration to moments.** All figures are illustrative numerical plots at hand-picked parameters (φ = 1, σ = .2 in Fig. 1; µ = φ = 1, σ = .8 in Fig. 2; φ = µ = 1 in Fig. 3; µ = φ = 1, σ = .2 in Fig. 4; µ = φ = 1, (ρ,σ) = (0.5, 1) in Fig. 5; φ = µ = σ = 1 in Fig. 6).

**Primitives (§2.1, pp. 8–9).**
- Two activists, a **leader** (she) and a **follower** (he), with initial blocks X₀ᴸ, X₀ᶠ ∈ ℝ, each **privately known to its holder**. Prior: jointly normal, mean µ > 0, variance φ > 0, covariance ρ ∈ [−φ, φ]. ρ is the paper's key parameter — "block interdependence".
- **Three periods, one trade each, no continuous time.** t = 1: the leader is the *unique* informed trader in a Kyle (1985) market; she submits θᴸ; the market maker sees total order flow Ψ₁ = θᴸ + σZ₁ and quotes P₁. t = 2: the follower *replaces* her as the unique informed trader; Ψ₂ = θᶠ + σZ₂, price P₂. t = 3: both simultaneously choose effort Wⁱ ∈ ℝ at private cost ½(Wⁱ)², and share value is W = Wᴸ + Wᶠ. Absent activism, share value is common knowledge and normalised to zero (p. 8).
- **"Leader" = the activist who trades first and is the sole informed trader in period 1. "Follower" = the activist who trades second, having observed P₁ (equivalently Ψ₁).** The identities are **exogenous**; who leads is not chosen. §5 studies *who would want* to lead ex ante, but never endogenises the assignment (see §6 below).
- Terminal position XTⁱ = X₀ⁱ + θⁱ. Payoff (eq. 2, p. 9): E[(Wᴸ+Wᶠ)XTⁱ − P_{t(i)}θⁱ − ½(Wⁱ)²]. Because effort is quadratic-cost and value is linear in effort, **the optimal effort is exactly the terminal block, Wⁱ = XTⁱ** — this is the functional form that buys everything. Payoffs collapse to E[(XTⁱ + XT^{−i})XTⁱ − P_{t(i)}θⁱ − ½(XTⁱ)²] (eq. 3, p. 9).
- **Value creation is effort-based and continuous.** The authors defend this as a linearisation: "while many outcomes can be binary, our model can be seen as a linearized version of such settings where the probability of success increases in total effort" (p. 12). Negative effort (value destruction) is permitted.

**Information / what is public.** Public information (F_t) is generated by the prior and the **total order flows** (Ψ₁, Ψ₂) (p. 8). Prices are public. **Individual trades and individual stakes are never public at any point** — footnote 16, p. 15: "All these expressions hold on and off the equilibrium path, as an activist's trades are hidden from others." There is no event in the model at which any activist's identity or stake becomes known to anyone.

**Equilibrium notion.** Linear-Gaussian Kyle equilibrium in **linear strategies** (eq. 4, p. 9): θᴸ = α_L X₀ᴸ + δ_L µ, θᶠ = α_F X₀ᶠ + β_F P₁ + δ_F µ; pricing affine in order flow with P_{t(i)} = E[Wᴸ+Wᶠ | F_{t(i)}]. Attention is restricted to **PBS ("positive block sensitivity") equilibria**: α_L > 0 and α_F > 0 (p. 9). Beliefs are Gaussian projections (Lemma 1, pp. 13–14). Second-order conditions are non-trivial *because* fundamentals are endogenous: 0 > 1 − 2Λ₂ (follower) and 0 > 1 − 2Λ₁(1 − β_F) (leader) (eqs. 14–15, p. 17).

**The mechanism.** The leader's FOC carries an extra term relative to Kyle: XTᴸ·∂E_L[XTᶠ]/∂θᴸ, called the **value of manipulation** (eq. 12, p. 16). Rewritten (eq. 16, p. 18) it is proportional to (∂Y₁ᶠ/∂Ψ₁ − ∂P₁/∂Ψ₁), the gap between how the *follower's* belief about the leader's terminal block and how the *price* respond to first-period order flow. Correlation ρ signs that gap: sign(∂Y₁ᶠ/∂Ψ₁ − ∂P₁/∂Ψ₁) = sign(β_F) = −sign(ρ) (p. 19–20).

**Benchmark used throughout.** α_K := √(σ²/φ), "the well-known slope coefficient in the traditional Kyle insider trading strategy" (p. 17).

## 4. Institutional facts used

All institutional material is **interpretive** — it motivates the reduced-form timing, it is never a state variable, a constraint, or a parameter of the model.

- **"Under the threshold" campaigns *(added by verifier)*.** p. 4 and fn 3, p. 4: "so-called 'under the threshold' campaigns have been on the rise"; the footnote reports that "Campaigns with blocks below 5% were majority in the U.S. in 2021 and the targets had higher market capitalization" (CNBC, 2022-01-15). This is a **threshold-margin** fact — that the 5% trigger is increasingly *avoided* — and it is the empirical motivation for their private-block assumption.
- **13D 5% threshold.** "Because blocks below 5% need not be disclosed, they can constitute private information" (p. 11). Median hedge-fund stake at disclosure 6.6% (Brav et al. 2021b); hedge funds buy ~1% of shares on the day the 5% threshold is crossed (Collin-Dufresne & Fos 2015) (p. 11). Blockholders in the 1%–5% range collectively own ~22% of the average firm vs ~20% for those above 5% (Lewellen & Lewellen 2022) (p. 11).
- **13D filing window.** Footnote 8, p. 11: the 10-day 13D deadline "has been shortened to 5 business days, while material amendments must be filed within 2 business days," citing the SEC 2023 press release (`sec.gov/news/press-release/2023-219`). The **only** other window reference is p. 10, "material adjustments to positions or intentions can be disclosed with a delay—historically, up to 10 days," and p. 13, "an average of 1% shares outstanding being purchased during the disclosure window."
- **"Trigger date."** Cited from Bebchuk et al. (2013) and Collin-Dufresne & Fos (2015): hedge funds trade "primarily on the day they cross the 5% threshold—the 'trigger date'—or the one after" (p. 10). Wong (2020): on the trigger date there is 36% more abnormality in others' trading (the sentence spans pp. 12–13); the single 13D filer's trades explain only 25% of turnover, with the unexplained component averaging 240% of normal-time levels (p. 33).
- **13F.** Footnote 9, p. 11: funds holding > $100 million must file 13F quarterly, so such blocks "can be hidden over even longer horizons."
- **Section 13(d)(3) group rules and the 2023 SEC "tipping" guidance.** §5.3, pp. 32–33: being treated as a group means the aggregate block counts against 5%, invites competition, and can trigger the target barring further purchases. The SEC guidance is cited to pp. 130–139 of Release 33-11253 (footnote 37, p. 33) — **this is the same release that is our Feb-2024 acceleration anchor, but CCKV cite it only for the tipping/group discussion, never for the window change** (the window change is cited to the 2023 press release instead).
- **Section 16(b) "short swing rule"** and insider-trading limits as a reason blocks stay below 10% (footnote 12, p. 13).
- **European law:** "acting in concert" under the Transparency Directive and the Acquisitions Directive (footnote 35, p. 32).
- **Group compliance as a control-outcome channel, in prose *(added by verifier)*.** p. 32 (H2): complying with the disclosure rule "means that a group necessarily invites undesired competition before achieving a desired block size, thereby making block acquisition more costly," and "the target firm may bar the acquisition of more shares by the group members—the identities of which are revealed upon disclosure—which may preclude the success of any engagement." A disclosure→control-outcome channel is *named* here and never modelled.
- **Rule 14a-2(b)(2) and coordination arithmetic *(added by verifier)*.** Footnote 36, p. 32: Rule 14a-2(b)(2) permits shareholder communication on voting intentions at minimal cost so long as proxies are not solicited; Lewellen and Lewellen (2022) find that reaching 25% of shares needs only ~5 shareholders contacted, and 50% about 27 institutions.
- **The empirical pattern they claim matches (pp. 26–27) *(added by verifier)*.** Brav et al. (2021b), US activist hedge funds 1994–2018: around disclosure and trigger dates there is "substantially more abnormality for small-cap firms, followed by mid-cap, and lastly for the largest firms (even featuring negative abnormality in this latter case)" (p. 27). This is the single closest thing to an empirical anchor in the paper, and the sign pattern their ρ-story is reverse-engineered to fit.
- **Other numbers cited, not produced:** ~$10M total campaign cost (Gantchev 2013), $2.43M intervention costs (Albuquerque et al. 2022) (footnote 2, p. 2); Becht et al. (2017) 1,700+ events, a quarter with multiple activists (p. 25); hedge funds' average holding duration > 530 days (Brav et al. 2021b) (p. 35); mega-caps 30% of major activists' targets in 2024 (Barclays 2024 review) (p. 27).

## 5. Referee-facing strengths / weaknesses

**Strengths.**
- A genuinely new and *clean* theoretical result: with endogenous fundamentals **and** multiple players **and** sequential moves, the Kyle unpredictability property breaks — and it breaks with a **sign that is pinned to an observable-in-principle primitive** (ρ). That is a rare thing: a directional prediction from a linear-Gaussian model that is not a knife-edge.
- The mechanism is isolated properly. The value of manipulation (eq. 16, p. 18) is exactly the belief-sensitivity gap ∂Y₁ᶠ/∂Ψ₁ − ∂P₁/∂Ψ₁; everything else in the model is standard Kyle. A referee can see immediately what is doing the work.
- Extensive robustness on the *source* of private information (Prop. 1), on whether correlation is needed at all (Prop. 2 — it is not, with two-sided repeated trading), on a passive leader (Prop. 3), on a hostile follower (Prop. 4), on multiple followers (Prop. 7). The result is not fragile to the specific private-information story.
- The liquidity result (Thm 2(iii)) is counter-intuitive in a productive way: more liquidity does **not** kill manipulation, because the leader's marginal incentive to manipulate is her own terminal block, which is itself endogenous and grows as she trades more aggressively (p. 24). This is the kind of point that survives a seminar.
- Honest about existence limits: they state ρ̲ ∈ (−φ, 0), prove non-existence for sufficiently negative ρ (Prop. A.5, p. 46), and flag which uniqueness claims are numerical (fn 20, p. 17).

- *(added by verifier)* **They position themselves against Maug (1998) explicitly, p. 7.** "This notion of shares' disposal favoring voice relates to models where selling by liquidity (or 'noise') traders facilitates block formation and activism (e.g., Maug, 1998; Kahn and Winton, 1998)… Instead, in our model, it is an activist who strategically creates favorable market conditions for others." Their liquidity channel is deliberately *not* the Maug channel — noise supply is replaced by a strategic activist supplying camouflage. Worth citing when we place our κ against both.
- *(added by verifier)* **The one-line statement of their liquidity sign, p. 7:** "A key novelty of our model is that noisier signals (here, order flows) can lead to more manipulation, despite beliefs (here, prices) becoming less responsive. This is because the leader's marginal incentive to manipulate beliefs—captured by her terminal block—is endogenous." Shorter and sharper than Q9 for the same purpose.

**Weaknesses / open flanks.**
- **"Dynamics" in the title is generous.** The baseline is three periods with one trade each. The genuinely dynamic version (repeated trading by both) is Prop. 2, proved only for σ near 0 and only in the Internet Appendix; the fully dynamic multiplayer model is named as *future work* (p. 35).
- **Effort = terminal block exactly.** Wⁱ = XTⁱ is a mechanical consequence of quadratic effort cost plus linear value. Every governance statement in Thm 2 is therefore a restatement of a trading statement. The authors concede the dependence on the cost structure and point to Back et al. (2018) for what changes (p. 12, p. 35).
- **No proofs on file for six of eight propositions.** Props 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, 8 rest on an Internet Appendix we do not hold, and footnote 42 (p. 55) parks general-ρ payoff expressions in a Mathematica notebook.
- **Liquidity comparative statics are limits plus a picture.** Theorem 2(iii) is only σ → ∞ and σ → 0; everything in between is Figure 3 at φ = µ = 1. There is no global monotonicity theorem in σ.
- **The empirical section is a reinterpretation exercise, and they say so.** The chain from ρ to a testable proxy runs through market capitalisation via three separate papers (Hadlock–Schwartz-Ziv 2019 → Lewellen–Lewellen 2022 → Brav et al. 2021b), and the authors concede on p. 27 that "these studies do not perfectly fit our setup" — hedge funds and private equity are commingled, and the firms analysed "need not be targets of activist campaigns."
- **ρ is not measurable.** The paper's entire sign prediction turns on the covariance of two *unobserved private* initial blocks. The proposed proxies (market cap, presence of short positions) are several steps removed.
- **Leader/follower identity is exogenous.** §5 shows who *would like* to lead; nothing selects who does. The authors name this as the third open direction (p. 35).
- Small production defects a referee will notice: Figure 1's caption reverses left and right relative to the text describing it (text p. 19: "The left panel in Figure 1 illustrates typical coefficients"; caption p. 19: "Right panel: leader's strategy coefficients"); "Parameters values" (Fig. 1 caption, p. 19); "maningful" (footnote 4, p. 5); "endogeneizes" (p. 35).

## 6. What they do NOT do (scope boundary)

**(a) Disclosure is never modelled — there is no 13D threshold, no filing date, no horizon T, and no public signal.** This is the single most important boundary for us, and it is unambiguous. Searching the full text: "13D" appears **three** times, all in prose or footnotes (p. 11 fn 8, p. 25, p. 33) *(corrected by verifier: the card said four including p. 12; a per-page `pdftotext -layout` extract returns three, and printed p. 12 carries no "13D")*; "5%" appears only in institutional discussion; the strings "premium", "tender", "merger", "acquirer" appear **zero** times in the body. The model's only public objects are the two total order flows Ψ₁, Ψ₂ and the prices P₀, P₁, P₂. Individual trades and stakes are hidden throughout (footnote 16, p. 15). **No agent in the model ever learns that a blockholder holds a block.** The game ends when the fundamental is revealed after effort (Q1), not at a filing.

**(b) On "CCKV stop at the crossing" — our note is right in substance and needs one correction in wording.** The model does not *stop at* the crossing; the crossing is **outside the model altogether**. The three periods are *interpreted* as sitting inside the pre-disclosure window: Q4, p. 11, "Our model is then best interpreted as taking place in such a pre-disclosure window when the activists have superior information and are gearing up to quickly finalize their positions and attack." The window's length is used only as an argument for why one round of trading per activist suffices (Q3, p. 10: trades "effectively remain hidden for some time"). **The choice and timing of crossing are not modelled** — the blockholder never chooses whether or when to cross 5%, never faces a deadline, and the disclosure rule never enters any payoff, constraint, information set or comparative static. Confirmed.

**(c) No takeover, bidder, premium, or control outcome.** These appear only in the related-literature paragraph, where the authors explicitly hand that territory to others (Q10, p. 7): Burkart & Lee (2022) for activists who "broker takeovers", Corum & Levit (2019) for proxy contests that "trigger ownership transfers", Burkart et al. (2000) for an incumbent selling a majority to a bidder. Their own model has no bidder, no tender offer, no premium, no vote, no control transfer. "Campaign success" appears only as the linearisation remark on p. 12 ("the probability of success increases in total effort") — success is never an outcome variable.

**(d) No data.** Section 4.2 is a reading of *other* people's estimates. They propose tests they do not run: "The empirical challenge is then to identify characteristics of target firms with enough underlying variation in block interdependence to test the above hypothesis" (p. 26).

**(e) Named future work (p. 35, verbatim in Q12 and here):** (i) "developing a multiplayer, fully dynamic, repeated trade version of Proposition 2… to shed light on time-horizon effects: on the one hand, the usual insider 'splitting trades' logic would say that trades should be small away from the end-game; but at the same time, that is when beliefs are most responsive, which would favor the dampening of trades"; (ii) "examining cost structures beyond the quadratic case… building on the innovative work by Back et al. (2018) could provide a more informed view regarding the interplay between market liquidity and the distortion of trades we have uncovered"; (iii) endogenising the emergence of leaders and followers, with a partial Internet-Appendix treatment that endogenises initial positions but keeps identities exogenous.

## 7. Implications for our position

**Which cell they occupy.**
- **OBJECT:** the leader's *expected trade* E[θᴸ | F₀] — equivalently, the predictability of informed order flow — and, mechanically downstream of it, ex ante firm value E[Wᴸ+Wᶠ] = 2µ + E[θᴸ] and the average price E[P₁] = E[P₂]. Cast in empirical language, the object is **abnormal buy-and-hold return around a disclosure event** (p. 26). It is **not** a takeover premium, **not** bidder entry, **not** campaign success.
- **MARGIN of the disclosure rule: none.** The rule is background prose in §2.2 and a compliance cost in §5.3. Neither the threshold margin nor the window margin is a parameter, a choice, or a comparative static anywhere in the paper.
- **IDENTIFICATION: theory only,** with a reinterpretation section and proposed proxies (market capitalisation, presence of short positions) that they concede are imperfect (p. 27).

**Where our claim collides with them — be precise about this.** Our claim is "the market learns about control events from order flow under a stake-triggered disclosure rule." That sentence has two halves and they own exactly one of them.

- **Collision on the first half.** "The market learns about an intervention from order flow" is *precisely* their pricing rule P_{t} = E[Wᴸ+Wᶠ | F_t] with endogenous W. They are not merely adjacent here; this is their core primitive, and they have the sharper result on it (Theorem 1: order flow is no longer conditionally mean-zero). **We cannot claim order-flow inference about an intervention as novel.** If our contribution is stated at that level of generality, a referee will hand us this paper and we lose. Our claim of novelty has to sit on the *partition*, not on the inference.
- **No collision on the partition — this is our whitespace, and it is wide.** CCKV have a single, permanently pooled information state. There is no flagged state, no event at which a stake becomes public, no discrete change in the market's information set. Our identity — the disclosure rule *as* the market's partition, splitting the world into flagged and pooled — has no counterpart in their model, and they never approach it. Note that they do the opposite of us on purpose: they interpret their whole model as living *inside* the pooled state (Q4).
- **No collision on control outcomes.** Zero occurrences of premium/tender/merger/acquirer. They explicitly cede the control-market leg to Burkart–Lee and Corum–Levit (Q10). Our premium wedge and bidder-entry margin are untouched.
- **No collision on the disclosure-rule margins.** Neither threshold nor window is a lever in their model. Our disclosure-attenuation claim (draft_v2's T2) cannot be pre-empted by them.
- **A live hazard on liquidity.** Both papers make liquidity the driving variable, and their sign is *surprising*: more noise trading → **more** manipulation, not less, because the leader's marginal incentive is her endogenous terminal block (Thm 2(iii.1), Q9). Two consequences. (i) Our κ comparative static must not simply assert that liquidity dilutes strategic distortion — a referee who knows CCKV will ask why the endogenous-block force does not run the other way in our model. (ii) Their Figure 3 shows |E[θᴸ]| **monotone increasing** in σ; our "hump" (draft_v2's R1, non-monotone minority gains in liquidity) therefore cannot be sourced from anything resembling their force. If we keep the hump, its driver must be visibly ours — most naturally the partition itself, since a threshold generates a non-monotonicity that a smooth Gaussian model cannot.
- **A second hazard: the single-blockholder assumption.** Theorem 1 says that as soon as a *second* informed activist exists whose position is correlated with the first, average order flow stops being zero. Any result of ours that reads inference off an order flow assumed conditionally mean-zero is exposed. We should either (a) keep one blockholder and say plainly that we abstract from leader-follower interaction, citing CCKV for what it would add, or (b) show our partition result survives their sign. (a) is the deliverable choice.

**Where they hand us the opening.** §2.2 is, read carefully, a *description of our paper written as an excuse*. They justify a three-period model by asserting that the pre-disclosure window is short, that trades cluster on the trigger date, and that the 10-day (now 5-business-day) delay is what makes trades "effectively remain hidden for some time" (Q3, p. 10). Every one of those is an institutional fact about the **window margin** — used as a modelling convenience and never modelled. **We model what they assume.** That is the cleanest one-line statement of our position against them, and it is defensible from their own text.

Reinforcing this: their named open direction (i) is explicitly about **time-horizon effects** — endgame versus splitting-trades — which is exactly what a filing deadline is (Q12 and §6(e)). If our window margin is implemented as a deadline at which the pooled state ends, we are doing a version of the problem they flagged as open, with the institutional content they left out.

**Practical drafting instructions this card implies.**
1. Never write "we show the market infers activism from order flow" without an immediate CCKV cite and a "unlike CCKV, our market's information set changes discretely at the threshold."
2. Cite CCKV p. 11 footnote 8 as independent confirmation that the 10 → 5 business-day change is the salient recent variation — a competitor treating it as background is evidence that the margin is unclaimed.
3. Use Q1 + Q4 together as the scope-boundary citation: their game ends at value revelation, and the trading rounds are interpreted as pre-disclosure. Neither the crossing nor the filing is an event in their model.
4. Do not claim novelty for multi-activist or free-riding results — Theorem 2 owns that. Our blockholder should stay single, by declared assumption.
5. If we ever want an activism-cost number for calibration, they cite Gantchev (2013) ~$10M per campaign and Albuquerque et al. (2022) $2.43M intervention costs (p. 2, footnote 2) — both already in our competitor set.
