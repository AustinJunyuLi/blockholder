Lesson 0003

Pricing a mixed order

The market turns a belief into a price, and the price turns out to sit inside its own definition.

Lesson 2 ended with a belief. The market saw a total order of +1 and put the chance of engagement at 0.824. A belief is not yet a price. This lesson turns beliefs into prices, shows why that step is a loop rather than a sum, and then explains why this paper calls κ liquidity.

The pricing rule

Market makers compete. If one quoted a price below what a share is worth on public information, another would step in and buy. Competition drives expected trading profit to zero, so the quoted price equals the expected payoff given everything the public knows. Write the terminal shareholder payoff as Y and the public information set as ℐ. Then

P(ℐ) = E[Y | ℐ]

This is the whole pricing rule. Everything hard about it lives in Y.

What is being priced

Two things can happen to a Harbor Tools share by the decision day. A bidder shows up, or no bidder shows up.

The two branches

No bidder. Shareholders keep the firm. Each share is worth its standalone value v, plus an improvement ΔV if Northstar engaged.

A bidder. Shareholders are bought out. They receive the prevailing price P, plus a takeover premium on top: m0 without engagement, and m0 + Δm with it.

Let a be one when Northstar engaged and zero otherwise, and let ℬ be one when the bidder enters. The model writes the payoff as

Y = (1 − ℬ)(v + aΔV) + ℬ(P + m0 + aΔm)

Read the takeover branch again. It contains P. A bidder does not pay the standalone value plus a premium; it has to beat the going market price, and the premium sits on top of that. So the price we are trying to compute appears inside the payoff we are averaging. This is not a modeling trick. It is what a premium means.

When does the bidder show up

The bidder is worth more to itself than to the market because it can run the firm differently. Its private synergy draw is S̄ + ξ, with mean synergy S̄ and a normal shock ξ. Bidding costs K. The bidder enters when what it gets beats what it must pay.

S̄ + ξ  ≥  P + K + m̄,    where m̄ = m0 + πΔm

Here π is the market's engagement posterior from lesson 2, and m̄ is the premium the bidder expects to owe. Because ξ is normal with standard deviation σξ, the entry probability is

p(P) = 1 − Φ ((P + K + m̄ − S̄) / σξ)

The one property to carry forward is the direction. A higher price makes the bidder's bill larger, so p falls as P rises. Entry probability slopes down in price.

Close the loop

Now average the two branches. Write ȳ = E[v | ℐ] + πΔV for the expected no-takeover value, and keep m̄ for the expected premium. Then

P = (1 − p)ȳ + p(P + m̄)

Collect the P terms. Subtract pP from both sides, factor, and divide by 1 − p:

P = ȳ + m̄ · p / (1 − p)

The premium is multiplied by the odds of entry, not by the probability of entry. That is the whole content of the loop. The takeover branch pays the price rather than the standalone value, so every dollar of premium gets levered up by the chance that the branch happens at all.

Work it once with numbers

Take the market's expected standalone value to be 100. Engagement adds 4 without a takeover, so ΔV = 4. The premium is 6 without engagement and 16 with it, so m0 = 6 and Δm = 10. Put the engagement posterior at the prior, π = 0.5. Then

The belief summaries

ȳ = 100 + 0.5 × 4 = 102

m̄ = 6 + 0.5 × 10 = 11

Suppose for a moment the bidder entered with probability 0.5 regardless of price. The odds of entry are 0.5 / 0.5 = 1, so P = 102 + 11 × 1 = 113. Check it by hand against the branches. Without a bidder a share is worth 102. With a bidder it is worth 113 + 11 = 124. Average the two at even odds and you get 113 back. The price reproduces itself.

Compare that with the answer most people write first. Averaging 102 and 102 + 11 gives 107.5. That number treats the bidder as paying the standalone value plus a premium. The missing 5.5 is the part of the premium that gets paid on the price itself.

Now let entry respond to price, with K = 5, S̄ = 129 and σξ = 20. Entry probability becomes 1 − Φ((P − 113) / 20). Tabulate the map against the price you fed it:

One crossing

At P = 100 the map returns 133.66, which is above 100.

At P = 113 the map returns 113.00, which is exactly 113.

At P = 120 the map returns 108.27, which is below 120.

The equilibrium price is 113, with entry probability one half. That the calibration lands on a round number is arranged, not general.

Why the crossing is unique

Look at what the two sides of P = ȳ + m̄ p(P) / (1 − p(P)) do as you raise P. The left side rises with slope one. On the right, p falls, so the odds fall, and if m̄ is not negative the whole right side falls too. A falling curve meets a rising line at most once, and the two ends guarantee it happens at least once.

The condition that keeps m̄ from going negative is the card's restriction m0 ≥ 0. Since Δm > 0 and π lies in [0, 1], m0 ≥ 0 forces m̄ ≥ 0 at every posterior the model can produce. Drop it and the right side can slope upward, where a crossing may be missing or repeated. Lesson 5 does that argument properly. For now, hold the picture: one curve down, one line up, one crossing.

The pricing lab

This lab runs lesson 2 and lesson 3 back to back. Pick the observed order, set κ, and it computes the posterior, then solves the price.

The lab holds the market's expected standalone value fixed at 100 while the engagement posterior moves. In the full model order flow updates beliefs about value as well, so this lab isolates the engagement channel on purpose. Noise size and Northstar's buy are normalised to one, as in lesson 2.

Price impact runs through the belief

Nothing in this model says the price moves by some fixed amount per unit of order flow. An order changes the price only by changing what the market believes. Raise π and three things happen at once.

  • The no-takeover branch is worth more, because engagement adds ΔV. This pushes the price up.
  • The takeover branch is worth more, because the premium rises by Δm times the odds of entry. This also pushes the price up.
  • The bidder's bill rises by the same Δm, so it enters less often. This pushes the price down.

The third channel is easy to forget and it is real. At the calibration above, moving π from 0.5 upward, the first channel contributes 4.00, the second 10.00, and the third −8.78. The net is positive here and gets damped by a further factor from the price feeding back into entry. That is a fact about these numbers, not a sign the model proves in general.

The same order, two liquidity levels

With no order seen, the price is 113.0000 and entry has chance 0.5000.

At κ = 0.30 a +1 order lifts π to 0.8235 and the price to 113.6932. Impact +0.6932.

At κ = 0.60 the same +1 order lifts π only to 0.5714 and the price to 113.1871. Impact +0.1871.

Why the paper calls κ liquidity

Doubling κ cut the price impact of the same order to about a quarter. Lesson 2 explains the mechanism: with more noise trading around, a buy order is easier to explain without Northstar, so the posterior moves less. Lesson 3 adds that the price moves only when the posterior moves. Put them together and higher κ means less price movement per unit of order flow.

That is exactly what a trader means by a liquid market. You can push size through it without moving the quote. So the paper defines liquidity as noise-trading intensity and gives it the symbol κ. It does not mean depth, volume, or turnover, and using those words for κ is a vocabulary error the project treats as a real one. On the empirical side κ is measured by an Amihud-type illiquidity ratio inverted, and Amihud's ratio is price move per unit of volume, which is the same quantity this lesson just computed.

Two limits to state now, before they get assumed away. This is not Kyle's linear rule: no λ multiplies order flow here, and the impact of an order is whatever Bayes plus the fixed point deliver, which is generally not linear. And κ enters through the posterior, so it does its work on the pooled cell. On the flagged cell the filing has already settled engagement, π = 1, and there is no inference left for noise to obscure.

pooledπ inferred from order flowκ damps the price impact
flaggedπ = 1 by the filingsame fixed point, no engagement inference

Where the loop actually lives

The self-referential fixed point sits at the day the bidder decides, because that is where the takeover branch is live. At an earlier trading day the price is an average of control-day values that have already been solved, so nothing refers to itself. The model also fixes the price before any trading as the plain unconditional expectation of Y, which is the 113.0000 the lab uses as its no-order baseline. Lesson 4 lays out the timeline that makes this precise.

Check yourself

1. On the takeover branch, shareholders receive

The bidder must beat the going market price, and the premium sits on top. That is why P appears inside Y.

2. In the solved price, the premium is multiplied by

Solving P = (1−p)ȳ + p(P + m̄) divides by 1 − p, which turns the probability into odds.

3. Raising κ makes a given order's price impact

More noise makes the order easier to explain without Northstar. The posterior moves less, so the price moves less.

4. In this paper, liquidity means

κ is noise-trading intensity. Depth, volume, and turnover are different objects, and the project treats the substitution as an error.
