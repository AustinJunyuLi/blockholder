Part I. The world the model lives in

Running example

Imagine Northstar Fund believes Harbor Tools is badly run and could be worth more under a new strategy or owner. Northstar quietly buys shares, considers pressing the board, and knows that a public filing may reveal both its stake and its plans. The market sees the trades but not the motive. A possible bidder is watching too. We will reuse this case so the equations put names on mechanisms the course will make explicit.

1. Firms, shares, and the agency problem

Start with what a share is. A share is a slice of two different things: a claim on the firm's cash, and a vote in its decisions. In a big public company these two things belong to people who never meet. A pension fund in Toronto owns 0.4 percent of the firm. A family in Osaka owns 200 shares. Nobody in that crowd runs the company. Professional managers do.

This division of labour creates a basic problem in corporate finance. Shareholders want the firm to be worth as much as possible. Managers also care about pay, safety, prestige, and control. Economists call this the agency problem. Jensen and Meckling's 1976 account formalises how conflicts between owners and managers can reduce firm value.

You might think the shareholders could just fire bad managers. In principle they can, by voting out the board. In practice a pension fund holding 0.4 percent may not pay for a fight to fix a company it barely owns. If the fight succeeds, 99.6 percent of the benefit goes to others. This is a free-rider problem: monitoring benefits all shareholders, so each small holder would rather someone else pay for it.

The market's own answer to undersupplied monitoring is concentration. Someone who owns a large block, say 5 or 8 percent, has real money on the line. A 5 percent stake in a company worth two billion is a hundred million dollar position. That exposure can make a fight worthwhile. That someone is a blockholder, and this whole project is about one such person.

2. Blockholders: exit and voice

Albert Hirschman wrote a small book in 1970 called Exit, Voice, and Loyalty, about organisations in decline. Members can leave, or they can stay and press for change. Finance uses the same vocabulary because a shareholder can sell or remain and intervene.

Voice means staying and pushing. Write letters, meet management, demand a strategy change, take a board seat, campaign publicly for a sale of the company. Voice costs effort and money, and it can improve the firm for every shareholder, which is the free-rider problem again: the blockholder pays the whole cost and keeps only her share of the gain.

Exit means selling. If the blockholder concludes the firm is hopeless, she can sell the block. Exit is not inert. Informed selling can move the price down, and a falling price can signal bad news to the board, other investors, and potential acquirers. Edmans formalised this channel in 2009. Exit works through the price.

Two further classic arguments set the tension this project inherits. Maug, in 1998, argued that liquidity, the ease of trading without moving the price, helps voice because a liquid stock lets a blockholder build a position before engaging. Coffee in 1991 and Bhide in 1993 stressed the opposite force. Easy exit can weaken the incentive to do costly monitoring. Whether liquidity strengthens or weakens governance is model-specific.

One vocabulary note that this project takes seriously. The person is called a blockholder until the moment she chooses voice. Only then is she an activist. Real reporting routes depend on legal eligibility and purpose; the model compresses that complexity into an engagement choice the market cannot observe before the flag.

3. What activists actually do

Making voice concrete. A hedge fund identifies a company it believes is undervalued because of mismanagement. It buys a block, say 6 percent. It then does one or more of the following, roughly in order of escalation.

Private engagement first. Letters and meetings with management and the board can remain out of public view. A campaign may start and end here.

Public campaigns next. A public letter, investor presentation, or demand list can call for a division sale, a cash return, a new chief executive, or the removal of a takeover defence.

Board seats and proxy fights. The activist nominates directors and campaigns for shareholder votes. A full proxy fight needs lawyers, proxy solicitors, communications advisers, and months of management attention. That cost is one reason a meaningful block is needed to justify it.

Pushing for a sale. Sometimes the demand is that the whole company be sold. Brav, Jiang, Partnoy, and Thomas documented activist objectives, tactics, and outcomes, including sale campaigns. Greenwood and Schor found in 2009 that takeovers account for a large share of activist returns. Voice can end in a control transaction, and dispersed shareholders receive the transaction premium.

All of this costs money: advisors, lawyers, campaigns, and the blockholder's own time. The model calls this the engagement cost, and voice only happens when the expected payoff covers it.

4. Takeovers, tender offers, and premia

A takeover is a change of control. One route is a tender offer: an acquirer publicly offers a price per share and shareholders decide individually whether to tender. The gap between the offer price and a reference market price is the takeover premium. The bidder may expect synergies, value created by combining the firms or running the target better, but it usually must share some expected value to persuade target shareholders to sell.

Why must the acquirer share? The free-rider problem appears again. Suppose the offer succeeds and the firm becomes more valuable. A small shareholder may refuse a low offer, hope everyone else tenders, and keep a share in the improved company. Grossman and Hart showed how this can stop an acquirer from capturing the gain it creates. The premium is how target shareholders receive part of the deal value. In this paper it is a control-outcome measure for target shareholders, not a measure of total social welfare.

Where does Northstar fit? Engagement may improve Harbor Tools even if no bid arrives, and it may make the company more valuable in a sale. The current model records the takeover channel with two numbers: m0, the premium without engagement, and the larger m1, the premium with engagement. Their difference, Δm = m1 − m0, is the premium wedge. The model takes this wedge as an input. It does not rebuild the tender game that might generate it.

One more piece: the bidder does not always come. In the model a potential acquirer draws a private synergy value and pays a fixed cost to bid. A higher engagement premium helps target shareholders if a bid happens, but it is also a higher price for the bidder to pay, so it can reduce bidder entry. That tension matters.

  • Bid probability asks whether any offer arrives.
  • Premium conditional on a bid asks how much target shareholders receive above the reference price if it arrives.
  • Expected engagement-related premium combines the engagement wedge with the market's belief that engagement occurred and the chance a bidder enters. This is the paper's main outcome.

5. How trading moves prices

Now the market itself. A share trades on an exchange. Buyers and sellers submit orders. Some orders sit in the limit order book waiting to be hit, and firms called market makers stand ready to trade at quoted prices, earning the spread between their buy and sell quotes. The prices you see are set by people who will lose money if they trade with someone better informed than they are.

That risk drives the quote. Suppose a market maker quotes 20.00 and someone buys a million shares. Maybe the buyer is an index fund rebalancing. Maybe it is someone who knows the company is about to be acquired. The market maker cannot tell. An order that may be informed therefore moves the quote up. Prices respond to order flow because flow may carry information. In Kyle's 1985 model, one informed trader hides her orders inside the flow of noise traders, people trading for their own reasons, and a competitive market maker prices the mix.

Noise is what makes hiding possible. If every share traded were information-driven, any purchase would reveal conviction. But pension funds rebalance, index funds track, and households trade for reasons unrelated to Harbor Tools. This flow is noise in the statistical sense. It can camouflage an informed order, though the model does not assume every aggregate price or premium response moves monotonically with noise. The paper's knob is liquidity, written κ between 0 and 1: the probability weight on nonzero noise orders.

Be careful with the word, because finance uses it loosely. Here liquidity is never depth, never volume, never turnover. It is one specific thing: the probability weight on noise trades. When the paper's empirics need a measurable cousin, it uses an Amihud illiquidity measure, inverted. The vocabulary is fixed in the project's CONTEXT.md for exactly this reason, and the glossary in this course follows it.

Prices also feed real decisions. If prices aggregate information, and decisions depend on prices, then trading affects outcomes. Bond, Edmans, and Goldstein surveyed this feedback in 2012. Edmans, Goldstein, and Jiang showed that takeovers respond to price signals. In this model the blockholder's trades move the price, which changes the bidder's entry decision.

6. The law: Schedule 13D, Schedule 13G, and the two margins

Everything above is strategic behaviour. Now the constraint that shapes it. The Williams Act of 1968 created the US beneficial-ownership disclosure system. It did not ban quiet stake-building. It made large positions reportable.

Start with beneficial ownership. It is the stake attributed under SEC rules and guidance, which can include indirect holdings, voting or investment power, groups, and some covered instruments. It is not always the number of shares registered in one account. Once beneficial ownership exceeds 5 percent of a covered class, the holder enters the 13D/13G reporting system unless an exception applies.

Schedule 13D is the fuller report generally used by control-oriented holders. It identifies the holder, stake, funding, transactions, arrangements, and, in Item 4, purpose and plans. Since 5 February 2024, an initial 13D is due no later than five business days after the triggering acquisition. Before that date the maximum was ten calendar days. A real filer can report earlier; the model assumes filing at the deadline. The SEC's final release states the amended deadlines and effective date.

The paper extracts two policy controls. The threshold margin is which stake paths become reportable. Lower the model threshold τ and more histories can be flagged. The window margin is the model's exact delay from crossing to filing. Shorten T and less trading fits before the flag. Real law instead sets an outer deadline and permits earlier filing, so the model is an abstraction rather than a literal calendar calculator.

Schedule 13G is a shorter alternative, but it is not simply the passive version of the same form. Three broad routes exist: exempt investors, qualified institutional investors, and qualifying passive investors below 20 percent who certify no control purpose. Eligibility and purpose both matter, and deadlines vary by filer class. A 6 percent holding can therefore appear on either schedule, but not because the investor may choose any label it likes. The SEC's beneficial-ownership interpretations explain the reporting routes. This paper focuses on the purpose-and-plans information in 13D's Item 4.

Amendments matter too. Once on 13D, a material change, with any acquisition or disposal of a full percentage point deemed material, triggers a 13D/A within two business days. A real activist's public history is a sequence of filings, not one.

Around the world, the same broad idea uses different rules. The UK's DTR 5 is a holdings ladder starting at 3 percent and does not have the US Item 4 purpose narrative. The EU has another ladder. The UK's separate 30 percent mandatory-offer rule is a control trigger, not a disclosure threshold. The current model is a stylised version of one first, purpose-revealing flag. It does not model ladders, 13G eligibility, early filing, or amendment sequences.

7. The standoff

Together, the three systems create the model's tradeoff.

From microstructure, section 5: Northstar wants to trade before its information is fully reflected in the price, and noise can hide its motive. From corporate finance, sections 1 to 4: engagement can raise Harbor Tools' value and the premium paid if a bid arrives, but a costlier deal can deter the bidder. From law, section 6: a triggering acquisition above the threshold starts a reporting clock, and a 13D makes the stake and plans public.

The legal deadline limits camouflage. Northstar can buy fast and cross early, keep its stake below the threshold while engaging privately, or sell. Each choice changes what the market sees and how much stock Northstar holds. The equilibrium answer depends on κ, τ, T, m0, and m1.

The paper asks one question about that world: when disclosure tightens, does the expected engagement-related premium become less sensitive to liquidity? The SEC shortened the real window in 2024, while other systems use different thresholds. The model separates those two margins instead of treating stricter disclosure as one undifferentiated policy.

Part II. The research idea

8. The rule is the partition

The partition is the paper's central object. Fix the day H on which the bidder decides. Take any complete public history up to that day, including order flow and prices. The disclosure rule sorts that history into exactly one of two cells.

Flagged. The filing landed by day H. In the model it truthfully reports engagement and the filed stake, so the engagement posterior is one. L2 goes further only under its identifying assumptions: the full flagged tuple must identify the informed component, with fixed policies and no feedback from realised flow to the plan.

Pooled. No filing by day H. The market sees order flow and updates its belief about whether an engager hides inside it. Some pooled histories remain ambiguous; others can reveal the type. Pooled means no filing, not guaranteed uncertainty.

The cells are exclusive, exhaustive, and keyed by the rule. The model's threshold and filing clock therefore determine its information split. In the project's current competitor map, none of the mapped papers uses the filing window as the primitive that draws this two-cell line. That is a bounded finding from the current source map, not a claim about every paper ever written.

Only voice raises the flag in this model. Exit and Hold never push the stake to the threshold, so they live their whole lives in the pool. This is a modelling restriction, not a claim that every real 13D cleanly proves activism. Real Item 4 language is narrative and real markets can learn before a filing through rumours or surveillance.

9. The clock and the one-way door

For a Voice plan, the model flag lands if and only if the stake, measured T model days before the decision day, had already reached τ. This clock equivalence is the starting point for the comparative statics. The condition is about a stake level on a model date. The filing follows from it.

The model disclosure clock

One voice plan over ten model business-day rounds. Tighten the threshold or the window and watch the information cell change.

Stake path is schematic, shape matched to the calibration's median voice terminal stake of 9.08 percent. The cell logic is exact: flagged iff B at day H−T has reached τ, which equals the filing landing by H.

The clock equivalence gives two unconditional classification facts. At fixed policies, lowering the threshold or shortening the window can move a history from pooled to flagged, never the reverse. It is a one-way door. D1 also leaves no third cell. L4 adds a threshold result only: at a common window, lowering the threshold raises flagged mass and lowers the pooled engagement share when the tightened pool remains nonempty. The window margin has no corresponding composition sign.

10. Liquidity lives in the pool

The partition determines where κ can affect inference.

In the flagged cell the model filing settles whether engagement is present. Under L2's identifying assumptions, residual order flow adds no information about engagement. The flagged posterior, price, entry probability, and premium average therefore do not move with κ. The assumption matters: the flagged tuple must identify the informed component.

In the pooled cell the market infers from order flow, as in section 5. More noise can make the same purchase less revealing. Under the theorem's fixed-policy assumptions, all of κ's effect on the expected premium is carried through this cell.

The figure below isolates the inference step. On one trading day a hidden buyer either places one standard buy or does not, with a fair prior on each. Noise is ternary, as in the model: zero with probability 1 − κ, and a standard buy or sell with probability κ/2 each. The market sees only the total order flow.

Camouflage: the same order flow, different noise

Bars show the probability of each total order flow when no engager is present and when one is. Pick an observation, then raise κ.

Exact Bayes arithmetic for this one-step teaching case: prior one half, one standard hidden buy, and the model's ternary noise. The paper's first round runs over many days with plan-dependent coarsened orders; a filing can end the pooled round and start the flagged round.

Notice what happens to observation +1. With little noise, it is probably Northstar's hidden buy plus zero noise. With much noise, it is more likely a noise buy with no engager. The posterior falls as κ rises. This widget teaches the Bayesian channel, not a global monotonicity claim for the full model.

11. Outcome and factorisation

Define the paper's outcome. Let π be the market's probability that engagement is present and p the probability a bidder enters. Then

Δact = Δm · E[π p]

It is the engagement premium wedge, weighted by belief and bidder entry. It is not the full takeover premium, the filing-day stock return, or welfare. Split this outcome over the legal partition and the law of total expectation gives

Δact = Ω · MF + (1 − Ω) · MP

where Ω is the probability the flag lands by day H, MF is the average premium inside the flagged cell and MP the average inside the pooled cell. This split is ledger result L1. At Ω = 1 the pooled average is undefined, and at Ω = 0 the flagged average is undefined; neither null-cell average is imputed.

Now ask how Δact responds to κ. Under T1's fixed-policy and identifying assumptions, the flagged term does not move with κ, and the cell weight Ω does not either. Only the pooled term survives:

S = (1 − Ω) · SP

with S the total κ-sensitivity and SP the pooled cell's own. The equation says something you can say at a whiteboard: whatever liquidity does to premia, it does through the pooled cell, so its force is scaled by the pooled cell's share.

Two things change when a rule tightens. The pooled share 1 − Ω shrinks, which reduces total sensitivity if the pool's own sensitivity stays fixed. This is the weight effect. The departing histories also change the pool's mix, so SP can move. This is the composition effect. Composition can reinforce or overturn the weight effect.

12. Two margins, two answers

Tighten the threshold, meaning lower τ. Under T1's full fixed-policy hypotheses, sensitivity falls without an extra dominance test: both the weight ratio and composition ratio lie in [0,1]. This is often shortened to "threshold attenuation is unconditional," but that phrase means unconditional on an extra sign test, not free of assumptions. One of those assumptions, A(τ), fails at every non-degenerate node in the implemented calibration, so T1(B) does not sign that calibrated pool.

Tighten the window, meaning lower T. T1 part C says sensitivity falls if and only if WT · CT ≤ 1. The weight half WT ≤ 1 is proved from one-way classification. The composition half CT is unsigned. A shorter window does not reclassify the most engaged histories first. It reclassifies whoever crossed early, and early crossing need not be ordered by engagement intensity. The remaining pool can therefore respond to κ more sharply than before. At the checked calibration WT · CT stayed below one at every node. The theorem leaves the general window sign open.

Attenuation arithmetic

First, the factorisation. Then, the theorem's margin arithmetic. Threshold mode shows its conditional C ≤ 1 restriction; window mode leaves C unsigned.

The baseline pooled weight is 0.8616; SP is set to 0.0739 so the displayed identity gives S ≈ 0.0637. This is arithmetic, not an independent calibration result. The threshold clamp belongs to T1's hypothesis stack, which the implemented calibration does not satisfy.

The predecessor draft bundled stricter disclosure into one claim. Draft v3 separates the margins. The threshold theorem gives attenuation under its hypothesis stack without an extra sign condition. The window theorem gives an if-and-only-if test because the remaining pool may become more sensitive. At the checked nodes, the window product is below one. Those nodes do not establish a general sign.

3. Tightening the window moves histories

A shorter window means the filing lands sooner after crossing, so more histories are revealed before the bidder decides. The door swings one way.

4. The window margin's attenuation condition is an iff because

WT ≤ 1 is proved from the clock equivalence. CT is unsigned, because early crossers need not be the most engaged histories, so the product can in principle exceed one.

13. Meeting the data

The empirical side is anchored on 5 February 2024, when the initial 13D deadline moved from ten calendar days to five business days. This is a dated change to the window margin. The paper asks one question of the data, registered as E1 before the run: did the realised trigger-to-filing delay fall after the deadline moved?

What has run. E1 enumerates the near-census of initial Schedule 13D filings from 2023Q2 to Q3 and from 2024Q3 to Q4: 616 filings before the change and 521 after, 461 and 435 eligible campaigns, 450 and 432 resolved. The committed result record reports that the median trigger-to-filing delay fell from six business days to five and that the share filed within five business days rose from 38.9 to 67.1 percent, a difference of 28.2 percentage points with a 95 percent cluster-bootstrap interval of 21.8 to 34.8. The empirical distribution shows the whole mass moving toward the deadline. Those are descriptive statistics with no control group. No causal effect is claimed.

What guards it. Three gates bind, and all three passed. The worst-case bound that assigns every unresolved campaign to both extremes still clears zero, at 26.3 to 29.4 points. The unresolved share differs by 1.7 percentage points between the two periods, against a registered cap of ten. A blind hand audit of 60 filings found no material parser error, against a threshold of three. The specification was committed before the run, and a fresh-clone rerun reproduces the result record.

What it does not do. The comparison identifies no effect on liquidity, returns, activism, bidder entry, or takeover premia, and it tests neither L2 nor T1. It establishes that the statutory clock moved realised filing timing, and nothing more.

14. What the paper refuses to claim

The paper states its exclusions alongside its theorems. Use this list to keep the result labels narrow.

  • No global window-margin attenuation sign. The window is an iff, and at the checked nodes the product stayed below one.
  • No κ-invariance claim for the filing-day jump J. Only the flagged cell averages are invariant, and J is a price move, not a cell average.
  • No equilibrium uniqueness, not even as a numerical regularity. Existence is claimed, uniqueness is not.
  • No welfare analysis and no optimal rule design. The planner apparatus of the old draft was dropped.
  • No claim that the predecessor draft's hump result survives. The current paper drops it.
  • No endogenous early filing, no partially revealing flagged trading, no continuous time.
  • No empirical value claimed for ωa, the disclosed share of engagements. The calibration's 61 percent is a property of the calibration.

16. Prices are averages, and one loop

The pricing rule is one line: the price at any public information set is the expected shareholder payoff given that information, P = E[Y | I]. Competitive market makers zero each other out, and this is what competition delivers. It is the Kyle logic of section 5 compressed into an expectation.

The payoff Y has two branches. If no bidder comes, a share is worth the firm's value v, plus the engagement improvement ΔV if engagement is on. If a bidder comes, a share is worth the price plus the premium m0 or m1 depending on engagement. Holding synergy fixed, bidder entry falls when the target price, entry cost, or expected premium payment rises.

The price depends on the entry probability because entry determines which branch of Y pays. Entry probability depends on the price because the bidder's payoff uses the target price. The model solves this loop as a fixed point. With m0 ≥ 0, the scalar fixed-point residual is strictly decreasing in P, which pins down one inner price. The uniqueness argument requires that sign restriction.

Prices at earlier dates are tower expectations of the solved control-node values, so the only genuine self-reference sits at the decision date. And the run-up and jump of section 13 are defined off this price path with an exact identity, no approximation.

17. Plans, cutoffs, and the equilibrium

The blockholder's strategy is not a single buy order. It is a complete contingent plan: a full schedule of what to hold on every business day as a function of her signal, plus the engagement decision. The model gives her a finite ordered menu of plans, least to most aggressive. At the calibration the menu is Exit, Hold, and Voice. The old draft's four-action structure, including Quiet Voice below the threshold, is a special case of this plan menu.

Ordering the menu by aggression makes equilibrium tractable. Under the single-crossing assumption A3, adjacent plans' payoff differences cross zero at most once in the signal. Optimal play is then a cutoff rule: low signals choose less aggressive plans and high signals choose more aggressive plans, with cutoffs k1 ≤ k2 ≤ ... between regions. Weak inequalities let a plan's signal region collapse, including the Hold region.

The cutoff rule

Sixty signal types on one axis. Drag the cutoffs. k1 cannot pass k2; set them equal to collapse Hold.

Defaults are the calibration's baseline equilibrium cutoffs, k = (1.2406, 1.5310). The outer residual there is 2.6 × 10−11 on the diagnostic scale and exactly zero on the binding payoff scale.

An equilibrium is then: cutoffs mapping signals to plans, sequential optimality within pooled and flagged rounds, Bayes-consistent beliefs on the path of play, competitive prices at their fixed points, the bidder's entry rule, and specified off-path beliefs. Under P1's long hypothesis list, a Brouwer fixed-point argument produces at least one equilibrium. Uniqueness is not claimed. A7-J is a joint tuple-identification condition used to pin flagged beliefs, while A6 requires a continuous, single-valued best-response map. The project measured A6 and the single-crossing condition failing at the implemented calibration. The proposition is a proved implication; its applicability there is open.

19. How this project knows what it knows

The last thing to understand is not a theorem. It is how the project separates a proof, a numerical check, an estimate, and an open question.

Every result carries a label: PROVED, NUMERICAL, ESTIMATED, or CONJECTURE. Labels do not move through prose edits. A change needs an executed check or an independent derivation. Before any label moves, one agent attacks the proof and another derives the statement from scratch. P1 was demoted in August 2026 when those passes turned out to cover different statements. A repaired statement later cleared a fresh pair of passes.

Every check script is committed with its output. Failures are kept, named, and classified as wiring or substantive. Two early A(τ) diagnostics measured the wrong object. A replacement check measured A(τ) directly and found that the calibration fails it. The record retains all three checks.

The baseline cutoffs, cell masses, window ratios, 18 pointwise dominance-and-contraction nodes, and unresolved solver nodes all come from committed checks. The filing-delay descriptives come from the executed EDGAR sample. The future causal estimates do not exist yet, and the report does not write them in the future tense as facts.
