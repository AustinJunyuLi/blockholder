\section{Literature and Position}
\label{sec:lit}

\subsection{Disclosure rules and stake accumulation}
\label{sec:lit-threshold}

Mandatory disclosure affects both the cost of acquiring a position and the
incentive to monitor or seek control. \textcite{grossmanhart1980disclosure}
identify the basic trade-off: disclosure protects target shareholders but makes
stealth acquisition more costly. \textcite{ordonezcalafibernhardt2022} analyse
an ownership threshold in a one-shot Kyle-style setting. Their policy instrument
is a static stake-size trigger, not the period between threshold crossing and
mandatory filing. Their discussion of objective-specific thresholds, including
sale-seeking campaigns, leaves open the corresponding question for filing
deadlines.

\textcite{bonettiduroormazabal2020} provide the closest empirical disclosure
design. They exploit the staggered implementation of the E.U.\ Transparency
Directive from 2007 to 2009 and find that tighter disclosure reduces toeholds
and control acquisitions, while moving target and acquirer announcement returns
in opposite directions. Their setting concerns ordinary corporate bidders, not
activist Schedule~13D campaigns, and combines a lower ownership threshold with a
shorter notification period. It therefore does not isolate the filing window.
Their bidding-competition mechanism emphasises deterrence: disclosure can invite
competing bids and increase an acquirer's cost \parencite{fishman1988}. In the
present setting, disclosure may instead facilitate bidder search by identifying
a target as available for sale. Whether certification or deterrence dominates
is an empirical question, with implications for heterogeneity by sale intent,
asset opacity, the set of natural buyers, and industry merger activity.

The strategic-trading literature supplies the basis for the reduced-form
accumulation cost. \textcite{back2018} endogenise activist trading and liquidity,
while \textcite{collindufresnefos2016} model trading on long-lived information
against stochastic liquidity. A quadratic cost captures their level prediction
that a longer trading horizon and greater liquidity reduce the marginal cost of
a given position. It abstracts from endogenous intra-window timing and
path-dependent liquidity. \textcite{collindufresnefos2015} show that Schedule~13D
filers trade on days when measured price impact is almost 30 per cent below its
sample average, cautioning against treating conventional pre-filing liquidity
measures as uncontaminated. The broader literature on exit, voice, and liquidity
provides the background \parencite{maug1998,kahnwinton1998,edmansfangzur2013,
edmansholderness2017}, but does not study a disclosure window or the timing of
takeover gains.

\subsection{Activism, control, and structural evidence}
\label{sec:lit-activism-theory}

\textcite{burkartlee2022} model activism as intermediation between an
underperforming target and the market for corporate control. Their activist
brokers a sale after a proxy-style campaign, using board control to mitigate the
Grossman--Hart and Jensen--Meckling free-rider problems. The toehold is
exogenous, however, and the model is entirely post-disclosure. The acquisition
of the toehold in anonymous predisclosure markets is left to future work.

\textcite{corumlevit2019} model secondary-market inference and disclosure of
activist ownership. An activist trades against a \textcite{kyle1985} market maker,
and ownership becomes public through a Schedule~13D publication step. That step
is instantaneous and is not governed by a disclosure threshold or filing
deadline. Their model nevertheless supplies the bidder-discovery mechanism used
here: an activist can signal that a target is available for sale. It also shows
that a credible proxy threat can raise takeover probability while reducing the
conditional premium through weaker board bargaining. The present paper studies
the filing window, which their model does not vary.

\textcite{cetemen2026} analyse leader-follower trading and wolf-pack
accumulation in continuous time. They mention the February 2024 acceleration as
institutional background, but do not make the window a choice or policy
variable. The structural literature likewise quantifies campaigns without
varying disclosure timing. \textcite{johnsonswem2021} estimate a dynamic
reputation model in which accumulation is subsumed in a lognormal campaign-cost
draw and filing coincides with campaign initiation. \textcite{albuquerquefosschroth2022}
decompose the average Schedule~13D announcement return of 6.34 per cent into
treatment, stock-picking, and sample-selection components of 75.2, 12.2, and
12.6 per cent. Their activism cost is a fixed scalar, and they do not study
takeover premia.

\textcite{celentanolevine2025} estimate a simulated-method-of-moments model on
2001--2019 data. They report that activist presence reduces the conditional bid
premium by 13.7 per cent, or 5.2 percentage points, because it weakens the
board's bargaining position. They also argue that the pre-announcement price
incorporates the activist's signal about a potential acquirer. A lower measured
premium therefore need not imply a lower total gain to target shareholders.
Their model treats the activist's decision and information as instantaneously
public and contains no disclosure timing. Finally, \textcite{gantchev2013}
estimate an average campaign cost of about \$10.5 million, roughly half in the
proxy stage. Those legal, advisory, and proxy-fight expenditures differ from
the market-impact cost of building a predisclosure stake used here.

\subsection{Takeover gains}
\label{sec:lit-measurement}

The standard takeover decomposition separates a pre-bid run-up from an
announcement markup. \textcite{schwert1996} measures the run-up from day $-42$
to day $-1$ and finds little substitution between the run-up and markup, with at
least two-thirds of the run-up added to the total price. \textcite{bett2014}
find materially stronger substitution after adjusting the run-up for changes in
the target's stand-alone value: the estimated slope changes from $-0.09$ under
the unadjusted method to $-0.39$. \textcite{eckbomalenkothorburn2025} summarise
the conventional event windows, including a run-up from day $-41$ to day $-2$
and a separate three-day announcement window. The decomposition in this paper
adds the activist filing as a distinct event and tests the joint movement of the
filing return, run-up, and markup.

Activism is closely associated with takeovers. \textcite{greenwoodschor2009}
show that acquisition outcomes account for much of the abnormal return to
Schedule~13D activism and estimate that activism raises takeover probability by
about eleven percentage points. \textcite{brav2008} report that selling the
company is an objective in roughly 14--18 per cent of campaigns and that about
15 per cent of activist targets are acquired. \textcite{boysongantchevshivdasani2017}
find that activist targets are more likely to receive bids and merge. That last
characterisation rests on an author-posted summary rather than the primary text,
so it is used only for this narrow point and does not support the sale-intent
codebook. None of these studies examines disclosure timing.

\subsection{The 2024 reform and the paper's position}
\label{sec:lit-reform}

The SEC's modernisation of beneficial-ownership reporting shortened the initial
Schedule~13D deadline, shortened amendment deadlines, and introduced structured
XML filing \parencite{sec2023modernization}. It declined to adopt proposed
Rule~13d-3(e), instead clarifying Item~6 derivative disclosure. February 2025
guidance further addresses when calls for a sale or restructuring, alternative
director nominees, or conditional voting support make the shorter Schedule~13G
unavailable \parencite{seccdi2025}.

The SEC's baseline indicates that the new deadline bound a minority of
campaigns. Roughly 29 per cent of 2022 initial filings, or 41 per cent of timely
filings, were made within five business days; about 80 per cent of filers had
completed accumulation by then; and 97 per cent had reached 90 per cent of their
reported stake. The SEC nevertheless projected approximately \$810 million in
annual foregone shareholder value.

\textcite{polk2024} is the only prior study focused on the acceleration. It uses
9{,}685 initial Schedule~13D filings from 2001--2022 and compares investors who
filed voluntarily within five days with those who waited the full ten. Its
outcomes are returns and trading volume around trigger and filing dates. The
sample ends before the reform and contains no campaign-resolution, bidder-entry,
takeover, or premium outcomes. A systematic search conducted on 2 August 2026
across SSRN, NBER, arXiv, SEC and DERA materials, forthcoming journal pages, and
author websites found no study of realised post-February-2024 Schedule~13D
outcomes. The closest related work, \textcite{celentanolevine2025}, neither
studies the reform nor uses post-reform data.

The paper's contribution is therefore specific. It treats the filing window as
a time constraint on post-threshold stake formation and evaluates realised
responses to the 2024 rule. The activist-as-intermediary mechanism and the
capitalisation interpretation of lower conditional premia are established in
the cited literature; they motivate, rather than constitute, the contribution.
