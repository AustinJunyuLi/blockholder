\section{Research Question}

In February 2024 the Securities and Exchange Commission accelerated the initial Schedule 13D filing deadline from ten calendar days to five business days, the first change to the accumulation window since 1968 \parencite{sec2023modernization}. The amendment compresses the time an activist has to buy shares quietly between crossing the five-per-cent threshold and disclosing the position, at the same time as the final rule declined to adopt proposed Rule 13d-3(e), which would have treated certain cash-settled derivatives as beneficial ownership. The main question of this paper is: did accelerating mandatory ownership disclosure change who launches activist campaigns, how activist stakes are formed --- including any migration of exposure into cash-settled derivatives that the final rule chose not to cover --- and how takeover value reaches target shareholders? We organise this into three sub-questions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Entry and composition.} Did the acceleration reduce the number of activist campaigns (the extensive margin), and did it shift the composition of campaigns towards more liquid targets, towards larger activists, or towards different campaign intents? The identifying variation is predetermined target-level exposure: the number of trading days required to add five percentage points of undisclosed ownership at the participation cap, $\mathrm{RTD}_i = (0.05 \times S_{\mathrm{out},i})/(\phi \times \mathrm{ADV}_i)$, with $S_{\mathrm{out},i}$ shares outstanding --- measured over a lagged pre-trigger window, so it does not depend on the campaign's realised accumulation or outcome. Because the statutory clock starts only at the five-per-cent crossing, the deadline caps the \emph{undisclosed increment} an activist can add beyond the threshold at $0.05 \times d/\mathrm{RTD}_i$ percentage points, so $\mathrm{RTD}_i$ scales how tightly the legal deadline $d$ bites.
  \item \textbf{Stake formation and substitution.} Among campaigns that still occur, did stakes shrink, did accumulation shift earlier or later around the trigger date, and did activists substitute equity accumulation with cash-settled derivatives disclosed under Item 6 --- a margin the final rule left uncovered when proposed Rule 13d-3(e) was dropped? Substitution predicts that exposure concentrates in precisely the high-$\mathrm{RTD}_i$ targets where the deadline binds.
  \item \textbf{Gain timing and anatomy.} Where do target shareholder gains actually land? Decomposing the total return into the filing return $G_{\mathrm{fil}} = \log(P^F/P^0)$, the post-filing run-up $G_{\mathrm{run}} = \log(P^B/P^F)$, and the bid markup $G_{\mathrm{mkp}} = \log(B/P^B)$, we ask whether disclosure policy shifts value between these components even when it leaves their sum unchanged, and whether activist-involved targets receive lower markups because the market has already capitalised the campaign.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Conventional Wisdom This Paper Challenges}

\subsection{Myth 1: faster disclosure must chill activism}

The policy debate has already decided the answer. The SEC's own economic analysis projected that the acceleration would impose costs on activists of roughly \$810 million per year in foregone shareholder value, on the argument that a shorter accumulation window raises the cost of the undisclosed stake an activist can add beyond the five-per-cent threshold and therefore deters value-increasing campaigns \parencite{sec2023modernization}. \textcite{polk2024} project reduced activist returns from historical data, and practitioner commentary at adoption predicted the effective death of the accumulation window. Yet the same regulatory tables that motivate the projection show that roughly 80 per cent of filers already completed their accumulation within the new five-business-day deadline, and the window binds materially on only a small minority of campaigns. If most activists never used the old window, the projected chilling may never materialise; and if constrained activists responded not by exiting but by moving exposure into cash-settled swaps through the gap left by the dropped Rule 13d-3(e), the reform may have changed the \emph{form} of activist ownership rather than its amount. Whether the reform chilled anything, and whether the response migrated into derivatives, is an open empirical question that, to date, no study has answered with realised post-implementation data. Existing evidence is either pre-reform projection \parencite{polk2024} or drawn from foreign disclosure regimes \parencite{bonettiduroormazabal2020}, which carry their own institutional baggage.

\subsection{Myth 2: activists sell targets cheap}

A second piece of conventional wisdom holds that activist involvement lowers takeover premia, and hence that activists sell their targets too cheaply. \textcite{celentanolevine2025} document that conditional takeover premia are indeed lower for activist-targeted firms, and the natural reading --- that the activist's presence weakens the target's bargaining position --- has entered the policy conversation. But the measured premium is a ratio whose denominator is the pre-bid price, and that price already capitalises the campaign: the market learns that a sale is more likely from the 13D filing itself and from post-filing trading, so part of the takeover gain is paid to shareholders before any bid is announced. Whether run-ups substitute for or add to the final offer is itself contested: \textcite{schwert1996} found little substitution --- the run-up was largely \emph{added} to the bid --- while \textcite{bett2014} show that once run-ups are adjusted for the target's stand-alone value change, run-up and markup do move against each other. The adjusted substitution result implies that measured markups can fall while total target gains rise. We treat this capitalisation argument as a measurement lemma rather than a headline claim, and we test it directly: the gain anatomy shows where gains land, in filing returns, run-ups, and markups, for activist-involved and matched targets, stated event-time-invariant so that the reform itself cannot mechanically truncate the window and mimic the result.

\subsection{Contribution}

Existing theory models disclosure thresholds as static size triggers \parencite{ordonezcalafibernhardt2022}, the activist as a broker or intermediary with an exogenous toehold \parencite{burkartlee2022}, control contests with instantaneous solicitation and disclosure \parencite{corumlevit2019}, and structural quantification of campaign and sale outcomes \parencite{johnsonswem2021, albuquerquefosschroth2022, celentanolevine2025}, but no paper models the filing \emph{window} as a time margin of stake formation or measures the realised response to the 2024 acceleration. The contribution of this paper is the realised evaluation: a dose-response design on predetermined required trading days, delivering the first post-implementation evidence on the acceleration's chilling and substitution margins. The deadline-accumulation model is the lens --- it generates the exposure measure and organises the predictions --- not a co-equal headline.
